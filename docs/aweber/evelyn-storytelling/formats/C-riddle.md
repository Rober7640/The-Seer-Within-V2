# C · The Riddle

**Status:** 🧪 NEXT TO BUILD — stress test #1
**In one line:** an old trick riddle → its answer IS the lesson → turn it onto love → her version → the chat.
**Source:** Matt Furey, *Tao of Email Copywriting*, PDF p.36 — "Why Frogs are Fat", an email by a MasterMind member, with Furey's critique.
**Best for:** a laugh + a quick, sharp point (Furey: *"one laugh per email is good. Two or more and you're doing great"*, p.30). Short emails. **Repeatability:** high — there are hundreds of old riddles; the work is finding ones whose answer turns cleanly onto love.

## The source

> Five frogs are sitting on a log. Three decide to jump off. How many are left?
>
> Five. They decided to jump, but never jumped.

Furey's note: **"Beautiful opener."** Then the email went wrong, and his critique is the rulebook:

| What the writer did | Furey's note | Our rule |
|---|---|---|
| Moved from the riddle into exercise tips | *"You stopped entertaining. Now you're educating."* | Don't teach after the riddle |
| Drifted to a "Wheel of Life" | *"Where are you going with this? I'm totally lost now."* | One riddle, one point |
| Told a second riddle (six birds on a feeder) | *"Not funny this time. Technique overused. Once is enough."* | **One riddle per email** |
| Never really sold | *"Sell something damnit… Tell a story that ties into your product somewhere and somehow."* | The riddle's point must lead straight to the chat |
| Weak P.S. ("Yeah, so what???") | — | P.S. calls back the riddle and asks again |

## The shape (≈300–420 words — short; ~50/50)

1. **The riddle, cold** — first line of the email, no warm-up. Bold. Set out like a puzzle so she tries to answer it.
2. **A beat to think** — one line ("Take a second. Most people get it wrong.") — optional.
3. **The answer** — short, on its own line. A small laugh.
4. **The turn** — what the answer means, in one or two plain lines. *"Deciding isn't jumping."*
5. **Onto love, first** — the version she lives: *"How long have you been 'deciding' about him?"* + 2–3 concrete versions (love first, then one other).
6. **Why she's still on the log** — ONE honest reason it's hard (no lecture, no tips list — Furey's warning).
7. **Pitch** — *"So here's my invitation."* → tell me what you've been deciding about, one to one → what I'll help you see (3 ➤ outcomes, bounded) → text link (first person, no precondition) → honest line → free minutes → reason not to wait (a callback to the riddle) → button → P.S. (the riddle again, one line).

## Rules

- **The riddle must be old/public** (folk riddles, nursery riddles) — never credit it to a person unless sourced. If a riddle's origin is known, a light true note is fine.
- **The answer must be a real "aha", not a groan** — test it on someone who hasn't seen it.
- **The turn must be honest** — no promise that "jumping" fixes anything.
- **Love first** in the turn and the pitch.
- **One riddle only.**
- **Don't explain the joke.**

## Subject / preheader

- Subject = the riddle itself, or its first half: *🐸 {name}, five frogs are sitting on a log…* — she opens to find out. ≤120 bytes, name first.
- Preheader = the question, never the answer: *"Three decide to jump off. How many are left?"*

## CTA

- Text link, first person, no precondition: *"Evelyn, I've been deciding for too long. Help me see what's stopping me →"*
- Button: *"Start my 1:1 conversation with Evelyn →"*

## Picture

Optional. If used: a pencil sketch of the riddle's scene (five frogs on a log) — `../scripts/sketch-prompt-template.txt`. Keep it small; the riddle should do the work.

## Chat notes

- **Big Idea:** the riddle's lesson in a phrase (e.g. "the five frogs — deciding isn't jumping").
- **Continue Seed:** asks the open question the email ended on (e.g. "What have you been deciding about for too long?"), and must work if she skipped the riddle.
- **Chat test:** a reader typing the riddle's answer back ("five!") or the joke itself — check Evelyn plays along.

## Stress-test questions (answer when we build it)

1. Does the riddle still land when the answer is in the preheader/subject area? (Keep the answer out of both.)
2. Can the turn reach love in ≤2 lines without feeling forced?
3. Does a 350-word email carry a strong enough pitch?
4. How many love-turning riddles can we actually find? (Need ≥5 for this to be a format, not a one-off.)

## Riddle bank (candidates — check the turn before using)

| Riddle | Answer | Possible turn |
|---|---|---|
| Five frogs sit on a log. Three decide to jump off. How many are left? | Five — they only decided | Deciding about him isn't doing anything about him |
| What's always in front of you but can't be seen? | The future | "Which is exactly my job." — a natural Evelyn tie |
| What can you catch but not throw? | A cold (or: feelings) | You catch feelings; you can't throw them back |
| The more you take, the more you leave behind. What are they? | Footsteps | Moving on leaves a trail; you don't have to erase it |
| What gets bigger the more you take away from it? | A hole | The more you avoid the conversation, the bigger the gap |
| What can you keep after giving it to someone? | Your word | Trust; the promise he made / the one you made yourself |
| What has to be broken before you can use it? | An egg | Careful — risks "a broken heart is useful" glibness |
