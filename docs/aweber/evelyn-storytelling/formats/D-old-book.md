# D · The Old Book

**Status:** 🧪 TO STRESS-TEST
**In one line:** a real, rare old book of fortune-telling or love-lore → one verbatim line from it → "the secret exists" → what it says about HER is the chat.
**Source:** Furey, *Tao of Email Copywriting*, PDF p.19 — "Are There Really Secrets To Hand Balancing" (John Wood). Furey's notes run alongside: *"Setting up Law of Scarcity and Availability"* · *"Psychology: I want it, too"* · *"Oh boy, now I MUST have them"* · *"And SELLING POWER."* His one fix: don't switch topic at the end — keep the close on the book.

## The source move
1. "I've collected material on this for years. It ain't easy to find."
2. "I hit the jackpot — a treasure trove from Professor E.M. Orlick… I may own the last surviving copies."
3. Quote it, verbatim: *"There is an old saying among gymnasts and acrobats that 'there is a trick within every trick'… knowing the right position of even one finger can save you months."*
4. "There is a great deal of wisdom in those words." → "Fortunately there are those willing to teach others their secrets." → link.

## Shape for Evelyn (≈400–550 words)
1. Open on the book as an object: title, year, what it looks like/what it promised (true, sourced).
2. Why it's rare/forgotten (true — e.g. out of print, once a bestseller).
3. **One verbatim quote** about love/fate that is surprising, funny or eerily right.
4. Evelyn's honest take — what the old writer got right, and what a book can't do (it can't know HER).
5. Pitch: the book reads everyone the same; I read you → one to one → 3 ➤ → link → button → P.S. (the quote again).

## Rules
- ⛔ **Never claim Evelyn owns a rare copy** unless true. "There's a book…" / "I've been reading…" is fine; "I own the last copy" is invented.
- The book must be real and public domain (archive.org / Project Gutenberg / HathiTrust); quote verbatim, cite edition + page.
- Old books contain dated/ugly ideas — pick lines that age well; never present them as fact.
- Love first.

## Candidates to check
- *Napoleon's Book of Fate* / *The Oraculum* (1822, claimed to be Napoleon's) — a famous question-and-answer oracle.
- Old dream books (19th c.) — "to dream of…" entries.
- Kelley, *The Book of Hallowe'en* (1919) — love charms (already sourced for 02).
- Old "fortune-telling by cards" manuals (playing-card cartomancy, 19th c.).

## Stress-test questions
1. Can we find quotes that are charming, not creepy or sexist?
2. Does "a book can't read you, I can" feel honest, not dismissive of the book?
3. Can the chat handle "what does the book say about me?" — chat-test it.
