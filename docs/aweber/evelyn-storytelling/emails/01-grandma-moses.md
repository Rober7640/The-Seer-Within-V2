**Content:**

There are two words I wish I could take out of people's mouths.

**"Too late."**

Too late to meet someone. Too late to start over. Too late to go back to the thing you loved.

I've been thinking about those words all week. And I keep coming back to a farm wife in upstate New York who the art world decided was too old, at 78.

Let me tell you about her.

Her name was Anna Mary Moses. Also known as "Grandma Moses."

[IMAGE] ../assets/grandma-moses-sipprell.jpg | Grandma Moses, smiling, seated with her hands folded in her lap | Grandma Moses, around 1950. / Photo: Clara Sipprell, National Portrait Gallery

She'd worked on farms all her life. She had ten children, and only five lived past babyhood. In 1927 she was widowed.

In her seventies she started stitching pictures in wool yarn. "Worsted" pictures, she called them. She gave them away to anyone who'd have them.

Then her hands gave out. At night she wrapped them in scarves and laid them on a chair beside the bed. The ache was "just like a tooth ache," she wrote.

**She couldn't hold the needle.**

Her sister Celestia said, "I think you could paint better and faster than you could do worsted pictures."

So she painted.

She took some to the fair with her jam. "I won a prize for my fruit and jam," she wrote, "__but no pictures__."

A few more sat in a drugstore window for years, gathering dust.

Here's where things get interesting.

In 1938 a New York engineer who collected art, Louis Caldor, stopped at that window. He bought them all, went to find the painter, and told her he'd make her famous.

**Her family thought he was crazy.**

Now, here's one thing I didn't know.

It wasn't the paintings that put the New York dealers off. **It was her age.** She was turning 78, and they didn't think she'd live long enough to pay back the cost of a show.

For two years, it looked like her family was right.

Then, in 1940, one new gallery said yes. Before long the whole country knew her name. She painted until a few months before she died, at 101.

Here are 3 things she taught me.

**1. Losing one way isn't losing everything.** Her hands couldn't hold a needle anymore. They could still hold a brush.

**2. "Too late" is an opinion, not a fact.** The dealers said she was too old. She painted for __23 more years__.

**3. Sometimes it takes someone outside your life to see it.** She thought of her painting as no more than "fancy work." The fair gave its prize to her jam. It took a stranger at a drugstore window to see what was there.

**Pitch:**

That third one is why I'm writing to you today.

When you've carried a "too late" for a long time, you stop seeing it clearly. You walk past it every day, like those paintings in the window.

So here's my invitation.

If there's a "too late" in your life, about love, about starting over, about something you put down years ago, come and talk to me. **Just you and me, one to one.**

Tell me what it is, and who decided it was too late. Type it the way you'd say it to a friend. You don't have to explain the whole story first.

Then I'll tell you plainly what I see:

➤ whether it's really closed, or only closed the way you tried it

➤ what's been holding it back

➤ the first step to take if the door is still open

[LINK] I'm ready to explore my life purpose and open my mind to new possibilities

I won't promise you a happy ending. I'll promise you an honest look, from someone who isn't standing as close to it as you are.

And if you've never talked to me before, **your first 3 minutes are on me.** That's enough for the question you've been sitting on.

She was nearly 78 when a stranger finally stopped at that window. __You don't have to wait that long.__
