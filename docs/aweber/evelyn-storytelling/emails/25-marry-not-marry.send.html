The true story of Charles Darwin's love list, and 3 things he taught me.
‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ 
 

Have you ever made a list about a man?

Maybe not on paper. Just in your head, at two in the morning.

Stay or go. Call him or don’t. One more chance, or no more chances.

No one right now? Then maybe your list is about love itself. Is it worth the trouble again?

A young man once wrote his down, in pencil. Let me tell you what he wrote.

His name was Charles Darwin. Yes, that Darwin.

Charles, painted to mark his marriage.
Watercolour: George Richmond, 1840 (public domain)

He was 29, home from five years sailing around the world on a ship called the Beagle.

Back in London, he had a question: should he get married?

So he did what scientists do. He made two columns: “Marry” and “Not Marry.”

At the top: “This is the Question.” He drew a circle around it.

Under Not Marry: “Freedom to go where one liked.” The “Conversation of clever men at clubs.” And a worry about “fatness & idleness.”

Under Marry: a “Constant companion.” A “friend in old age.”

And, squeezed in between the lines: “better than a dog anyhow.”

(Such a romantic.)

Then: “Only picture to yourself a nice soft wife on a sofa with good fire.”

And at the bottom: “Marry — Marry — Marry Q.E.D.”

(Q.E.D. is what you write at the end of a math proof. There, I proved it.)

Here’s what I noticed. There’s no woman’s name on that list.

But there was someone. His cousin, Emma Wedgwood. They’d been friends for years.

That summer he went to stay with her family. He meant to ask her.

He didn’t. He was too scared.

And Emma wasn’t at all sure how he felt about her.

Now, here’s the part I love.

On 11 November 1838, he finally asked. It took her by surprise. She said yes.

In his journal: “The day of days!”

He told her about his summer fright. Her verdict: “It was certainly a very unnecessary fear.”

Emma had one worry. Her faith ran deep, he had doubts, and she was afraid it would come between them.

Soon after their wedding in January 1839, she wrote to him about it. Face to face, she said, “I cannot say exactly what I wish to say.”

Near the end, she wrote: “I should be most unhappy if I thought we did not belong to each other forever.”

At the bottom of that letter, in his own hand, he wrote:

“When I am dead, know that many times, I have kissed and cryed over this.”

They were married for 43 years. In old age, he called her “my greatest blessing.”

Here are 3 things he taught me.

1. Even clever people make lists about love. He’d sailed around the world, and he still wrote “better than a dog.” So if you’ve made one about a man, you’re in very clever company.

2. A list can’t choose for you. By the time he wrote “Marry” three times, he’d already chosen. What no list could give him was the nerve to ask.

3. Say the scary part out loud. Emma couldn’t say hers to his face, so she wrote it down. He called it “her beautiful letter.”

That third lesson is the one I’d like to hand you.

I think most of the lists in our heads leave one line off. The scary one. “What if he doesn’t feel the same?” “What if I’m too old for this?” “What if I choose wrong again?”

Emma found a way round that. She wrote hers down.

You can do the same, with me. Here’s all that happens when you click:

You write out your list. Both sides, or only the line you keep leaving off. No one right now? Then write your list about love itself.

I read every line of it, the scary one too.

Then I answer you honestly, and we look at what your list is really saying.

If you’ve gone over the same list so many nights you know it by heart, you’re not being silly.

A list feels safer than a feeling. It lets us weigh things up without saying the scary part.

That’s not weakness. It’s being careful with a heart that’s been knocked about before.

Charles needed three goes at “Marry.” Most of us need a few more.
