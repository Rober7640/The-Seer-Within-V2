Does he love me?

It's not a question you can just ask him. So for a long time, girls asked something else.

A daisy. An apple. A pot on the stove. In Tennessee, a bird.

In parts of New England, these little love tests were called "projects." In other places, one girl would just turn to another and say, "Let's try our fortunes."

In 1896, a folklore collector named Fanny Bergen put 139 of them in one chapter of a book.

Here are five from her book.

1. The daisy. Pull off the petals one at a time. "He loves me, he loves me not." The last petal is your answer. Even then, Bergen called this one "common at the present time."

2. The apple. Name an apple for him and count its seeds. An old New York rhyme tells you what your number means: "Six he loves, / Seven she loves, / Eight they both love."

3. The pot. In Maryland, if a pot was boiling over, you put a little stick in one of its handles and named it for the one you liked best. "If he loves you in return, the water will cease to boil over; if not, it will continue."

4. The turkey-buzzard. In Tennessee, if you saw one flying alone, you called up to it: "Hail! Hail! Lonely, lonesome turkey-buzzard." Then you asked it if he loved you. If it flapped its wings, he was true. If not, he was false. (Small problem. Turkey-buzzards hardly ever flap. They mostly glide.)

5. The daisy, the long way. In New Brunswick, girls pulled their petals to a longer rhyme: "He loves me, / He don't; / He'll have me, / He won't; / He would if he could, / But he can't."

Can't what? The daisy doesn't say.

That's the trouble with all five. You get your answer. Loves me, loves me not. Flap, no flap. Then they stop.

Not one tells you what to do with that answer. Or what he's really doing.

That part was never in the daisy. So skip the petals, and ask me.

P.S. One more from Bergen's book.

In northern Ohio, if a prickly twig stuck to your dress, you named it for him. "If it drops, the lover is false; if it sticks, he is true."

But that twig never took a step. You did all the walking. It just hung on, and got called true.

So here's a better question. Who's doing the moving?

Who calls first. Who waits up. Who says sorry.

If it's mostly you, that's worth a real conversation. Tell me about him, or about love in general, and what's been going on. We'll look at it together.

Being the one who moves closer doesn't make you needy.

It usually means you cared first, and kept on caring when it got hard.

It wears a person down, though. All that guessing. Counting petals in your head.

No wonder so many of us end up asking a daisy.
