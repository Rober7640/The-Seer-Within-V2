**Content:**

**Is he thinking about me right now?**

It's one of the oldest questions in love. It turns up at the sink, at a red light, or just as you're falling asleep.

And you can't exactly ring him up and ask.

So since at least Roman times, people have watched for signs instead. Little ones. Mostly in their own bodies.

[IMAGE] ../assets/burning-ears-sketch.jpg | A pencil sketch of a woman at a kitchen sink, a cup in one hand, pausing with her other hand raised to her ear and a small half-smile |  | 280 | noborder

Here are seven of the old ones, and what people said they meant. Somebody wrote every one of them down.

**1. Your ears burn.** Someone's talking about you. Nearly 2,000 years ago, a Roman writer called Pliny said everyone believed it. (For him, the ears rang.)

**2. You sneeze.** In China, about 1,800 years ago, a scholar wrote that when people sneezed, they'd say, "Someone is talking about me."

**3. You want to sneeze, but you can't.** An old Boston saying: someone loves you "and doesn't dare to tell it."

**4. Your eye itches.** Someone wants to see you, and can't.

**5. Your nose itches.** One old version says the outside means someone out of town loves you. The inside means someone in town. (Well, that narrows it down.)

**6. A hairpin slips out of your hair.** Your beau is thinking of you. In the 1890s, that one was known all over America.

**7. Your shoelace comes undone.** An old New York rhyme: "At that very moment / Your true love thinks of you."

Which one is yours? __A list can't tell you that.__

**Pitch:**

Now read them again. Every one of them says **someone.** Not one of them says who.

Even the shoelace only says "your true love." It doesn't say which man that is.

An old Latin verse talks to its own ear about this: "Chattering ear, why do you ring at me all night? You say someone, I don't know who, is thinking of me now."

That's the part you really want, isn't it? Not "someone." Him.

**And that part isn't in this email.** It can't be. Your sign isn't anyone else's sign.

Here's what happens when a sign comes with no name. Your ear goes warm, and off you go. Is it him? Is it someone else? That's a lot to hang on one warm ear.

**Who do you hope it is?**

[LINK] Evelyn, I don't need a sign. I want to talk about him

Noticed a sign lately? Tell me which one, and who came to mind first.

Noticed nothing? __You don't need a sign.__ Just tell me who, and what's been going on.

Never talked to me before? **You get 3 minutes free to start.**

Your ears may never burn. __You can still ask.__

**→ Tell Evelyn who's on my mind**

— Evelyn

**P.S.** Seven old signs, and not one of them can say his name. You can.

## Notes

**Substance device: the list — 7 items** (01 used 3 lessons; 7 here per the README's "vary the count"). The list IS the device, so no other device is stacked on it. Each item = bold lead (the sign, in her body or clothes) + one plain line (what the old belief says) + where useful a place or date, so it reads as recorded folklore, not as a claim. The last item (the shoelace's "your true love") hands off to the turn ("It doesn't say which man that is").

**Shape (G-list.md, open list):** hook = her desire as a question ("Is he thinking about me right now?") + why people needed signs ("you can't exactly ring him up and ask") → picture → "Here are seven of the old ones, and what people said they meant" → the 7 → "Which one is yours? __A list can't tell you that.__" → **Pitch:** the turn (every one says *someone*, none says who; the Latin verse saying exactly that) → "that part isn't in this email" (Furey's "tell them they aren't getting it all") → the warning (a sign with no name sends you guessing — a general observation, no invented client) → (2026-10-08) the straight question "**Who do you hope it is?**" → link → the two paths, one line each → free minutes → reason not to wait → button → P.S.

**Furey's two warnings, kept:** (1) the WHAT only — the email never says how to find out who (two folk "who" tricks were left out on purpose, see below); (2) Mistake #4, over-teaching — one line per sign, no explanations of why they "work".

**Truth rules applied:** every sign is framed as "what people said they meant", and items 3, 5, 7 carry a source tag in the line itself ("An old Boston saying", "One old version", "An old New York rhyme"). Where the same book gives a sign rival meanings (eyes, nose), the email picks one and labels it as one version. No side (left/right) is named, because the old sources disagree. The Latin verse is called "old", not "Roman" (authorship unknown).

**Pitch shape:** Straight question (operator 2026-10-08: "every email seems to say 'here's the invitation'"). The two paths follow, compact (noticed a sign → which one + who came to mind first · noticed nothing → "__You don't need a sign.__ Just tell me who"). No ➤ list (dropped). **Signs dropped or left out (and why):**
- **Hiccups** (someone talking about / thinking of you) — dropped: the only record found was one secondhand 2012 student report in the USC Digital Folklore Archives ("Love Hiccups", from an Indian friend's mother); not in Bergen or Hazlitt. Not enough to state as a documented old belief.
- **Dropping a knife / fork / spoon** — sourced ([BER] nos. 761–764: knife = a lady will call, fork = a man; no. 764 "if a spoon, your cousin"), but it's a sign that a *visitor is coming*, not that someone is thinking of you. Off-theme; saved for a "visitors" list (see `../formats/G-list.md`).
- **The cheek "who" test** ([BER] no. 1340: wet a finger, touch the burning cheek and name the people you suspect; "the one at whose name it grows cool was speaking of you") — sourced, but it's a DIY way to find out *who*, which would give away the HOW the chat sells. Left out on purpose. (A chat-side gem: Evelyn could mention it if a reader asks.)
- **Hong Mai's "women especially"** (12th c., on people who say "someone is talking about me" when they sneeze) — true and funny, but a second laugh and a gendered jab; kept in Sources as corroboration. Option for a P.S. if the operator wants a Columbo P.S.
- **Bergen no. 1433** ("If you sneeze once, a girl is thinking of you; twice, she is wishing for you; thrice, it is a sign of a cold." *Alabama*) — the best joke in the book, but it's about "a girl" (aimed at men); changing it to "someone" would bend it.
- **Buttoning your dress unevenly** ([BER] no. 373, "your lover is thinking of you") — the very next entry (no. 374) says it means "you will be a widow". Left out.
- **Apron string, collar slipping round to the ear** ([BER] nos. 364, 372) — sourced, but they repeat the "something comes loose" idea of the shoelace.
- **Shakespeare's Beatrice, "What fire is in mine ears?"** (*Much Ado About Nothing*, Act 3 — read by Warburton, cited in [HAZ], as the burning-ears saying) — lovely, but an extra history beat over the word budget.
