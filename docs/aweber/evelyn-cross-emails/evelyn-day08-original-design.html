Most people read it exactly wrong, dear.
‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ 
 

There's a particular silence I can hear in a marriage from a long way off, dear. Not the cold kind. The kind where two people who still love each other are each waiting to be reached for first.

I wrote a letter to a woman sitting in exactly that silence this morning, and drew the two chairs that go with it, turned a little away from one another. I'm sending it on, in case you know the distance.

Here's what I told her.

“You've started counting the quiet. The talks that used to spill now end early. You lie awake taking the temperature of it, asking the dark whether this is the slow end of something, or only a cold stretch in the middle.”

And here's the thing I most wanted her to have, because it's the thing that frees you. A cold season is rarely two people who've stopped loving each other. It's far more often two people who both still love, and are both, quietly, frightened — each waiting for the other to reach first, because reaching first feels like admitting you care more. Like being the one who lost. So you both sit, wanting the very same closeness, each reading the other's stillness as proof they don't want it. Two people can freeze a whole warm thing solid that way, dear, both of them cold, both blaming the chill on the other.

The standoff only ever breaks from one side. The side that decides being close matters more than being right about who started it.

So don't sit him down for the big talk. That's heavy, and it asks the frightened one to confess first. Do the small thing instead. One warm, ordinary gesture, with no scoreboard attached. His tea made the way he likes it. A hand on the shoulder on your way past. Not “we need to talk,” just “I'm still here.” You're not surrendering, dear. You're the brave one, stepping off the scoreboard first, and showing the other it's safe to come down too.

And if you genuinely can't tell the cold season from the ending, dear, you don't have to read it alone in the dark. come and talk it over with me — your first few minutes are free, and we'll tell one from the other, gently.

— Evelyn
