I understand now what she was really counting.
‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ 
 

My mother counted the housekeeping money twice every Friday at the kitchen table. The same notes, the same brown envelope, twice over, as if the second count might come out different from the first.

It never did. But she counted it anyway, every week of my childhood, and I want to tell you what I've come to understand she was really counting — because I think you may have inherited the very same arithmetic, and never once been told you're allowed to put it down.

She wasn't counting money, dear. She was counting safety. And there was never, in her whole life, quite enough of it to make the number feel finished.

Here's the thing it took me half my own life to see, so let me hand it to you and spare you the years. A fear of money is almost never about money. It's a feeling, learned in the body long before you had words for it, that the ground is not solid and could open at any moment. A child raised near that fear doesn't learn a fact about the bank. She learns a posture — shoulders up, jaw set, one eye always on the door. And a posture, unlike a fact, doesn't change just because your circumstances later do.

That's why the usual advice slides straight off you. The budgeting book. The clever app. The kind friend who says “but look at the numbers, you're fine.” They're all talking to the arithmetic. And your trouble was never the arithmetic. You can be, on paper, perfectly safe, and still feel that Friday-night clutch in your chest when the envelope comes out. The numbers say one thing. The body, still loyal to your mother's table, says another — and the body shouts louder.

Now, I've sat with a great many women on this, and the fear wears different coats. Let me say a word to each of you, because you won't all know yourselves in the same sentence.

For the woman who grew up watching every penny counted — the scarcity is in your hands now, even though the cupboard is full. You save not for a plan but against a flood that already came and went, years ago, in someone else's life.

For the woman who married into someone else's worry — you took on a fear that was never even yours. You carry a husband's tight grip, or his mother's, as faithfully as if you'd been born to it.

For the woman who earns well now and still can't spend a shilling on herself without a flinch — hear me. Your trouble isn't greed, and it never was. It's that spending on yourself was filed long ago under waste, and spending on others under love — and you've been quietly starving on the difference.

And for the woman who's frightened to so much as open the banking app, dear, who lets the letters sit unopened on the side — I see you most of all. You're not lazy and you're not foolish. You're bracing. You learned that looking is when the bad news arrives, so you've stopped looking, the way a child hides her eyes and believes it makes her unseen.

Here's the crueller turn in it, and then I'll give you something to do. Somewhere along the way, the carefulness stopped feeling like fear and started feeling like virtue. Being tight became being good. Being frightened became being sensible. And so to let money in — to spend, to receive, to loosen the grip even a little — comes dressed up as a small betrayal of your own character. That's the trap with the cleverest lock, because you'll defend it as your better nature.

So I won't tell you to “be confident with money.” You'd laugh at me, rightly. Two small, doable things instead.

The first. Next time the clutch comes — at the till, at the letter, at the thought of the bill — stop, and ask it one question: is this fear mine, or is it hers? You'll be astonished how often it's hers. And a borrowed fear, once you've caught it being borrowed, loosens almost on its own.

The second. This week, do one small thing your mother at that table could never let herself do. Buy the good coffee. Leave the generous tip. Let a kindness land without rushing to repay it. Something tiny, and only for the pleasure of it. The clutch will come — let it. Sit in the discomfort and watch what happens, which is nothing. The ground holds. No flood arrives. You're gathering the one thing arithmetic could never give you: evidence, in your own body, that you're allowed.

You are not bad with money, dear. You were taught to stand guard over a danger that, for you, may already have passed. You're allowed to set the second count down.

— Evelyn

I read every one, and they teach me more than any book has.
