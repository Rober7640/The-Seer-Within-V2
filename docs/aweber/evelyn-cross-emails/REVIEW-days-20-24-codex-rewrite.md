## Day 20 — Grandma Moses · DOOR

**Subject:** Grandma Moses put down the needle

**Preheader:** She didn't stop making pictures. She changed how she made them.

Anna Mary Robertson Moses made pictures with embroidery thread before she became known for painting.

Then arthritis made it painful to hold a needle. She was already in her seventies.

Her sister suggested that she try paint instead.

So she did.

Some of her paintings later sat in the window of a drugstore in Hoosick Falls, New York. In 1938, a collector passing through town noticed them and bought the lot. Moses continued painting for the rest of her life and died at 101.

In her nineties she wrote, “I look back on my life like a good day's work, it was done and I feel satisfied with it.”

What stays with me isn't the fame that came later.

It's the decision she made before any of that happened.

When the needle became too painful, she didn't treat that as proof that making pictures was over. One way of doing it had ended. That wasn't necessarily the same as the whole thing ending.

Sometimes the distinction matters.

A change in health, a retirement, a move, or the loss of someone who shared an activity with you can close the old way completely. The question is whether it also closed the thing you valued about it.

What actually stopped: the whole thing, or one way of doing it?

If there's something you stopped and you're no longer sure which answer is true, come and speak with me one-to-one.

Tell me what it was, when it changed, and what made the old way impossible or no longer workable.

We'll look at what it gave you, which part you still miss, and whether another form is realistically open. I can't promise the thing can be restarted. I can help you get clearer about what actually ended.

**→ Help me see what actually ended**

Evelyn

**Source note.** The Kallir Research Institute records that Moses made embroidered “worsted” pictures, that arthritis made holding a needle difficult, and that her sister Celestia suggested painting. It also records Louis Caldor finding her paintings in a Hoosick Falls drugstore window in 1938 and buying the lot. The quoted reflection is from Moses's 1952 autobiography, *My Life's History*. She died on 13 December 1961 at 101.

## Day 21 — the robin · PURE GIFT

**Subject:** When a robin makes you stop

**Preheader:** I can't tell you the bird is a message. But I understand why the belief lasted.

There's a saying in Britain and Ireland: when robins appear, loved ones are near.

The connection between robins and remembrance goes back a long way. A broadside printed in 1695 even told of a robin that stayed around Westminster Abbey after Queen Mary II's funeral.

I can't tell you that a robin carries a message from someone you've lost. I don't know that, and I won't pretend I do.

But I understand why people have kept the belief.

An ordinary day doesn't make much room for grief. The shopping, the telephone, and the making of dinner still need doing.

Then a robin lands nearby, and for a few seconds you stop.

If someone comes to mind, you can let yourself remember them on purpose. You don't have to decide whether the bird was sent or whether its arrival meant anything beyond the moment itself.

The pause can still be worth having.

And perhaps that is enough for one small bird to give you.

Evelyn

**Source note.** The modern saying is widely used in Britain and Ireland, but this draft does not claim a date for its wording. *The Westminster Wonder*, a broadside printed in 1695, reported a robin remaining at Queen Mary II's mausoleum after her funeral. The email treats that as a historical account, not proof of a supernatural message.

## Day 22 — the bundle of sticks · DOOR

**Subject:** None of his sons could break it

**Preheader:** An old fable about what changes when you stop carrying something alone.

There's an Aesop fable about an old man and his sons.

The sons keep quarrelling, so their father gives them a bundle of sticks and asks them to break it. None of them can.

Then he unties the bundle. The sticks break easily one at a time.

The usual moral is that there is strength in unity.

But look at what doesn't happen.

The individual sticks don't become thicker or harder to break. Tie them together again and the arrangement changes what they can withstand.

That gives us a useful question whenever the words “I should be able to manage this on my own” come up.

Is the difficulty proof that you aren't capable—or was this particular load never well suited to one person?

The fable can't answer that for you. And asking for help won't make every responsibility disappear.

But another person may be able to take one practical part, help you make a decision, or simply hear the whole version before you decide what to do.

You can begin with someone you already trust. A sister, a friend, or the person who knows part of the story.

If you would rather begin with someone outside the situation, come and speak with me one-to-one.

Tell me what you're carrying, how long you've carried it, and who, if anyone, already knows.

We'll sort through what genuinely needs to remain yours, what might be shared, and what kind of help would make a practical difference. The aim isn't to hand your life to somebody else. It's to identify one part you may not need to hold alone.

**→ Help me find what I can hand over**

Evelyn

**Source note.** *The Old Man and His Sons*, also called *The Bundle of Sticks*, is Aesopica Perry 53 and appears in Babrius 47. Its familiar moral concerns strength through unity. The observation that the individual sticks remain physically unchanged is Evelyn's reading of the story, not part of its stated moral.

## Day 23 — the message · REPLY

**Subject:** “I was just thinking about you”

**Preheader:** The timing isn't the only part worth noticing.

You know the sort of coincidence I mean.

Someone comes to mind while you're doing something else. Then, not long afterwards, a message arrives from them.

Some people read that as a sign. Others call it chance.

I can't settle that argument from here.

What I would notice is what you were thinking before the message arrived.

Was it an old memory? Something you wanted to tell them? A question about what happened between you? Or did their name simply pass through your mind for no obvious reason?

The timing of the message can't answer that. It can't tell you what they were thinking, why they contacted you, or what should happen next.

But it may give you a reason to look more carefully at your own thought before the coincidence turns it into a story about them.

So if this has ever happened to you, write back and tell me two things: who was it, and what had been on your mind?

Even one line is enough.

Evelyn

**Source note.** No external mechanism is claimed. The email presents a familiar hypothetical experience and explicitly declines to infer telepathy, another person's thoughts, or unfinished business from it.

## Day 24 — Two of Pentacles · DOOR

**Subject:** “I can't keep doing both”

**Preheader:** The Two of Pentacles holds two very different meanings at once.

I pulled the Two of Pentacles this morning.

A young man holds a pentacle in each hand. A green cord loops around them in the shape of a sideways figure eight, and one of his feet is lifted as if he is dancing.

Behind him, two ships ride high waves.

Waite described this as a card of gaiety and recreation. He also gave it meanings such as obstacles, agitation, and trouble.

That is an odd pair, but a useful one.

Keeping two things moving can feel lively or necessary. It can also take more attention than either one appears to need from the outside.

The card doesn't tell me that you are juggling two demands. And if you are, it doesn't decide that one of them must be dropped.

That part depends on what the two things are, what each one asks of you, and where the strain is actually appearing.

If you're trying to keep two commitments going and you're no longer sure whether both can continue as they are, come and speak with me one-to-one.

Tell me what the two demands are, how long you've held them together, and where you first began to feel the strain.

We'll look at what each one gives and takes, what truly needs to remain, and what could change without making the decision for you. I can help you see the trade-off more clearly; the choice will remain yours.

**→ Help me understand the trade-off**

Evelyn

**Source note.** Exact card inspected: `docs/aweber/tarot-images/two-of-pentacles.png`. Visible details used here are the two pentacles, the looped cord, the lifted foot, two ships, and high waves. A. E. Waite's *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot* describes the figure as dancing and gives both “gaiety, recreation” and “obstacles, agitation, trouble” among the upright meanings. No visible detail is turned into a personal fact about the reader.
