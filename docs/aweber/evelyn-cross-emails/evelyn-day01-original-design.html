It was never laziness, dear. It's older than that.
‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ 
 

Most mornings I write the letter first, dear. Today I drew first. I couldn't find the sentence until I'd drawn the thing.

Two hands. One holding the other.

I drew it for a woman who feels guilty every time she sits down, and somewhere in the drawing I understood something about her I want to hand to you.

She thinks she feels lazy. She doesn't. What she feels is unsafe, and those are not the same thing, which is why the usual cures never touch it.

Somewhere long ago she learned she was loved for what she did. The meals, the managing, the help given before it was asked for. Love came in through the door marked useful, and rarely the other one. So now, when she sits with empty hands, it doesn't feel like rest. It feels like standing in a doorway with nothing to offer. Exposed. About to be found out. That guilt isn't laziness, dear. It's a guard she posted as a girl, to keep herself useful, because useful felt like the only safe thing to be.

You can't argue a guard like that down. It has heard “you deserve a rest” a thousand times and not moved an inch. It doesn't believe words. It only believes evidence.

So don't try to rest tonight. Too big a word; it sets the guard pacing. Do the smaller, stranger thing. Sit down for the length of one cup of tea, and while you sit, say out loud the thing you would have been doing. “I am not folding the washing.” “I am not answering her message.” “I am not earning my place in this room.”

Then watch what happens. Which is nothing. The washing waits. No one stops loving you. The sky you've held up with both arms for years stays exactly where it is without you.

You are not learning to rest. You're too well-guarded for that yet. You're gathering evidence, one cup at a time, that you were never the thing holding it all up. You were only the one who was never allowed to put it down.

If that guard has stood so long you can't remember posting it, dear, come and sit with me a while and we'll have a proper look at who taught you to. Your first few minutes are free; talk to me the way you'd text an old friend.

— Evelyn
