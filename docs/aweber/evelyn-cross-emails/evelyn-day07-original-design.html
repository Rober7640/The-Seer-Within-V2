A bird let itself into my kitchen this morning, dear. Stayed the length of a breath, and left. The old women in my family had a saying for it, and I think it knows something about the worry you woke with.

A bird that comes in and goes again, they'd say, is news only passing through. Don't grip it too hard, either way. Not the good, not the hard.

I've come to think they were wiser than they knew. Most of what lands on us in a week — the sudden worry, the low mood, the bright hope — is a bird in the doorway. Here a moment. Gone by evening. And we know that, and still we do the thing that keeps it. We stare at it. We follow it round the room.

Here's the bit worth understanding, dear. We grip a worry because some part of us believes that watching it is the same as controlling it. “If I keep my eye on this, I can stop it landing.” But attention is food. The worry that would have flown out by supper becomes a lodger, because you kept the door open by standing there staring at it, hour after hour.

So don't try to not worry. You can't shoo a bird by chasing it; you only knock the china about. Do the opposite. Give the worry an appointment. When it lands, say to it plainly: “Not now. I'll think about you at six.” And go back to your day. Most won't keep the appointment, dear. You'll get to six and find the bird flew out hours ago, while you weren't feeding it.

Whatever comes through your door this week, let it be a visitor. The room was always yours. It stays yours when the bird is gone.

And if something flew in long ago and signed a lease, dear — a worry that's made itself far too at home — come and talk it out with me. Your first few minutes are free, and some lodgers only leave once there's a second person in the room.

— Evelyn
