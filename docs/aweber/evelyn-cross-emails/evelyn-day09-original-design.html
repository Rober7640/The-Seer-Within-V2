An old story about the anger you're still carrying.
‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ 
 

A boat struck mine on the river once, dear. Hard. I spun round with the shout already in my mouth — to find the other boat empty. No one in it at all.

Just a boat the current had slipped from its mooring. And I stood there with all that anger and nowhere to put it, and felt it go out of me like a breath. It's an old story, that, older than me, but I keep it close, because it explains something about the anger you're still carrying.

The collision was the same either way. The jolt, the cold water, the fright. What decided whether I burned over it all day or let it pass was one thing only: whether I believed someone meant it.

That's the whole hinge of anger, dear. Not the hurt. The intent. Anger needs a someone in the boat — a someone who saw you, and chose it. And here's what the years have taught me: most of the boats that struck us were emptier than they felt. People rowing blind in their own fog, slipped loose by currents we never saw, hitting whatever was in the water. It doesn't make the blow hurt less. But it changes entirely what you carry afterward. A cruelty aimed at you is a wound you guard for life. A boat that drifted into you is just a thing that happened on the river.

So here's the work, and it's yours to do today, alone. Bring one boat to mind. Just one — the one you're still angry at. And ask it honestly: was it ever as occupied as it felt? Was there really a someone at the oars, choosing you? Or a frightened, fog-blind person rowing badly through their own bad weather, who barely saw you at all? Sit with that ten minutes, dear. Some of the weight loosens from the asking alone, because you've been carrying a verdict that may never have been true.

And if it won't loosen — if the boat had a face, and a name, and you still can't tell whether it was ever empty — then don't force it alone. Come and we'll look at the water together.
