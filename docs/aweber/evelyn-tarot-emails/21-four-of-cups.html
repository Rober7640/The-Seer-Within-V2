Sometimes it isn't him. It's a heart that's forgotten how to be pleased.

I drew a card for you this morning, dear — the one thing that can waste a perfectly sunny day. A woman sits with her arms crossed, so busy being unimpressed with what she has that she can't see the gift being held out at her elbow.

A figure sits under a broad tree on the grass, dear, arms folded tight across the chest, a small frown settled on the face. On the ground in front, three golden cups stand in a neat row — and the figure stares at them with a look that says, plainly, is this all? And meanwhile, off to one side, out of a small cloud, a hand has quietly appeared, holding out a fourth cup. A gift. Freely offered. Right there. And the figure isn't even looking at it — too busy being unimpressed with the three.

This is the Four of Cups. The card of discontent — of the sulk that can't see the gift, of the woman so fixed on what's disappointing her that she misses the cup being offered right at her elbow. It's a gentle card, dear, and a slightly embarrassing one, because there is not one of us who hasn't been the figure under that tree.

Look first at the crossed arms, dear. Discontent closes you — physically. Folded arms cannot receive a single thing: not the offered cup, not the help, not the kindness, not the reach. The mood itself is the thing doing the blocking. It isn't that nothing good is coming to you. It's that nothing good can get through arms that are crossed against the world. The sulk builds its own wall.

Then the three cups she's glaring at, dear — the good things she already has, gone flat and invisible from sheer familiarity. Those three cups are almost certainly fine: a decent life, real blessings, perhaps a decent man among them. But she's looked at them so long they've stopped counting for anything. That's what discontent truly is, dear — a slow blindness that grows, like moss, over everything you've grown used to. The cups didn't get worse. Her eyes just stopped seeing them.

And the fourth cup, held out from the cloud — that's the new thing, dear. The fresh chance, the kindness, the opportunity, the invitation being extended to you right now, today. And the whole quiet warning of the card is this: while you sit fixed on the disappointing three, you are missing the four. The gift doesn't force itself on you. It just waits, patiently, at your elbow — and if your arms stay crossed, it eventually withdraws back into the cloud, un-taken.

And if you suspect there's a fourth cup near you that you've been too restless or too guarded to see, dear — tell me what might be offered, and we'll look for the gift together.

For the one unsure of the man she has: here's the honest question, dear, and only you can answer it. Is your uncertainty real discernment — or is it the Four of Cups? Because there is a kind of restlessness that would find a flaw in any man alive, that looks at three good cups and only ever asks “is this all?” If you keep feeling he's not quite enough, check first whether the lack is in him, or in your looking. Uncross your arms and actually see what he's offering — the fourth cup he keeps extending that you've been too dissatisfied to take. Sometimes the man really isn't the problem, dear. Sometimes the problem is a heart that has quietly forgotten how to be pleased.

For the one waiting for him to come: the Four of Cups warns the waiting woman of a slow danger, dear — that the waiting curdles into a general discontent, arms crossed against the whole of life, until you can't see the fourth cups being offered all around you: a friendship deepening, a chance opening, a small joy held out plain. Don't let the one cup you don't yet have — him — blind you to the four that are being handed to you daily. Gratitude isn't a consolation prize, dear. It's the very thing that keeps your arms open enough to actually receive him when he finally does come.

For the one grieving: I'll be tender here, dear, because your crossed arms aren't a sulk — they're a shield, and you came by them honestly. But the Sun told you only yesterday that the light comes back, and the Four of Cups tells you exactly how to let it in: unfold your arms, just a little, just once. The world has been quietly holding out fourth cups to you all the way through this grief — a friend's call, a stranger's kindness, one good small hour — and a closed heart can't take a single one of them. You don't have to grab at anything, dear. Just uncross. Just enough to receive the next small kindness that comes.

So here's your small thing tonight, dear — go and find the fourth cup. First, name three things you have that you've stopped even noticing — the three cups gone invisible from familiarity. Say them out loud, and really look at them, as though they had only just arrived in your life this morning. Then ask yourself one honest question: what is being offered to me right now that I've been too restless, or too guarded, to see? Some fourth cup — a person, a chance, an invitation, a kindness — held out quietly at your elbow. Name it. And tomorrow, do one small thing to reach for it. Uncrossed arms can receive, dear. Folded ones never once could.

Tomorrow I'll turn over the Hierophant, dear — the old keeper of vows and traditions, the card of commitment and the “official,” and the sharp question of whose blessing you've really been waiting on. We'll talk about your own vow versus the borrowed script. But tonight, the Four. Uncross your arms. Find the fourth cup.

And if you can't tell whether it's real discernment or just a restless discontent, dear — whether the gift near you is worth reaching for — tell me what's on your mind, and we'll take it from there. It helps to have another set of eyes on what's actually being offered — your free minutes are still waiting.
