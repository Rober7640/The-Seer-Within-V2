A wish ungranted is sad. A wish unmade is sadder.

I drew a card for you this morning, dear — the old wish card. It grants what your heart asked for. But first it asks a question most women find surprisingly hard: what do you actually want?

A contented figure sits on a low wooden bench, dear, arms folded easy across the chest, a satisfied half-smile on the face — looking for all the world like someone who has everything they ever asked for. And behind, on a long curved counter draped in blue cloth, stand nine golden cups in a gleaming arc: a full display, a bounty, every last one of them his. It is the picture of a wish granted. The old readers called it, simply and plainly, the wish card.

This is the Nine of Cups. The card of satisfaction, of contentment, of getting the very thing your heart asked for. But it arrives, dear, with a question a great many women find surprisingly hard to answer — and I want to sit you down with it tonight: what, exactly, do you actually wish for? Not what you're supposed to want. Not what would keep everyone else content. What you want.

Look at those nine cups, dear — a wish granted in full. But a wish can only ever be granted if it's first been made. And here is what I've noticed, over so many years and so many women: an astonishing number of them have never once let themselves clearly name what they want. They can name what they're supposed to want, what would please the family, what's “realistic” for a woman their age. But the true, specific, slightly-embarrassing wish of their own heart? Never spoken aloud, not even alone. And an unspoken wish, dear, is a wish that can never come true — not because the world refused it, but because it was never once named to be granted.

There's a caution folded in the card too, dear — the old “be careful what you wish for.” Because it's easy to wish shallow: to wish simply for “a man,” full stop — and a woman who's handed exactly that, and nothing deeper, can find herself sitting with all nine cups on display and still faintly empty inside. So wish deeper. Not “a man,” but “to be truly known.” Not “a wedding,” but “to feel at home in my own life, with someone.” Reach past the obvious wish and name the real one hiding underneath it.

And here's the part I most want for you, dear: I want you to want things again beyond the love. What would genuinely delight you? What have you wanted so quietly, for so long, that you've forgotten it even counts as a wish? A trip. A craft taken up again. A room of your own. A kind of freedom. It doesn't have to be a man to be worthy. The Nine of Cups says, plainly: you are allowed to want, out loud, and wanting is not greed — it's simply how a whole life gets aimed.

And if there's a wish you've carried so long and so quietly that you've never once said it out loud, dear — tell me the wish you've never said, and we'll say it into the light together.

For the woman who's stopped wanting: dear, somewhere along the way you may have quietly given up on wanting — decided it was safer not to hope, easier not to name the thing, less painful just to expect little. But the Nine of Cups says a wish ungranted is a sad thing, and a wish never even made is sadder still. So let yourself want again, out loud, tonight. Not only the love — the whole life. The trip, the work, the home, the freedom, the small daily joy you've been telling yourself you can live without. You cannot be handed a bounty you never let yourself ask for. Naming the wish is not childish, dear. It's the first and bravest step of every good thing that has ever come true.

For the one waiting for him to come: the Nine of Cups sharpens your wish, dear — because “I just want him to come” is far too small and too vague a thing to build a life around. So wish deeper. What do you actually want the love to feel like? To do in your days? What would truly happy look like — with him, yes, but also entirely apart from him? A woman with a clear, deep, specific wish recognizes the right man the moment she sees him, and is never once fooled by the wrong one who merely happens to be standing there. Vagueness is what makes women settle, dear. Get specific about the wish, and you'll stop mistaking “available” for “right.”

For the one unsure of the man she has: the Nine asks one clean question, dear — does being with him grant the real wish, or only the surface one? Because it's entirely possible to have all nine cups on display — the partner, the status, the box neatly ticked — and still sit there faintly hollow, because the wish he grants isn't the wish your heart actually made. So name your true wish first, honestly, if only to yourself. Then ask whether he is the granting of it, or merely the appearance of it. That isn't ingratitude, dear. That's refusing to spend your one and only life on a wish that was never really yours to begin with.

So here's your small thing tonight, dear — make the wish, out loud. When the house is quiet, finish this sentence honestly, and say it aloud, to no one but yourself: “What I actually want is _______.” Let the first true thing come — not the acceptable version, not the small safe one you've trained yourself to offer. Say it plainly, the way a child makes a wish: with no apology, and no “but that's silly.” You don't have to know how on earth it will come true. The Nine of Cups asks only that you name it — because a wish you are finally brave enough to say out loud has already, quietly, begun to come real.

Tomorrow I'll turn over the Wheel of Fortune, dear — the great turning wheel of timing and cycles, and what it knows about the season changing, the tide coming back, the turn you can feel is near. But tonight, the Nine. Make your wish, out loud.

And if you've forgotten how to want, dear — if it's been so long since anyone asked what you actually wish for that you're not sure you'd know how to answer — tell me what's on your mind, and we'll take it from there. Sometimes a wish only comes clear once you say it to someone — your free minutes are still waiting.

— Evelyn
