the sky above the wreckage is still yours, dear

I drew a card for you this morning, dear — and after the slow water of yesterday, this one asks you for only one thing. Look up.

A woman kneels at the edge of a still pool under a night sky, dear. She wears nothing — nothing hidden, nothing held up to defend herself with. One knee rests on the land, one foot just touches the water. In each hand she holds a jug, and she is pouring: one stream back into the pool, one onto the dry earth, where it runs off in five small rivers through the grass.

And above her, the sky has opened. One great star, eight-pointed, burns in the middle of it, with seven smaller stars set around. In the tree behind her, a single bird has lifted its head.

This is the Star. The card that comes, in the deck, directly after the Tower — after the lightning, the falling, the year that took the roof clean off your life. It is the deck's quietest promise: that after the worst of it, you lift your eyes, and the sky is still there. Still full. Still yours.

I know which women this card comes for, dear. It comes for the one who has stopped expecting good news. The one who learned, somewhere along the way, that hoping is just setting yourself up to be hurt again — and decided it was safer to keep her eyes on the ground. The Star is not foolish about what you've been through. It is painted to arrive after the disaster, not instead of it. It simply tells you the disaster was not the last word.

See that she's bare, dear, kneeling out there in the open. Not exposed — unguarded. The Star is what becomes possible only once you've already lost the thing you were so frightened of losing: you stop performing. You stop holding the towel up. There's a strange, clean freedom on the far side of a heartbreak you actually survived — you've been through the very thing you dreaded, and here you still are, under the sky, with nothing left to protect. That bareness isn't weakness. It's the beginning of being truly seen.

And watch what she does with her two jugs. She pours one back into the pool and one onto the dry land — she gives to the deep water and to the daily ground both. The card teaches the same lesson as yesterday's: don't empty yourself into the grief and leave the living unwatered. Five little rivers run off into the grass — the five senses, the old readers said. The road back to hope is not a thought you think yourself into, dear. It's through the senses: the warm water, the night air, the first good cup of tea you actually taste. Begin there.

One large star, seven small. Seven was the old count of the wandering lights — the ones that move and return, move and return, the proof written in the sky that what sets also rises. You have watched something set, dear. The Star is the card that answers: not the same, and not on your schedule — but the light comes back around. It always has.

And if you've kept your eyes down so long that looking up feels foolish, dear — tell me what took the hope out of you, and we'll find the first star together.

For the one not sure she'll ever love again: the Star is your card tonight, dear, drawn for you on purpose. So hear it plainly: the capacity to love was never in him. It was in you — it's the water in your own two jugs, and you are still holding both. Love did not leave when he did. It pooled, and it's waiting, under a sky that is genuinely — not just to comfort you — still full of light. You will pour again. The Star does not deal in maybe.

For the one unsure of the love she's in: the Star asks one gentle question, dear — does he let you be bare? Not your body. Your unguarded, unperformed self. The Star love is the one where you finally set the towel down. If you've spent years quietly managing yourself around him, that is worth looking at honestly. The right sky lets a woman kneel down and be seen.

For the one still waiting for him to come: stop searching the dark road for headlights, dear, and look up instead. The women love finds are rarely the ones with their eyes fixed on the horizon. They're the ones who became their own light in the dark and stopped needing a man to switch it on. Tend your own sky. Become so quietly luminous that arriving in your life feels, to the right one, like coming in from the cold.

So here's your small thing tonight, dear — go and find one real star. Step outside, or to a window, and look up until you've found a single point of light, and rest your eyes on it for one whole minute. While you do, say one true thing you're still hoping for — not a man, not a date, just the shape of the life you secretly still want. Say it out loud, to the one star. You're not being childish. You're working the exact muscle the heartbreak tried to take from you: the lifting of the head.

Tomorrow I'll turn over the Lovers, dear — and we'll talk about the one choice everyone believes is about another person, and never quite is. But tonight, the Star. Go and look up.

— Evelyn
