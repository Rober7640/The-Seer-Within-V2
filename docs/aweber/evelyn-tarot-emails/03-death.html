I drew the card everyone dreads this morning, dear. Don't look away — in thirty years at this table, it has almost never meant what its name makes you fear.

A figure in black armor rides a white horse, dear — slowly, across a field in the early light. He carries a black banner, and on it, a single white rose, five petals open.

Beneath his horse, people lie in the path. A king has fallen. A child kneels with flowers. A young woman turns her face away. Only a bishop stands and faces him, hands lifted. And in the far distance, between two pale towers, the sun is on the horizon. Not setting, dear. Rising.

This is Death. And in all my years at this table, I have almost never once seen it mean what people fear.

Let me tell you what it means instead. Death is the card of the thing that is already over — and the mercy of letting it be over. The figure rides slowly. He is not cruel and he is not in a hurry. He doesn't cause the ending; he arrives after it's already happened, to clear the field so something can grow where the old thing stood.

Look who he passes. The king — power, the old life — has already fallen. But the child kneels, unafraid. And the bishop faces him with raised hands, because the wise know what the frightened don't: this card has never been about the grave. It's about the morning.

That's the white rose on the black flag, dear. Five petals, open, alive — carried into the ending. Life held up in the middle of what looks like loss. And the rising sun between the towers is the card all but shouting it: there is an after. There is always an after.

The number is thirteen — the one everyone fears, the number folk cross the street to avoid. But thirteen is just twelve and one more. The full circle, and the first step past it. Not the end of the count. The beginning of the next.

And if you're standing in the rubble of an ending right now, dear — tell me what ended, in whatever words come, and we'll look for the morning in it together.

For the one whose marriage or love came to an end: Death already came, dear — the day it ended. You've been living in the field afterward, mistaking the cleared ground for a wasteland. But look up — the sun is rising between the towers. That light is the part of the picture you haven't let yourself turn and face yet.

For the one who lost the person, not the relationship: Death does not erase a love, dear. The rose still blooms on the banner; it rides with him. What you carried doesn't die because its form did. It changes shape, and comes with you into the morning. Loving again is not a betrayal of it. It's how the rose stays open.

For the one certain it's simply too late for her: the card will not let you say that. It puts the sunrise in the frame on purpose. “Too late” is a story; the rising sun is a fact the deck has insisted on for five hundred years.

For the one still keeping an old love half-alive: the rider is patient, dear, but he does not stop. The kindest thing you'll ever do for the love that's coming is to let the love that's over be, finally, over. You cannot greet the morning with both hands holding the night.

So here's your small thing this week, dear: name the ending. Take one thing you already know is over — and have been carrying as though it were merely sleeping. Write it down plainly: this has ended. Then one sentence beneath it, not about grief but about ground: what does its ending make room for? You don't have to believe the answer yet. You only have to let the field be empty enough for something to be planted.

Tomorrow Temperance rises — how a heart actually heals: slowly, mixing the old water with the new. But tonight is Death. The field is clear. The rose is open. Look at the sun, dear. It's rising.

And if your heart is tender tonight, dear — if there's an ending you can't quite turn and face alone — tell me what's on your mind, and we'll take it from there. You don't need the words ready, dear — just start, and I'll meet you. Your free minutes are still waiting.

— Evelyn
