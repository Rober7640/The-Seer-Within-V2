You were taught being alone is a failure. This card disagrees.
‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ 
 

I drew a card for you this morning, dear — and after yesterday's bright, crowded rainbow, this one sends you up a mountain alone, with a single lamp. You've been taught that's a punishment. I want to change what it means.

An old figure wrapped in a grey cloak stands alone at the very top of a snowy peak, dear, at night. In one hand, a long staff to steady the climb; in the other, raised out into the dark, a lantern — and glowing inside it, a single six-pointed star. He is not lost up there. He is not weeping. He is not searching the valley for someone who forgot to come. He has climbed to this height on purpose, above the noise and the crowd and the chatter, and he holds his small, steady light out into the night.

This is the Hermit. The card of chosen solitude — of the woman who steps back from the crowd, lifts a single lamp, and goes looking for the one kind of truth you can only ever find alone. And I turned it up for you tonight, dear, because somewhere along the way you were taught that being alone is a failure, a waiting room, a sign that something is wrong with you. The Hermit has climbed all this way to tell you it is nothing of the kind.

Start with the one detail that changes everything, dear: he climbed up here on purpose. His solitude is chosen, not inflicted — and that single fact transforms the whole of it. The very same evening alone can be a punishment or a retreat, a locked cell or a mountain summit, and the only real difference is whether you are enduring it or using it. The Hermit uses it. He turned the empty room into a high, quiet place where a person can finally hear herself think.

Now the lantern, dear, and the star burning inside it. Look where his light comes from. Not from a husband's approval. Not from a crowd's applause. Not from a lover's eyes turned admiringly on him. It's a star, carried inside his own lamp — the light of knowing exactly who you are with no one else in the room to reflect it back. And that light, dear, is the very thing a good love quietly requires you to already own. Because a woman with no light of her own asks the man to be her sun — and no man alive can survive being someone's only source of warmth. It's too much to ask of him. It was always meant to be a star you carried yourself.

And see how he holds the lamp — out ahead, and a little to the side. It lights his own next step first, yes. But it also shines back down the path, for whoever is climbing behind him in the dark. The wisdom you win alone, dear, doesn't stay locked up in you. It becomes the lamp you hold out for your daughter, your friend, the younger woman coming up the same hard trail. Your solitude is not being wasted. In the cold and the quiet, it is being forged into the exact thing you'll one day give away.

And if the quiet has been trying to tell you something lately, dear, and you haven't had anyone to say it out loud to — tell me what the quiet has been saying, and we'll hold the lamp up to it together.

For the one alone and aching about it: dear, I won't pretend the empty chair doesn't hurt. It does. But I want to change what you believe it means. This solitude is not your evidence that something's wrong with you — it is the Hermit's mountain. Cold, yes. Quiet, yes. But it is the one place where you can finally hear your own voice under all the noise of who you were supposed to be by now. Don't wish the whole season away, praying to be rescued off the peak. Climb it. Learn what it has been waiting to teach you. You will walk back down a different, stronger woman than the one who went up.

For the one waiting for him to come: here is the Hermit's stern kindness, dear — the lamp has to be yours before he arrives, not after. If you're waiting for a man to come and finally switch your light on for you, you'll seize the first one who flatters you in the dark and call it love. So light your own lamp first: your own interests, your own peace, your own steady sense of who you are. Then, when a good man does come, he'll be drawn toward a light that's already burning — and you'll never again have to fear the dark of him leaving, because the flame was yours the whole time.

For the one unsure of the man she has: the Hermit asks whether you can still be alone inside your relationship, dear — whether you've kept a lamp of your own, or handed him your only match. A love in which you've dissolved entirely into him isn't closeness; it's a slow disappearing. Take one evening this week wholly to yourself, on purpose, and notice how it feels — and notice how he takes it. A steady love can easily survive you climbing your own small mountain now and then. A fragile one can't — and that, dear, tells you something worth knowing too.

So here's your small thing tonight, dear — light one lamp. Take twenty minutes entirely alone, on purpose: no phone, no voices, no scrolling. Just you and the quiet. And in it, do one single thing that is only for you and reflects no one else back — read the poem, sit with the tea, look a long while out the window, write down the one true sentence. The point was never the twenty minutes, dear. It's proving to yourself that your own company holds a light — that you are someone, fully, even when there is no one there to see it.

Tomorrow I'll turn over the Three of Swords, dear — the plain, pierced heart, the card nobody ever wants to draw. We'll talk honestly about heartbreak, and the strange mercy that hides inside the rain. But tonight, the Hermit. Climb a little. Light your own lamp.

And if the solitude has felt more like a sentence than a summit lately, dear — if the quiet has grown too heavy to sit in alone — tell me what's on your mind, and we'll take it from there. Even a Hermit passes another lamp on the path now and then — your free minutes are still waiting.

— Evelyn
