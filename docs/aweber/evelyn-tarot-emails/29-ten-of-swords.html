The grimmest card in the deck is hiding a sunrise.

I drew a card for you this morning, dear — the one that looks like the cruelest in the whole deck. Don't turn away from it. It's actually one of the most hopeful cards there is, and I'll show you exactly why.

A figure lies face-down on the cold ground at the water's edge, dear, and ten swords are driven into the back, all in a row. At first glance it is the grimmest picture in the whole deck — the very worst has plainly happened, with no softening and no mercy in it. But lift your eyes to the horizon. The black night sky is cracking open along its lower edge, and a band of gold is rising there. The dawn is coming. The sun, in the very same card as the swords, is on its way up.

This is the Ten of Swords. And I know how it looks, dear — like the cruelest card in the deck, the bottom of the bottom. But it is, in truth, one of the most hopeful cards there is, and I would never lay it in front of you without telling you why. Because everything terrible in this card has already happened. It's behind the figure now, not up ahead. And that gold on the horizon is not decoration, dear. It's the entire point.

Look first at what's true here, plainly: the worst has already come. The swords are in. The blow has landed. The thing you dreaded most is done. And there's a strange, real relief buried in that, dear — because the dread of a loss is so often worse than the loss itself, and the Ten of Swords is the exact moment the endless waiting-for-the-axe is finally, mercifully over. You're no longer bracing for it. You're past it. And past it is entirely different ground to stand on than still-afraid-of-it.

Then count the swords, dear: ten. Ten is the end of the whole number line — there is no eleventh sword. The card has bottomed out completely, all the way, nothing held in reserve. And do you see what that means? For the first time in a very long while, every single direction left to you is up. When you are truly at the bottom, dear, the one thing you can be absolutely certain of is that the next move cannot be further down. There isn't any further down. You've found the floor.

And that dawn, breaking gold along the horizon exactly as the figure lies there — the card insists, stubbornly, on holding both at once: the worst night and the first light, in one single image. Rock bottom and daybreak are painted together, dear, because that is the truth of it. The bottom is where the turn begins. You do not climb out of a pit that's still getting deeper — you climb out of one that has finally struck solid ground. And this one has.

And if you're at the bottom tonight, dear, and you can't yet make yourself look up at that horizon — tell me about the worst of it, and I'll sit with you until you can see the first light coming.

For the one at rock bottom: dear, if you are face-down on the ground tonight, I won't stand over you chirping about silver linings. The swords are real. What happened was as bad as it feels. But I will point, gently, at the horizon — because the worst has already happened. You are not waiting for the blow anymore; it landed, and you are still here, still breathing, on the far side of the very thing you were so sure would finish you. And there is no eleventh sword, dear. It cannot get worse than the bottom. Which means the next turn, whenever you're ready to lift your head, is up — and the gold on the edge of that sky is rising for you too.

For the one braced for a blow that hasn't fallen: so much of your fear, dear, is the dread of the thing that hasn't happened yet — the bracing, the flinching, the 3 a.m. certainty that it's all about to come crashing down. The Ten of Swords has an odd comfort for you: even if the worst did come, it would be survivable, and it would be over, and the sun would still come up the next morning. Sometimes the most freeing thing you can do is look the dreaded ending full in the face and see — even that has a dawn behind it. You are stronger than the catastrophe you keep rehearsing in the dark.

For the one starting over after ruin: the Ten of Swords is, quietly, the card of the clean slate, dear. When a thing has ended completely — not half-ended, not maybe-still-fixable, but truly and fully over — there's a strange freedom loose in the wreckage: nothing left to protect, nothing left to lose, nothing to do but stand up into a brand-new dawn with empty hands. The worst endings, dear, sometimes make the most honest beginnings. You've hit the floor — and the floor, at last, is something solid to push up from.

So here's your small thing tonight, dear — name the dawn. Name plainly the worst thing you've come through: the sword, the ending, the bottom. And put it in the past tense on purpose — say it out loud: that happened. And I am still here. Then go looking for your horizon: name one single thing, however small, that is a little better now than it was at the very worst of it. One inch of gold on the edge of the sky — a morning you got up, a laugh that caught you off guard, an hour that didn't hurt as much. That inch is the dawn, dear, and it's your proof the wheel already turned. Rock bottom is behind you. The light is on its way up.

Tomorrow I'll turn over the World, dear — the very last card of the whole long journey, the one where everything comes round to wholeness and completion, and a woman dances in the middle of it all, finally whole. But tonight, the Ten. The worst is behind you. Look to the horizon.

And if you're lying at the bottom of it tonight, dear — too worn out to believe in any sunrise — tell me what's on your mind, and we'll take it from there. No one should have to find the horizon in the dark alone — your free minutes are still waiting.

— Evelyn
