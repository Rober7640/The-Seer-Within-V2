# The Hook Library

*The hook is the first thing the reader meets after the subject, and it's the most fragile part of the whole email — the part that quietly dies when someone writes on autopilot. This file exists so it doesn't. Read it before writing any hook.*

The hook and the reframe are **different jobs**: the **hook is the door** (get them to read the second sentence), the **reframe is the room** (the payoff). A great email needs both. This file is about the door.

---

## The worked exemplar — dissecting the best one we have

> **"She watered a dead plant for a year. I finally saw why."**

This is the bar. Here's *why* it works, so you can rebuild it, not just admire it:

1. **A concrete, strange image.** Not "someone who couldn't let go" (abstract) — *a dead plant, watered, for a year*. You can see it. The eye can't slide off a specific picture.
2. **A withheld payoff.** *"I finally saw why"* promises there's a reason, and refuses to give it. The brain needs the loop closed. (Contrast the dead version: "why we cling to the past" — the payoff is already spent.)
3. **An implied story.** Someone did this. For a *year*. There's a person, a stubbornness, a small heartbreak — a whole situation you're now inside before you've decided to keep reading.
4. **A gentle wrongness.** Watering a *dead* plant is faintly irrational. Wrongness is a snag; the reader stops to resolve it.
5. **Zero cleverness.** No pun, no wink, no "you won't believe." It trusts the image. Cleverness announces "marketing"; plainness reads as a real person telling you a real thing.

**The formula underneath it:** `[concrete strange act, stated flatly] + [withheld reason]`. That's reusable across every format.

## What makes a hook (the five levers)

Every strong hook pulls at least three of these. If a hook pulls none, rewrite it.

| Lever | What it does | Tell it's missing |
|---|---|---|
| **Concrete image** | Gives the eye something it can't slide off | You can't picture it |
| **Withheld payoff** | Opens a loop the brain must close | It already told you the point |
| **Implied story** | Drops you mid-situation, mid-stakes | Nothing happened; it's a statement |
| **Specificity** | "four months," "11:11," "twice in five minutes" — specifics read as true | Vague quantifiers ("a lot," "always") |
| **Stakes / a snag** | A small wrongness or cost that demands resolving | Nothing's at risk; nothing's odd |

**The first sentence has exactly one job: get them to read the second.** Keep it short and frictionless. Sugarman's rule — the reader should be sliding before they've decided to.

## The hook patterns (taxonomy) — mapped to formats

Each format leans on one or two of these. Full detail lives in each `formats/NN.md`; this is the shared menu.

1. **The incoming letter** — open on someone's real (composite) message, verbatim, in a quote box.
   *"A man wrote to me last night. Three lines."* → used by **01 (Q&A)**. Instant story + a real voice.
2. **The strange act / image** — a concrete, faintly irrational behavior, stated flat.
   *"She watered a dead plant for a year."* → **02 (parable)**. The exemplar pattern.
3. **The blunt reversal** — set up the common belief in one breath, then break it.
   *"Everyone dreads this card. They've got it backwards."* / *"They have it backwards."* → **03 (card)**, and the pivot line in most emails.
4. **The line said twice** — quote a small self-deceiving sentence and flag the repetition as the tell.
   *"'I'm fine on my own.' They said it twice. That's the tell."* → **04 (the tell)**.
5. **The named euphemism** — say the kind word the reader uses, then promise to look under it.
   *"You call it 'giving them space.' Here's what it really is."* → **05 (stop calling it that)**.
6. **The half-truth** — grant a popular saying's truth, then withhold the more important half.
   *"'If they wanted to, they would' is half a truth. The other half matters more."* → **06 (the myth)**.
7. **The omen / recurring sign** — name the thing they keep seeing, then deny the meaning they were given.
   *"You keep seeing it. It doesn't mean what they told you."* → **07 (the sign)**.
8. **The confession** — Evelyn admits something, which disarms and opens a loop.
   *"I almost didn't send this."* → a strong alt-opener for 02/03/07 (use sparingly; don't let it become the frame).

## Graded bank — calibrate your ear

**Strong** (a lever-stack you can feel):
- "A man wrote to me last night. Three lines." *(letter + withheld content + implied story)*
- "She watered a dead plant for a year. I finally saw why." *(strange act + withheld reason)*
- "'I'm fine on my own.' They said it twice. That's the tell." *(specific line + a snag + a promised reveal)*
- "Once a week, I open the door. Today is the day." *(concrete ritual + a real, live window)*

**Weak** (and the fix):
- ❌ "Have you ever felt like love just isn't in the cards?" → generic, no image, no story. **Fix:** open on one specific person doing one specific thing.
- ❌ "Today I want to talk about self-worth." → announces the topic, spends the payoff, zero pull. **Fix:** dramatize it — a scene, a line, a letter — and let the theme emerge.
- ❌ "The Tower is a card of sudden change and upheaval." → encyclopedia voice, no reader, no snag. **Fix:** put a face across the table and a reversal in the second line.
- ❌ "You won't believe what one reader told me…" → clickbait cadence; sophisticated readers bounce, and it fatigues. **Fix:** just tell it, flatly and specifically. Plainness out-pulls hype here.

## Anti-patterns (why these fail on *this* list specifically)

- **Stating the lesson in the subject or hook.** Kills the loop before it opens. The reframe must be *withheld* until the pull-quote.
- **Manufactured curiosity with no substance behind it.** A gap you can't honestly close = a broken promise = an unsubscribe. Only open loops you pay off.
- **Escalating clickbait.** The audit is blunt about this: emoji-warning curiosity gaps lifted opens for weeks, then decayed (38% → 19%) as the list learned to read them as marketing (see [[evelyn-email-audit]]). Hooks earn their open by being *interesting*, not by being *loud*. A great hook is a true, specific thing said plainly.
- **Warm-up lines.** "It's been a while." "I hope this finds you well." Cut them. Start on the image.

## The 30-second hook check

Before you keep writing, look at your first two sentences and ask:
1. Can I **picture** something? (concrete image)
2. Do I **need to know** what comes next? (withheld payoff)
3. Is there **a person and a situation**, not just a statement? (implied story)
4. Would I say this **out loud to a friend**, or does it sound like "writing"? (voice)

Two "no"s means rewrite the hook before you touch the body. The body can be fixed later; a dead hook means no one reads the body at all.
