There's a woman who keeps a lighthouse on a stretch of coast where the ships stopped coming years ago. A bridge took them, then a bigger port up the shore. And still, every dusk, she climbs the stairs and lights the lamp.

People find it a little sad, dear. All that faithful light thrown out over an empty sea. *"Who's it even for?"* they ask. *"Nothing's coming."*

But I don't think she climbs those stairs for the ships.

> **↳ THE REFRAME.** She isn't standing at the glass night after night, waiting for something to finally appear. She lights the lamp because a lit lamp is a life still tended, and a dark one is just a tower that gave up. The light was never for the ships. It's for her.

That is the thing about waiting, dear. Done one way, it hollows you. You stand at the window with your eyes fixed on the horizon, your whole life on hold for an arrival that keeps not coming, and the tower goes cold around you while you watch.

Done another way, it keeps you whole. You tend the lamp. You keep the rooms warm and the stairs swept and the light going, not to summon anyone, but because that is simply what a lived-in life looks like, ship or no ship. And a tower that's lit and warm is, as it happens, the kind of place a traveler can actually find in the dark.

So tonight, don't stand at the glass counting the empty water. Go and light one lamp in your own life, the thing you keep saving for "once they come." Do a small piece of it now, for you.

If you know which lamp you've let go dark while watching the sea, come and tell me.
