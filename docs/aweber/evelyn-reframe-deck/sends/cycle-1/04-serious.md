Someone told me twice, in the same hour, that they weren't looking for anything serious.

Twice, dear. Nobody made them say it. It arrived the first time unprompted, and then again, ten minutes later, pinned onto the end of a sentence that didn't need it.

That is the tell. Not the words. The *second* time.

Because a real preference gets said once, calmly, and set down. *"I take my coffee black."* You don't repeat it three ways and watch my face to see if I believed you. When a sentence comes back around, it isn't information anymore. It's a rehearsal.

> **↳ THE REFRAME.** *"I'm not looking for anything serious"* isn't always a preference. Said twice, it's a flinch: you're not uninterested in love, you're insuring yourself, out loud, against being caught wanting it.

Here's why it works, dear. If you say it first, and often enough, then when the thing doesn't happen you get to keep your face. *I never wanted it anyway.* The line is a wall you build in advance, so no one can watch you hope and lose.

The trouble is the wall doesn't only keep out the loss. It keeps out the person on the other side of it, taking you at your word, backing quietly away.

The advice you'll hear is *just be honest about what you want.* But that aims at your words, when the thing that needs to move is lower down: the wanting itself, which has learned that said out loud, it mostly gets you hurt.

So the practice is small: catch the sentence. The next time you hear yourself say a thing twice, stop and ask what you'd have to feel if you admitted the opposite.
