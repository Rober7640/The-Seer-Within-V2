Here's a little story, dear: a woman takes six dance classes without once leaving her chair.

She arrives early. Puts her coat over the same seat. Taps the rhythm against her knee while the beginners cross the floor, turn the wrong way, apologise, and start again.

When the teacher invites her over, she says, *“Once I know the steps.”*

By week six, she can name them all.

Her friend thinks she needs more confidence. *“You're better than half of us already,”* she says. But another compliment gives the woman no idea where to put her hand when a partner stands too far away.

Eventually, she agrees to one song.

Her partner takes a smaller step than she expects. She slows down. Their elbows bump; they shift a little apart. Neither adjustment appeared in the sequence she memorised from her chair.

> **↳ THE REFRAME.** She had learned the steps alone. The missing part was learning how two people make room for each other, and she needed someone beside her to practise that.

You can think carefully about the relationship you'd like. Read, listen, notice what matters to you. Those things help. Yet some useful information only arrives when another person gets a turn.

What happens when you suggest a different café? When you admit you didn't catch their joke? Can a small awkward moment pass without someone making you pay for it?

You don't have to make a whole relationship out of an invitation.

Try one small shared thing, dear. A short walk with someone you'd like to know better, with a time to head home. During it, allow one ordinary preference of yours into the conversation. Then notice how you both handle the adjustment. That gives you something actual to consider before deciding whether you'd like another walk.
