An apology is ready to send. Yesterday's is still on the screen. Imagine a man pausing over it with this question, dear:

> *"I told my sister something my partner asked me to keep private. I said sorry, admitted it wasn't mine to share, and offered to speak to my sister. My partner said they need a little time. Should I send another apology? I hate thinking they're still upset with me."*

Stay with that last bit.

His regret may be entirely sincere. But *"I hate thinking they're still upset"* adds a second job to the message. He wants to care for the hurt, and he wants relief from knowing he caused it.

Those wishes can arrive together.

> **↳ THE REFRAME.** Beneath "should I apologise again?" there may be another question: "Can the person I hurt help me feel better about having hurt them?"

That is a tender thing to notice about yourself. It doesn't make the first apology false.

The trouble with *"just tell them how sorry you are"* is that another account of your distress can leave them with something else to manage. They may feel pressed to say it's all right before they've had time to decide what they need.

**Look for what the next message adds.**

A missed acknowledgement matters. So does following through on a repair they've agreed to. A longer description of how terrible you feel offers something different. The distinction is in the message, not in how strongly you feel compelled to send it.

Before sending, read your draft once. Underline any new acknowledgement or concrete action. If you can't find one, save it for now and let the discomfort have somewhere else to go: a walk, a private note, a trusted listener. Then check whether an agreed repair still needs doing.

You can take responsibility, dear, without asking for immediate reassurance.
