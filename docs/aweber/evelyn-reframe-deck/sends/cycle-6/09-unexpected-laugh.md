Suppose a dog steals a birthday napkin, gets it caught on its nose, and backs indignantly out of the room. You laugh before anyone else does.

For a moment, it is simply funny.

Then, perhaps, you remember that you're grieving. The laugh stops halfway through, as though you've been caught doing something you ought to explain.

*How can I find that funny when they're gone?*

That guilty feeling can be so convincing, dear. Especially if you've begun to recognise the weight of missing someone as the way you keep them close. A sudden light moment may feel suspicious precisely because you didn't work up to it or give yourself permission.

> **↳ THE REFRAME.** A laugh answers the funny thing in front of you. It doesn't answer how much you loved someone, or how much their absence matters.

You aren't being asked to settle the whole loss whenever something pleases you. A conversation can catch your interest. A ridiculous dog can make you laugh. The person you miss can still matter when your attention is occupied elsewhere.

That's why *“They'd want you to be happy”* may fail to help. You might agree and still feel you owe an explanation for the happiness. Now you're trying to establish what they would approve of, too, when nobody can supply that permission on their behalf.

You can leave that question unanswered.

The next time something pleasant reaches you, try letting the moment finish. Hear the end of the joke. Take another bite of the good peach. If guilt arrives, notice it without immediately giving a full account of how much you still hurt.

This isn't an assignment to feel better, dear. There is no cheerful performance to maintain afterwards. It is only a little room for an experience you were already having.

Sometimes the hardest part is what you think someone would conclude if they saw you enjoying yourself. That is something we can talk through in a chat. Tell me about the good moment and the worry that followed; we can look at what you felt you had to defend.
