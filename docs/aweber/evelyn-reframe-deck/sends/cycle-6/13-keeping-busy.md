Three errands before lunch. A cupboard to sort afterwards. In an imagined diary after a loss, every hour has a job waiting for it.

*“Keeping busy helps,”* its owner says.

It may help a great deal, dear. A familiar task gives the morning a shape. Leaving the house can bring a welcome conversation. There can be comfort in knowing what to do with your hands when so much else feels unfamiliar.

You don't have to give up those comforts to grieve properly. There is no required number of quiet hours.

But look again at that diary. The errands are there. The cupboard is there. Lunch hasn't made the list, and a friend's offer to bring some over received *“Don't worry, I'm keeping busy.”*

> **↳ THE REFRAME.** When *“keeping busy”* leaves no room to be fed, rested, or helped, activity is being asked to do the work of support.

A finished cupboard can give you a little satisfaction. It cannot also be your lunch. And someone hearing how full your week is may reasonably think you already have the company and help you need.

That can leave a strange gap: plenty accomplished, very little received.

*“Keep yourself occupied”* doesn't address that gap. Neither does being told to stop everything and sit with your feelings. The useful question is more ordinary: what does this day contain that actually cares for you?

Try one swap, dear. Choose an optional task that can wait without causing trouble. Use its place for something concrete: accept the meal someone offered, or ask a trusted friend to eat with you. If no one is available, use the time to make an easy lunch and sit down to eat it.

You haven't failed at the day because the cupboard stays untidy. You've changed what one part of the day is for.

If choosing that swap feels difficult, we can start with your actual day in our chat. Tell me what's filling it and what keeps getting missed. I'll help you consider one manageable way to make room for care, with the support you have available.

**→ Make room for the support I need**

— Evelyn
