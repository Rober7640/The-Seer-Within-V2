He carried a key for three years to a flat he had no right to enter.

Brass, worn pale at the edge, on the ring between his car key and the one for the shed. It rattled every time he paid for petrol. He knew the locks had been changed within a month of him going.

His sister asked him to take it off. Gently at first, then less gently, on a Sunday, in front of everyone. She said he was being stubborn. She said you cannot get on with anything while you're carrying the door to the thing that finished.

She was right about the stubbornness and wrong about what it was for.

> **↳ THE REFRAME.** He wasn't keeping the flat, dear. That ending had been done to him, from the first sentence of it to the last, with nothing in the whole business left for him to do. The key was the only piece of it still in his hands. As long as it sat on that ring, there was one part of the ending he had not yet performed, and he would perform it on a day he picked.

We all keep one, dear. The number nobody has dialled in two years. The folder you've never opened and have never once deleted. The standing order for a magazine that came for them.

Everyone reads these as an inability to accept what happened. Usually it's the reverse. When a thing ends without your consent, you are handed the whole loss and given no part in it, and a person needs at least one act of their own inside a story this size. So we hold one small ending back, unspent, because it is the only piece we own.

Which is why *you need to let go* does so little. It asks you to be passive about the ending all over again, and passive is precisely the injury.

So don't wrestle it off your ring today. Choose the date instead. Put a Saturday four weeks out in the diary, and when it comes, take the key off with your own hands, in your own kitchen, at an hour you picked. Same act, entirely different meaning.

And if you know exactly what's still on your ring, come and tell me. We'll pick the day together.
