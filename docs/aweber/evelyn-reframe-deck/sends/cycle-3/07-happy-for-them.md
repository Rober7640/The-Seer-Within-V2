*"I'm happy for them."* She said it twice in one phone call, dear. The second one wasn't for me.

Nobody had accused her of anything. I hadn't asked. A friend had got engaged, she'd said the right thing, and then a few minutes later it came out again, into a gap in the conversation, aimed at nobody.

We don't repeat what we're settled about. You have never had to tell a person twice that you like the sea.

> **↳ THE REFRAME.** The sentence you say twice is the one you're arguing with. And *"I'm happy for them"* is almost never a lie. It's half of a true sentence, and somewhere you learned the other half isn't allowed out of the house.

The whole sentence goes: *I'm happy for them, and I want mine, and today it hurts to watch.* All three true at once. Only the first gets said aloud, because the other two have been filed under ugly. So you hand over the acceptable piece, and everybody thanks you for your grace.

Here's what that costs, dear, because there's always a bill. A want you aren't allowed to name doesn't leave. It only stops being specific. It goes vague and heavy and starts to feel like something wrong with you rather than something missing from your life. Vague wanting is the worst kind. You can't act on it, only carry it and be quietly ashamed of the weight.

So try this the next time it lands. The engagement photo. The pair ahead of you in the queue doing nothing in particular. Instead of the big word *lonely*, get specific about what you actually envied just then. Not *a relationship*. Something exact. Someone to be annoyed at the seating plan with. A person who knows how you take your tea. Somebody in the passenger seat on a long drive.

That isn't self-pity, it's information. Vague wanting keeps you exactly where you are. A specific want has a next move in it, and half the ones people name to me can be had from a friend, a Tuesday, or a phone call they've been putting off.

You don't have to stop being happy for them. That part was true. You just have to stop letting it be the only part you're allowed to say. So tell me yours, dear. Not the sentence you say twice. The thing you actually wanted while you watched them get it.
