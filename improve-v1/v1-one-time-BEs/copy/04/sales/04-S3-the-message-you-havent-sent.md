{{ subscriber.first_name | capitalize }},

**The message you haven't sent him is fine. The day is wrong.**

You know the one. You've written it, read it back, changed a line and put the phone down. You've done that more than once.

Every time, you've tried to fix the words. The words were never the problem. Your bridge week is coming: a few days, somewhere in the next three weeks, when that message lands as an invitation. On any other day, the same words land as pressure.

### Count the sentences

Open it now, if you can, and count the sentences.

One of them says what you want. The rest explain. Why you feel this way. What he did. How long it's been. What you need him to understand.

Each time you rewrote it, you added another one of those. You were trying to make it so clear that he couldn't take it the wrong way. But a man who's pulling away takes anything the wrong way on the wrong day, however well it's put.

When the day comes, keep the sentence that says what you want, and cut most of the rest. That part I'll give you for nothing.

### Why the day matters more than the words

Here's the cup I read for you. Seven shapes came up in the leaves. A bird pulling away. A broken heart. A withering tree. A boat in rough water. A bridge. A lighthouse. A butterfly.

`[IMG: esl-cup]`

*One strand of leaf along the rim, bottom left of the photo: mark 5, the bridge. That's where your bridge week shows.*

Look at number 5. It's a single strand of leaf, up under the rim, following the curve of the cup. In a tea cup, the higher a leaf sits, the sooner it comes. Your bridge is close.

And it's thin. One line of leaf. A thin bridge carries one thing, which is why your message has to come down to the one sentence that says what you want.

Before your bridge week, there's no bridge to walk on yet. Your message has nowhere to go except onto a man who's still pulling away. So it lands as pressure, however gently you put it.

Inside your bridge week there's one evening that carries the message. Your reading tells you which.

### The night you'll want to send it

If you send it before you know your day, dear, it won't go at a good moment. It'll go after two cold days, late at night, when you can't stand the quiet any more. That's how these messages go out.

And a message that lands at eleven at night reads like a woman who's been up for hours writing it. All the care you put into it turns into pressure the moment he sees the time.

Keep it where it is until you know your day.
