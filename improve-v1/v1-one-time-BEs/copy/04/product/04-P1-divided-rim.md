# Your cup is turned

`[IMG-CUP]`

*Your cup, turned and read: the handle, the rim, the base, and seven leaves marked in red.*

Dear,

Your cup's turned, and I've been through it twice. Once last night with the lamp pulled down low over
the kitchen table, and again this morning by the window, because there was one leaf I didn't trust
myself on at midnight. I'll tell you which one when we get to it. I've marked your seven in red and
numbered them, so you can find each one on the photo as I go.

Your cup reads as a Divided Rim. What runs between you and him comes warm or cold, with nothing in
between, and you can't tell from one evening to the next which you'll get. The plainest place the cup
shows it is just left of your handle: the heart came down in two halves with bare china between them,
and a heart in two halves is hot and cold.

A couple of things below will go against what you've been telling yourself. The bird, mostly. Read
to the end before you decide they're wrong.

---

## How a cup is read

You've seen your seven leaves. The timing's in where each one sits, and I look at three things in
every cup.

**The handle is you.** A leaf close to the handle is close to you: yours, or right on top of you. A
leaf across the cup is as far from you as the cup allows. That side belongs to people you haven't met,
and to things you've let drift so far off they're as good as strangers. Your handle's at the lower
right of the photo. Three of your seven sit close to it: the tree, mark 3, just above it; the heart,
mark 2, just left of it; and the lighthouse, mark 6, a little further round at the upper right.

**Height is time.** The rim and the band just under it hold the days: a fortnight either side of now,
the near future and the near past. Below that, the further down a leaf runs, the further back it
reaches. Halfway down is a couple of months. The base is what's buried, there before you noticed
anything. A leaf that trails down the wall has a history, and how far it trails tells me how long.

**The first light sets the order.** Seven leaves, three days each: twenty-one days. The leaves tell me
what each stretch is about, whose it is, and how near or far back it sits. They don't tell me which
stretch comes first, because leaves don't land in a queue. The cup does. When I first turn a cup, I tip
it slowly toward the light, and the whole cup reads from the mark that catches the light first. That
mark tells me which man you're dealing with. A woman whose man runs hot and cold has to stop a different
thing first from a woman whose man has gone flat, so the same seven leaves reach her in a different
order. In yours the light went straight to the heart, mark 2, and lit the bare china between its two
halves before it touched anything else. That's a hot-and-cold cup, so I've read your days in that order,
and I'll show you why it runs as it does.

So each of your seven has a whose, a how long, and a place in the order. I'll show my working as I go.

---

## The bird, and how long he's been going

I'll be straight with you about this one, because it's the leaf I nearly got wrong.

Your bird is mark 1, across the cup from your handle, toward the top of the photo. His head's a
bright, pale-gold fleck up under the rim, with a dark clump below it for the body. If I'd stopped at
the head, I'd have told you he started going a fortnight ago, and you'd have believed me.

But a bird's more than its head. Under the body, smaller bits of leaf trail down the wall, smaller and
further apart the lower they go, until they run into the loose specks scattered across your base. At
midnight I couldn't tell where the tail ended and the specks began, so I turned the cup three times
before I'd say, the last time this morning with the window behind me. The last piece still in line
with the rest of him sits almost exactly halfway down. Below that the bits lie every which way, and
they're specks.

A bird near the rim is a fortnight old. A bird at the base has been going since before you noticed
anything at all. Yours runs from the rim to halfway, so I had to choose which end to read.

I read it from the tail. When a bird takes off, the tail's the last part of it to leave the branch,
so in a cup the tail marks where the flying started. The head only tells you how far he's got.

**He started going about two months ago, and whatever started it has nothing to do with the last
three weeks.**

The last three weeks are the head, the part you've been living in and going over at night. They
aren't where this began. You've been trying to fix something two months old with what happened last
week, and when it didn't work you blamed the way you'd handled it.

You didn't handle it badly, dear. You were working on the wrong end of the bird.

And look where he sits: across the cup, on the side that belongs to somebody you haven't met. On his
cold days, that's exactly who you're talking to.

A fortnight-old bird and a bird from the base would have you doing two opposite things this week. A
fortnight-old one you'd speak to straight away, and one from the base you'd leave alone. Two months is
neither: set hard enough that speaking now lands as pressure, recent
enough that the right words on the right day still get through. So your days start quiet, and the
bridge waits for day 17.

Read that last row twice, dear. The butterfly's the highest thing in your cup, and highest means
nearest, so the first change that actually arrives isn't his. It's yours, and it's under way already.
It sits nearly opposite your handle, where I'd look for somebody you haven't met, and that's you at the
end of these days. It comes last because you can't see it until it's finished.

## Your cup: the Divided Rim

Here's the heart I promised you.

Find mark 2 on the photo, low down and just left of your handle. Two dark clumps sit side by side, and
between them there's a gap of pale, bare china. Two halves of one heart, with nothing joining them. In
your cup, that's where the divide is.

The two halves are his two temperatures as they land on you: the evening he's entirely himself, and the
evening you could be anyone. The gap is where you've been living, the hours between one and the other,
waiting to see which man writes.

You've looked for the pattern in his hot and cold and you can't find it. Look at the gap. It's bare
china. There's no pattern sitting in it for you to find, and you can stop looking tonight.

The heart's low, too, down in the thick band of leaf near the base. Low means settled. The swing's been
running long enough to sink in, which is why it feels like the weather now and not like something he's
doing.

Is it you? No. The heart sits by your handle because that's where the swing lands, not where it starts.
Where it starts is across the cup, with the bird. And the tree, what the two of you had, sits right
beside your handle: thinned and bare at the stalks, and still one clump. If the swing
came from something you'd done, I'd expect that tree broken apart, and it isn't.

So the swing is in him, driven by something in his own week that you aren't being told about. You've
gone back over the cold days looking for what you did differently. You didn't do anything differently.

Here's the part you can use. A hot-and-cold pattern gets fed by the way it's answered. Warm days get
rewarded with long, quick replies. Cold days get met with reaching: the extra message, the "everything
okay?". Both teach him that his temperature sets yours.

So one instruction runs under every stretch below. **Answer the hot day and the cold day the same.**
Same warmth, same length, same speed, whichever man writes that evening.

Then the order, and why it runs the way it does. Your heart comes first, because it's yours: three days
of being level while the swing finds nothing to feed on. The bird's next, because his head's up under
the rim and the going's happening now: you let it. Then the tree, because it's the nearest leaf to your
handle and the first thing you can actually reach: one small, ordinary message, at the root. A man who
runs hot and cold will answer the ordinary on a cold day when he won't answer anything serious. Only
once the ordinary's been answered do you go across to the boat, on his side, and ask about his weather.
Then the lighthouse, close on your side, so you step onto the bridge from solid ground. Then the bridge.
The butterfly closes it.

Your quiet comes first, and it's long: nine days with nothing new from you, bar one small message about
nothing. Being level whatever he does is what starves the swing.

---

## How much is recoverable

The question that decides this isn't whether there's somebody else. It's how long he's been going,
and how much of that is recoverable. How long is two months. Here's how much.

**Most of it.** I'll take it leaf by leaf, so you can see I'm not just being kind.

**The warmth is recoverable.** Look at the heart again. Both halves are there, side by side, both dark
and solid. A man who's entirely himself with you on some days hasn't gone the whole way, and your cup
kept his warm half as solid as his cold one.

**What you had before is recoverable.** A thin trail of leaf runs down the wall from your tree toward
the base. That's its root, and it goes a long way back. The way you talked before the relationship
turned into a subject is still there, all the way down.

**The two months are recoverable.** A tail halfway down the wall is set, but it isn't buried. None of
your seven is lying on the base, and only the base is out of reach.

**The ease isn't.** Your tree's stalks are bare. The leaves that dropped off don't go back on the
branch, and those leaves were the easy part, when it ran by itself and neither of you had to think about
it. Whatever grows up there again will be new, and it grows from the root.

Recoverable doesn't mean he's coming back, and I'm not going to promise you that. It means the thing's
still there to be got back. In your cup, most of it is.

---

## Your twenty-one days

Count from **the first evening after you read this.** Not from a date. From this evening.

Each stretch takes its instruction from its leaf, from what the leaf is and where it sits, and I'll give
you the leaf first. Your cup has all twenty-one days in it. One message matters more than all the
rest, and it has a day and an hour on it.

### Days 1–3 · the broken heart · *Say nothing*

`[IMG-HEART]`

*Mark 2, the broken heart: low in the cup, just left of your handle.*

The two halves you've already seen, low by your handle with bare china between them. Near the handle
makes this one you, and now.

When a wet leaf breaks in a cup and you push the halves back together, they don't join. One slides
over the other and you lose the shape of both. You leave them where they've fallen and let the cup dry
round them.

So you don't close gaps this week. Not the gap with him, and not the one left by the last conversation
that ended badly. You send nothing. If he writes, answer warmly and briefly, and don't add a question to
keep it going. The Divided Rim rule starts here. Warm on day 2, you answer warm and short. Cold on day
3, you answer warm and short. The two replies should look like they came from the same woman on the
same evening.

**What goes wrong here** is the softener. Two good days, and on the third you send "hope your week's
going okay", because it doesn't feel like breaking anything. You've pushed the halves back together.

**This week:** leave the last conversation where it fell, too. Don't scroll back up to reread his
last message for tone. Rereading pushes the halves together in your head.

### Days 4–6 · the bird · *Let the distance be distance*

`[IMG-BIRD]`

*Mark 1, the bird: across the cup from your handle, head up under the rim.*

You know the tail already. For these three days it's the head that matters, up under the rim with the
rest of him trailing down the wall behind it. That's the shape of a bird on its way up and out, and his
head's in the days.

You can't call a bird back out of the air. You can wait it out, or you can chase it, and chasing only
drives it on, because anything coming up behind a bird is a reason to keep going.

Anything you send in this stretch is chasing, whatever it says, because a man in the middle of going
reads every message as a pull.

So you start nothing. If he starts something, be entirely normal, and be the one who ends the
conversation first, with no ceremony and no reason given. "Right, I'm going to get on. Night." And go.

Ending it first is the whole of days four to six.

With his head up in the days, these are the days his temperature swings hardest. Your end doesn't move
with him. You're warm, you're brief, and you're the one who leaves.

**What goes wrong here** is answering the warm day properly. He sends something that sounds like the
old him, and in your relief you answer at length, straight away, with a question back. He's just
learned that warm gets rewarded.

**This week:** each time you end a conversation first, put the phone face down straight after. You
don't stand at the window watching a bird go.

### Days 7–9 · the withering tree · *One small ordinary thing*

`[IMG-TREE]`

*Mark 3, the withering tree: just above your handle, the nearest mark to you.*

Just above your handle, closer to you than anything else in the cup: a dark, branching clump with bare
stalks sticking out of it, and a thin trail of leaf running down from it toward the base.

A tree with bare stalks and a long root is withering, not dying, and you water it at the root.
Water poured over the branches runs off. Water at the root takes days to climb, but it gets there.

The root is the ordinary stuff the two of you had before any of this became a subject. The branches
are the subject itself, and you've been watering the branches for weeks.

So this is where you make contact, and it's about nothing. Something you'd have sent him two years ago
without thinking. A photo of a dog he'd find funny. A line about the man on the bus eating soup out of
a flask. No question in it, or one so small it needs a single word back.

Once, in these three days. Not three times. And don't wait for a warm day to send it. Waiting for his
weather is how the swing has been running you.

**What goes wrong here** is the line at the end. "Anyway, hope you're okay." A kiss you wouldn't have
put there two years ago. Anything about the two of you is water on the branches, and it undoes the six
quiet days you've just done.

**This week:** choose the thing on day 7, before you know his mood, and send it on day 8 or 9. If he
answers with one word, that's a good answer.

### Days 10–12 · the boat · *Ask, don't tell*

`[IMG-BOAT]`

*Mark 4, the boat: on the far left wall, the far side from your handle.*

On the far left wall, the far side from your handle: a loose group of clumps lying low and spread out,
the way a hull sits in the water. It's halfway down the wall, so the weather you're both in is about as
old as the bird. And the boat's the only one of the seven that carries two people.

Row a boat with one oar and it goes round in a circle, however hard you pull. You've been rowing on
your own for weeks. Every long message and every "can we talk" was a stroke with one oar. Now you put
your oar in lightly and wait for his.

So you ask, and you don't tell. The small thing on days 7 to 9 went to the tree, on your side of the
cup. This one goes across to his. Ask him something about himself, not about the two of you: whatever
the last two months have really been about for him. His work, his family, the flat. Let him answer at
his own length, and don't fill the gaps.

Here's where you start finding things out. Most women find out it was never about them. A man who runs
hot and cold talks about what's driving it when he's asked about his week, not his moods.

**What goes wrong here** is turning his answer into something about you. He says work's been grim, and
you say "is that why you've been so distant?" Your oar's back in on its own, and the boat turns.

**This week:** one question, on day 10 or 11, about his life. If he answers at length, listen and ask
one more. If he answers short, leave it short. Either way, you finish the exchange first.

### Days 13–15 · the lighthouse · *Go to your fixed point*

`[IMG-LIGHTHOUSE]`

*Mark 6, the lighthouse: upper right, on your handle's side, just under the rim.*

Upper right, on your handle's side of the cup, just under the rim and above the pale tea stain: one thin
upright streak of leaf, standing on its own in clean china.

Near the handle is close to you, and just under the rim is now. Your fixed point's been a few inches
from you all along, steady, with nothing touching it.

A lighthouse doesn't go to ships. It stays where it is, it's lit, and the ships find their way by it.

The lighthouse is your own fixed point: the friend, the sister, the work, the swimming, whatever held
you level before this started. You've been navigating by a moving point, and a man who runs hot and
cold is the most moving point there is.

These three days are when you go back to it. One real evening, properly, with the person or the thing
it is. Not to talk about him. Do it whether or not the boat went well. The lighthouse sits right before
the bridge because the bridge goes better from a woman who's just spent an evening on solid ground.

**What goes wrong here** is going to the lighthouse and spending the evening talking about him. You've
used up the one evening that was meant to be yours.

**This week:** arrange it on day 13 and have it done by day 15. If you can't think who it is, it's
whoever you'd have rung first a year ago.

### Days 16–18 · the bridge · *This is the week*

`[IMG-BRIDGE]`

*Mark 5, the bridge: lower left, right up under the rim.*

Lower left, right up under the rim: a thin line of leaf lying along the edge of the cup. Going round the
cup, it sits between your heart and the boat, between your side and his, and higher than either.

The bridge is what you bought. This is your bridge week: days 16 to 18, the one stretch where what
you say lands as an invitation and not as pressure. A week earlier, the same words are an exit.

A bridge spans a gap, and you can walk it from either end. So you build a short one, and you stand on
your side of it. A long bridge is a woman crossing the whole way herself. Yours is thin, one line of
leaf, and a thin bridge carries one thing.

In plain terms: one thing, said plainly and briefly. Something true about what you want, with no case
attached, no history, and no request for an answer.

**Your day is day 17. Send it early in the evening, between six and eight.**

A bridge carries its weight at the middle of its span, so of its three days the middle one takes it.
And your bridge sits just under the rim, level with your lighthouse on the other side of the cup.
Leaves at the same height keep the same hour, and a lighthouse is lit at dusk. So the message goes as
the lamps come on. Not after nine: a message that lands at eleven reads as a woman who's been up
composing it.

Day 17 doesn't move for his weather. Cold that day, it goes. Warm, it goes, and you don't make it longer
because he's in a good mood.

Say it, and then stop. Don't add the paragraph that explains it. The explaining paragraph is the bridge
he walks away over. If there's a message you've been redrafting for weeks, this is its week. Cut it to
a third of its length. Keep the sentence that says what you want, and take out the ones that say why.

**What goes wrong here** is the follow-up: no answer inside the hour, so at half past nine you send a
second message to soften the first. The second message is the long bridge.

**This week:** write it on day 16 and leave it alone. On day 17, read it once at six, cut anything
that's crept back in, and send it before eight. Then put the phone away. On day 18, answer whatever
he's said, warmly and at his length, and don't refer back to it.

### Days 19–21 · the butterfly · *Look at what changed in you*

`[IMG-BUTTERFLY]`

*Mark 7, the butterfly: top left, on the rim, the highest leaf in the cup.*

Top left, sitting on the rim itself and higher than anything else in your cup: a small fleck with two
lobes, two small wings, nearly opposite your handle.

A butterfly's change happens inside a closed case where nobody can see it. By the time the wings show,
the work's done, and coming out is only the last few minutes of it.

So there's no instruction toward him in these three days. Whatever he's said since day 17, answer it
the way you've answered everything for three weeks.

The butterfly's yours. Look back over the twenty-one days. You'll have gone three weeks without
composing anything at two in the morning. You'll have answered a hot day and a cold day the same and
found nothing fell apart. The woman who did that is the somebody you hadn't met.

Whatever he does or doesn't do, that part's yours, and it doesn't reverse.

**What goes wrong here** is turning day 21 into a deadline: deciding that if he hasn't done something
by tonight it's over, and sending the message that says so. Day 21 is where the days stop. Nothing's
due on it.

**This week:** on day 21, take ten minutes and write down three things you did in these three weeks
that you couldn't have done on the evening you bought this. Keep the paper. Don't send it to anybody.

---

## Three things to expect

**One: the quiet will feel like losing.** Days 1 to 9 will feel like giving up ground, because doing
nothing feels like doing nothing. It isn't. Those nine days are the only stretch where the swing stops
being fed. Women who drop the days drop them here, on day five.

**Two: he'll go cold first, then very warm, once.** For the first few days his cold side wins: shorter
replies, longer gaps. Then, around day five or six, he'll send something that sounds exactly like the old
him: long, funny, easy. That's him looking back over his shoulder, checking whether the old answer
still comes back. Give it the length you'd give a cold day. The next day he'll be cold again. Neither
one is news about you.

**Three: one person's going to talk you out of this.** Somebody close to you, and you already know
who, is going to hear that a tea reader has given you a three-week plan and tell you to just be honest
with him and say how you feel. They'll mean it kindly. They've watched you be unhappy and they want it
over.

Being honest with him is day 17. It's in the days. What they're suggesting is doing it tonight, which
is the one thing this reading exists to stop. Said tonight, the right words land as pressure, and
you'd spend months undoing them.

Don't argue with them. Don't tell them what you're doing.

---

## What this reading cannot do

One honest limit, and then I'll let you go.

Everything above is about what you say and when. Leaves are good for that.

There are marks in your cup I haven't given you, because they aren't symbols. Down on the base, below
the end of the bird's tail, there's a scatter of loose specks, too small and too broken to make a shape.
The base is what's buried.

The bird doesn't tell me whether there's somebody else, and the specks don't either. If there's a
second presence in this, that's where she'd be: under everything, too broken up for me to
name. If there isn't, they're specks.

Either way, the days above work around it. I can tell you the shape of this and what to say. I can't
make anything stop.

Making something stop is different work, and it isn't this one. I'll write to you about it another
time, if it turns out to matter.

---

Follow the days, dear, and don't read ahead to the bridge on day two.

You stopped guessing the moment you had a sequence. Everything after this is just following it. Your
calendar's on the next page.

— Evelyn
