# Your cup is turned

`[IMG-CUP]`

*Your cup, turned and read: the handle, the rim, the base, and seven leaves on the wall.*

Dear,

Your cup's turned, and I've been through it twice. Once last night with the lamp pulled down low over
the kitchen table, and again this morning by the window, because there was one leaf I didn't trust
myself on at midnight. I'll tell you which one when we get to it.

Yours is a Divided Rim. If you ticked that box on the way in, you'll know why it fits. If you didn't
tick anything, the cup's done it for you: there's a line running down the inside of this one, and
it's the plainest thing in it.

A couple of things below will look as if they argue with my letter. The bird, mostly. Read to the end
before you decide they do.

---

## How a cup is read

In my letter I gave you the seven leaves, but I hadn't turned the cup, so you had the what and none
of the where. I look at three things in every cup.

**The handle is you.** A leaf close to the handle is close to you: yours, or right on top of you. A
leaf opposite the handle is as far from you as the cup allows. That side belongs to people you haven't
met, and to things you've let drift so far off they're as good as strangers.

**The rim is the days.** The top finger's width of the cup, just under the lip, holds the near
future and the near past. Any leaf that touches that band has days on it. I read them round
from the handle the way the sun goes, left to right as you look in, and each leaf takes three days.
Seven leaves touched your rim. Twenty-one days.

**Down the wall is back in time.** Below the rim, the further down a leaf runs, the further back it
reaches. Just under the lip is a fortnight. Halfway down is a couple of months. The base is what's
buried, there before you noticed anything. A leaf that trails down the wall has a history, and how far
it trails tells me how long.

So each of your seven has a when, a how long, and a whose. I'll show my working as I go.

---

## The bird, and how long he's been going

I'll be straight with you about this one, because it's the leaf I nearly got wrong.

Your bird sits just past your heart, wings open, with its head up in the rim band right under the
lip. If I'd stopped at the head, I'd have told you he started going a fortnight ago, and you'd have
believed me.

But the tail doesn't stop under the lip. It trails down the wall in a thin dark line and ends almost
exactly halfway down.

A bird near the rim is a fortnight old. A bird at the base has been going since before you noticed
anything at all. Yours is in two places at once, so I turned the cup three times before I'd say which
end to read, the last time this morning with the window behind me.

I read it from the tail. When a bird takes off, the tail's the last part of it to leave the branch,
so in a cup the tail marks where the flying started. The head only tells you how far he's got.

**He started going about two months ago, and whatever started it has nothing to do with the last
three weeks.**

The last three weeks are the head, the part you've been living in and going over at night. They
aren't where this began. You've been trying to fix something two months old with what happened last
week, and when it didn't work you blamed the way you'd handled it.

You didn't handle it badly, dear. You were working on the wrong end of the bird.

My letter said a fortnight-old bird and a bird from the base would have you doing opposite things
this week. A fortnight-old one you'd speak to straight away. One from the base you'd leave alone. Two
months is neither: set hard enough that speaking now lands as pressure, recent enough that the right
words on the right day still get through. So your days start quiet, and the bridge waits for day 17.

---

Read that last row twice, dear. The butterfly's the highest thing in your cup, and highest means
nearest, so the first change that actually arrives isn't his. It's yours, and it's under way already.
It comes last in the days because you can't see it until it's finished.

---

## Your cup: the Divided Rim

Here's the line I promised you.

Your bird's two wings don't match. The near wing, toward your heart, is thick and dark. The far wing
is thin, hardly more than a stain. Between them a channel of clean white china runs from the lip
straight down the wall, where the last of the tea ran out when I turned the cup over. Two temperatures,
one bird, and a clean gap down the middle. A divided rim looks exactly like that.

You've looked for the pattern in his hot and cold and you can't find it. There isn't one. The channel
runs through his leaf and nowhere near yours. If the swing came from something you'd done, the line
would run past your heart, beside the handle, and it doesn't.

So the swing is in him, driven by something in his own week that you aren't being told about. You've
gone back over the cold days looking for what you did differently. You didn't do anything differently,
and you can stop looking tonight.

Here's the part you can use. A hot-and-cold pattern gets fed by the way it's answered. Warm days get
rewarded with long, quick replies. Cold days get met with reaching: the extra message, the "everything
okay?". Both teach him that his temperature sets yours.

So one instruction runs under every stretch below. **Answer the hot day and the cold day the same.**
Same warmth, same length, same speed. You're not the one with the channel through you, and you don't
behave as if you were.

Your quiet comes first, and it's long: nine days with nothing new from you, bar one small message
about nothing. Being level whatever he does is what starves the swing.

---

## How much is recoverable

My letter said the question that decides this isn't whether there's somebody else. It's how long he's
been going, and how much of that is recoverable. How long is two months. Here's how much.

**Most of it.** I'll take it leaf by leaf, so you can see I'm not just being kind.

**The warmth is recoverable.** You can see it on the near wing of the bird, thick and dark. A man
who's entirely himself with you on some days hasn't gone the whole way.

**What you had before is recoverable.** The tree's roots run unbroken from the rim almost to the
base. The way you talked before the relationship turned into a subject is still there, all the way
down.

**The two months are recoverable.** A tail halfway down the wall is set, but it isn't buried. Nothing
in your cup has gone to the base, and only the base is out of reach.

**The ease isn't.** The crown of your tree is thin. The leaves that dropped from the top don't go back
on the branch, and those leaves were the easy part, when it ran by itself and neither of you had to
think about it. Whatever grows up there again will be new, and it grows from the root.

Recoverable doesn't mean he's coming back, and I'm not going to promise you that. It means the thing's still there to be got back. In your cup, most of it is.

---

## Your twenty-one days

Count from **the first evening after you read this.** Not from a date. From this evening.

Each stretch takes its instruction from the shape of the leaf that governs it, and I'll give you the
shape first. I told you the cup had the exact days. It does. One message matters more than all the
rest, and it has a day and an hour on it.

### Days 1–3 · the broken heart · *Say nothing*

`[IMG-HEART]`

*The broken heart, tight against the handle.*

Two pieces of leaf with a crack between them, pressed against the handle. That makes this one you,
and now.

When a wet leaf breaks in a cup and you push the halves back together, they don't join. One slides
over the other and you lose the shape of both. You leave them where they've fallen and let the cup dry
round them.

So you don't close gaps this week. Not the gap with him, and not the one left by the last conversation
that ended badly. You send nothing. If he writes, answer warmly and briefly, and don't add a question to keep it going.
The Divided Rim rule starts here. Warm on day 2, you answer warm and short. Cold on day 3, you answer
warm and short. The two replies should look like they came from the same woman on the same evening.

**What goes wrong here** is the softener. Two good days, and on the third you send "hope your week's
going okay", because it doesn't feel like breaking anything. You've pushed the halves back together.

**This week:** leave the last conversation where it fell, too. Don't scroll back up to reread his
last message for tone. Rereading pushes the halves together in your head.

### Days 4–6 · the bird · *Let the distance be distance*

`[IMG-BIRD]`

*The bird, wings open, head under the lip and tail trailing down.*

You know the tail already. For these three days it's the wings that matter, and they're open. The
bird's in the air.

You can't call a bird back out of the air. You can wait it out, or you can chase it, and chasing only
drives it on, because anything coming up behind a bird is a reason to keep going.

Anything you send in this stretch is chasing, whatever it says, because a man in the middle of going
reads every message as a pull.

So you start nothing. If he starts something, be entirely normal, and be the one who ends the
conversation first, with no ceremony and no reason given. "Right, I'm going to get on. Night." And go.

Ending it first is the whole of days four to six.

In your cup the divide runs through this leaf, so these are the days his temperature swings hardest.
Your end doesn't move with him. You're warm, you're brief, and you're the one who leaves.

**What goes wrong here** is answering the warm day properly. He sends something that sounds like the
old him, and in your relief you answer at length, straight away, with a question back. He's just
learned that warm gets rewarded.

**This week:** each time you end a conversation first, put the phone face down straight after. You
don't stand at the window watching a bird go.

### Days 7–9 · the withering tree · *One small ordinary thing*

`[IMG-TREE]`

*The withering tree, crown at the rim and roots running down the wall.*

Past the bird, on the far side of the channel, a tree. Its crown is in the rim band, thin, a few bare
twigs. Its roots run down the wall, dark and unbroken, nearly to the base.

A tree that's thin at the top and sound at the bottom is withering, not dying, and you water it at
the root. Water poured over the leaves runs off. Water at the root takes days to climb, but it gets
there.

The root is the ordinary stuff the two of you had before any of this became a subject. The leaves are
the subject itself, and you've been watering the leaves for weeks.

So this is where you make contact, and it's about nothing. Something you'd have sent him two years ago
without thinking. A photo of a dog he'd find funny. A line about the man on the bus eating soup out of
a flask. No question in it, or one so small it needs a single word back.

Once, in these three days. Not three times. And don't wait for a warm day to send it. Waiting for his
weather is how the swing has been running you.

**What goes wrong here** is the line at the end. "Anyway, hope you're okay." A kiss you wouldn't have
put there two years ago. Anything about the two of you is water on the leaves, and it undoes the six
quiet days you've just done.

**This week:** choose the thing on day 7, before you know his mood, and send it on day 8 or 9. If he
answers with one word, that's a good answer.

### Days 10–12 · the boat · *Ask, don't tell*

`[IMG-BOAT]`

*The boat, in the rim band, its bow turned back toward the handle.*

A boat in the rim band, its bow pointing back toward your handle. That's the good news in this cup:
the weather you're both in is coming toward you. And the boat's the only one of the seven that carries
two people.

Row a boat with one oar and it goes round in a circle, however hard you pull. You've been rowing on
your own for weeks. Every long message and every "can we talk" was a stroke with one oar. Now you put
your oar in lightly and wait for his.

So you ask, and you don't tell. With the boat turned toward you, the small thing gets an answer. Now
ask him something about himself, not about the two of you: whatever the last two months have really
been about for him. His work, his family, the flat. Let him answer at his own length, and don't fill
the gaps.

Here's where you start finding things out. Most women find out it was never about them. A man who runs
hot and cold talks about what's driving it when he's asked about his week, not his moods.

**What goes wrong here** is turning his answer into something about you. He says work's been grim, and
you say "is that why you've been so distant?" Your oar's back in on its own, and the boat turns.

**This week:** one question, on day 10 or 11, about his life. If he answers at length, listen and ask
one more. If he answers short, leave it short. Either way, you finish the exchange first.

### Days 13–15 · the lighthouse · *Go to your fixed point*

`[IMG-LIGHTHOUSE]`

*The lighthouse, opposite the handle, tall and upright.*

Opposite your handle, high in the rim band, a tall upright leaf with a broad foot and a bright patch
of china at the top. A lighthouse.

Opposite is the place for people you haven't met. Your lighthouse is sitting where a stranger ought to
be, and that's how far off you've let it drift.

A lighthouse doesn't go to ships. It stays where it is, it's lit, and the ships find their way by it.

The lighthouse is your own fixed point: the friend, the sister, the work, the swimming, whatever held
you level before this started. You've been steering by a moving point instead, and a man who runs hot
and cold is the most moving point there is.

These three days are when you go back to it. One real evening, properly, with the person or the thing
it is. Not to talk about him. Do it whether or not the boat went well. The lighthouse sits right before
the bridge because the bridge goes better from a woman who's just spent an evening on solid ground.

**What goes wrong here** is going to the lighthouse and spending the evening talking about him. You've
used up the one evening that was meant to be yours.

**This week:** arrange it on day 13 and have it done by day 15. If you can't think who it is, it's
whoever you'd have rung first a year ago.

### Days 16–18 · the bridge · *This is the week*

`[IMG-BRIDGE]`

*The bridge, in the rim band between the lighthouse and the handle.*

Coming back round from the lighthouse toward your handle, a bridge: a long arched leaf, both ends
clear, thickest in the middle.

The bridge is what you bought. It's the one stretch where what you say lands as an invitation and not
as pressure. A week earlier, the same words are an exit.

A bridge spans a gap, and you can walk it from either end. So you build a short one, and you stand on
your side of it. A long bridge is a woman crossing the whole way herself.

In plain terms: one thing, said plainly and briefly. Something true about what you want, with no case
attached, no history, and no request for an answer.

**Your day is day 17. Send it early in the evening, between six and eight.**

Your bridge is thickest in the middle, and a bridge carries its weight at the middle, so of its three
days the middle one takes it. The bridge also runs straight to the lighthouse, and a lighthouse is lit
at dusk. So the message goes as the lamps come on. Not after nine: a message that lands at eleven reads
as a woman who's been up composing it.

Day 17 doesn't move for his weather. Cold that day, it goes. Warm, it goes, and you don't make it longer
because he's in a good mood.

Say it, and then stop. Don't add the paragraph that explains it. The explaining paragraph is the bridge
he walks away over. If there's a message you've been redrafting for weeks, this is its week. Cut it to
a third of its length. Keep the sentence that says what you want, and take out the ones that say why.

**What goes wrong here** is the follow-up: no answer inside the hour, so at half past nine you send a
second message to soften the first. The second message is the long bridge.

**This week:** write it on day 16 and leave it alone. On day 17, read it once at six, cut anything
that's crept back in, and send it before eight. Then put the phone away. On day 18, answer whatever
he's said, warmly and at his length, and don't refer back to it.

### Days 19–21 · the butterfly · *Look at what changed in you*

`[IMG-BUTTERFLY]`

*The butterfly, right on the lip, the highest leaf in the cup.*

The last leaf round before the handle, sitting on the lip itself: two pairs of wings, open, higher than
anything else in your cup.

A butterfly's change happens inside a closed case where nobody can see it. By the time the wings show,
the work's done, and coming out is only the last few minutes of it.

So there's no instruction toward him in these three days. Whatever he's said since day 17, answer it
the way you've answered everything for three weeks.

The butterfly's yours. Look back over the twenty-one days. You'll have gone three weeks without
composing anything at two in the morning. You'll have answered a hot day and a cold day the same and
found nothing fell apart.

Whatever he does or doesn't do, that part's yours, and it doesn't reverse.

**What goes wrong here** is turning day 21 into a deadline: deciding that if he hasn't done something
by tonight it's over, and sending the message that says so. Day 21 is where the days stop. Nothing's
due on it.

**This week:** on day 21, take ten minutes and write down three things you did in these three weeks
that you couldn't have done on the evening you bought this. Keep the paper. Don't send it to anybody.

---

## Three things to expect

**One: the quiet will feel like losing.** Days 1 to 9 will feel like giving up ground, because doing
nothing feels like doing nothing. It isn't. Those nine days are the only stretch where the swing stops
being fed. Women who drop the days drop them here, on day five.

**Two: he'll go cold first, then very warm, once.** For the first few days his cold side wins: shorter
replies, longer gaps. Then, around day five or six, he'll send something that sounds exactly like the old
him: long, funny, easy. That's the warm wing of the bird checking whether the old
answer still comes back. Give it the length you'd give a cold day. The next day he'll be cold again.
Neither one is news about you.

**Three: one person's going to talk you out of this.** Somebody close to you, and you already know
who, is going to hear that a tea reader has given you a three-week plan and tell you to just be honest
with him and say how you feel. They'll mean it kindly. They've watched you be unhappy and they want it
over.

Being honest with him is day 17. It's in the days. What they're suggesting is doing it tonight, which
is the one thing this reading exists to stop, and exactly what the woman in my letter did. She said
the right thing on the wrong day, and it cost her four months.

Don't argue with them. Don't tell them what you're doing.

---

## What this reading cannot do

One honest limit, and then I'll let you go.

Everything above is about what you say and when. Leaves are good for that.

There's one mark in your cup I haven't given you, because it isn't a symbol. Opposite the handle,
below the lighthouse and about halfway down the wall, there's a fleck of dark leaf, too small and too
broken to make a shape. Halfway down is two months. The far side is somebody you haven't met.

I can't tell you what the fleck is. If there's a second presence in this, that's where she'd sit, and
two months back is when she'd have come into it. If there isn't, it's a fleck.

Either way, the days above work around it. I can tell you the shape of this and what to say. I can't
make anything stop.

Making something stop is different work, and it isn't this one. I'll write to you about it another
time, if it turns out to matter.

---

Follow the days, dear, and don't read ahead to the bridge on day two.

You stopped guessing the moment you had a sequence. Everything after this is just following it. Your
calendar's on the next page.

— Evelyn

  lit at dusk. Relative days only.
