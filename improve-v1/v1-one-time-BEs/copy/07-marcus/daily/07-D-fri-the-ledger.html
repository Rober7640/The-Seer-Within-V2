The Four of Pentacles gets read as greed. It isn’t — it’s the card of holding on after the danger has passed. It came up beside the two cards that keep the accounts on it: one for what that costs, one for what it has paid. If money is the thing you’ve been quietly managing around, this is about that, and the first of it shows inside a fortnight.

Marcus.

Three off one cut this morning, which is the deepest the deck has opened all week.

One cut, before it’s light, and I read what’s sitting on it. Nobody asked.

Three isn’t a fork and it isn’t a pointer. Three is a set of books. One card for the thing, one for a cost, one for a payment — and read in that order they stop being three cards and become an argument.

Before I turn them, {{ subscriber.first_name | capitalize }}, the thing that decides how you read a ledger.

Everything is paying for something. Nobody keeps a losing arrangement out of stupidity, and if you’ve been in one for years then it is paying you in a currency you would not say out loud — safety, a story that still works, not having to find out. The spread doesn’t judge the arrangement. It shows you both columns and lets you do the subtraction.

The thing itself: the Four of Pentacles

A figure sitting on a stool with a coin clutched to the chest with both arms. One balanced on his head. Two more under his feet. And a whole town behind him that he has his back to.

Look at the arms. He isn’t counting the coin, or spending it, or even looking at it. He is gripping it. And with both arms occupied there is nothing left to do anything else with.

This is the card of holding on after the danger has passed. Something happened once — a bad year, a bill that arrived at the wrong time, a person who let you down over money — and the grip made complete sense then. The grip is still on.

What it won’t tell me is which year the grip started. Every Four of Pentacles has a date behind it and that date is what the card is actually about. Two of the six find it. This one can’t.

One thing it has cost: the Five of Pentacles

Two figures out in the snow. One on a crutch, one wrapped in a shawl. And beside them, close enough to touch, a lit stained-glass window with five gold coins worked into it.

They are not looking at the window. That is the entire card and it has been the entire card for five hundred years. The help is right there, lit, at head height, and the two people passing it have their eyes on the ground.

Maybe you’d call it not wanting to be a burden. Maybe you’d call it we’ll manage. Maybe you’d call it not telling anyone how bad the month actually was, which most people would rather do in the snow than in a lit room.

And nothing in that card says either of them did anything wrong. Nobody in the Five of Pentacles is being punished. It is a card about a habit of the eyes, not a verdict on your judgement — and people who have never once been careless end up in it.

One thing it has paid: the Nine of Pentacles

A woman standing alone in a walled garden thick with vines. Nine coins around her. A hooded falcon sitting on her gloved hand, and a small snail down by her hem.

This is the payment column, and I want you to see it properly. She built that garden. The wall is hers, the vines are hers, the bird is trained and hooded and sits where she puts it. Whatever the grip has cost you, it also bought something, and this card says what: you do not have to ask anyone.

That is not nothing. It is most of what people mean by security, and you paid for it.

But look again, because she is on her own in there, and the falcon is hooded — a hunting bird, kept blind, on her hand. The card will not tell me what she stopped letting off the glove.

Four of the six answer that. Three can’t.

What you’re looking at is called the Ledger

Nine cards. Three you can see. Six you can’t.

Nine isn’t a number I picked. A ledger is two columns and a line under them — three costs and three payments, and then the balance and what it would take to close the account. You’ve been shown the top row of each column and nothing else.

Three of nine. That’s one entry a side.

And the six aren’t more examples. These three told you that there are two columns. The six tell you which one is heavier, and what leaving would cost — which is arithmetic, and three cards cannot do arithmetic.

Here they are, still face down.

The rest of the cost column
The second cost · The third cost
The rest of the payment column
The second payment · The third payment
And the line under them
Which column is heavier · What leaving would cost

These 6 are still in my hand

So let me lay the six that finish it, and I’ll tell you which column is actually heavier.

Back to the first card, and the part nobody points at

The town behind him.

It’s drawn in full — walls, roofs, towers — and he is sitting with his back to all of it on a stool at the edge of the frame. The Four of Pentacles is not a card about a man with too little. It is a card about a man positioned so that nothing can reach him, which he arranged himself, and which is working.

So here’s the one to carry today. Don’t confuse the amount with the grip. They move independently, and I have read for people with a great deal who hold it exactly like that man, and for people with very little who don’t.

And the question you’re probably asking

Most people bring me some version of when does it get easier. That has no answer in any spread I lay, because it’s a date and the cards don’t hand out dates.

Try this instead: what is the grip currently buying me?

That one has an answer, and it’s usually something you would defend if I named it — which is the tell. It sits in the six I haven’t laid.

So put that in front of me, in your own words.

What’s in your two columns?

Tell me, and I’ll lay the other six.

Do that and you stop running the account on feel, which is the most expensive way anybody has ever run one.

— Marcus, at the table most mornings
