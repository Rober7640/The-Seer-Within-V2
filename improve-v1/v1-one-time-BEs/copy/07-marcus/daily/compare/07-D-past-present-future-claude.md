{{ subscriber.first_name | capitalize }} —

Marcus here.

I cut the deck before it gets light and read whatever is sitting on the cut. One cut. Nobody asked me for this one.

This one was already face up.

A deck is face down. Every card in it, the same way round, every morning, because I'm the one who put it away. This morning there was a card lying the wrong way up in the middle of the pack, looking at me before I'd cut anything.

Fourteen years and I've stopped arguing with that. A card that turns itself over has already decided who it's for.

Have a proper look at it before you read what I make of it. It's all in the picture already.

Years ago, above a shop I used to read out of, a woman called Ida. She had a rule about not asking. She could give you the year she learned it and the room she was standing in.

She'd been keeping it so long she'd stopped calling it a rule. She called it not wanting to be a burden. What changed for Ida wasn't that she asked somebody for something and got it, though she did. It was finding out the rule had expired about nine years before she stopped obeying it.

Here's what I can see from one card and what I can't. I can see the rule is on you, and I can see it's old. What I can't see is what it took off you this week — the thing you didn't ask for on Tuesday because asking would have made you too much.

There's better news than that in this card and I'll show you where before the end.

### The past: the Five of Pentacles

`[IMG-2 — the RWS scan: the Five of Pentacles]`

Two figures out in falling snow. One is on crutches, barefoot, head down. The other is wrapped in a shawl with a bandage round her head. Set into the wall behind them, a stained-glass window with five gold coins in it, lit from inside.

They're walking past it. Neither one looks up.

Now here's the part people go straight past. That's a church wall, and a church has a door. Nothing in that painting is stopping those two going in — there's no lock in the frame, no gate, nobody turning them away. They just don't look up, because looking up isn't what people like them do.

That's what was put in you once, and it stayed. Not the bad year. The rule you took out of it: that wanting a thing is fine, and asking out loud for it makes you the sort of person nobody can carry.

I don't know who taught you yours. Some women tell me it was a mother who sighed. Some tell me it was a man who said she was a lot, once, in a voice she can still hear.

And I want to be plain about this, because it's where people flinch. Nobody in that picture is stupid. It's cold, they're hurt, and they're keeping moving, which is what you do. The rule was correct the year you learned it — asking really did cost you something then. That's why it went in so deep, and it's why you've never had cause to check whether it's still true.

Here's what this card keeps back, though. It shows me the rule and roughly how old it is. It won't show me what it's charging you now — this week, in the sentence you cut down to nothing before you sent it. And it won't tell me what another year of it takes.

Two cards say. One can't.

### Back to the window, and the better news

Go and look at it again. Past the two of them.

The light is on. Somebody lit it and left it lit, and it has been burning the whole time those two have been walking past it in the snow.

The warm thing didn't close, {{ subscriber.first_name | capitalize }}. Nobody shut it and nobody has been standing in the doorway deciding who's allowed to ask. That's the better news, and it's been in the picture since before you opened this.

If you take one thing out of this letter, take this. A rule you learned in a bad year keeps working long after the year has finished. Ida gave hers nine.

### The question you came with

Most people write to me with some version of *am I asking for too much*. Here's the straight answer instead of the kind one. I can't tell you what's reasonable to want — nobody can, and anybody selling you a number for it is guessing.

Here's the question I'd rather have.

**What did I not ask for this week?**

That one has an answer, and you'll know it's the right one because it'll be small and slightly embarrassing. A sentence you shortened. A thing you said you were fine with.

So [send me that question]({{BOOKING_URL}}?c=2&s=past-present-future), in your own words. It doesn't have to be tidy.

So, {{ subscriber.first_name | capitalize }}. What didn't you ask for this week?

[Tell me that, and I'll turn the other two]({{BOOKING_URL}}?c=3&s=past-present-future).

I'm not going to tell you two cards make you bolder on a Thursday. Nothing on this table has ever put a sentence in anybody's mouth.

Here's what happens instead. You catch yourself cutting one down, once, this week — and this time you know its name and roughly what year it came from. That's the whole of it. You stop obeying something without noticing you're obeying it.

— Marcus, at the table most mornings
