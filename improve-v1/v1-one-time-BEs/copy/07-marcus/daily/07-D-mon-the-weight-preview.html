The Ten of Wands gets read as being overloaded. It isn’t — it’s the card of the load you picked up yourself and then forgot was optional. If you’re carrying something in a relationship that was never formally handed to you, this is about that, and it starts to show inside a fortnight.

One cut before it’s light, and I read what’s sitting on it. Nobody asked for this one.

A single card doesn’t open a fork. What it does is point at something already in the room and refuse to look away from it.

Here’s the trouble with a card that points, {{ subscriber.first_name | capitalize }}. It shows me the load. It cannot show me whose it was originally — and that is the whole reading, because nobody puts a thing down until they know who handed it over.

You have already chosen. Carrying isn’t something being done to you. It’s a thing you do, every morning, and have done for long enough that it stopped feeling like a decision.

What’s on you: the Ten of Wands

A figure bent almost double, carrying ten heavy staves in both arms. The bundle is so wide it covers his face. And in the distance, a small town with the lights on.

Two things in that picture. He is nearly there. The town is close. And the load is blocking his view of it — the only reason the last part of the walk is hard is what he insisted on bringing.

Maybe you’d call it being the reliable one. Maybe you’d call it easier to do it myself than ask twice. Maybe you’d call it what happens when you’re the one who notices things — the birthdays, the tone of a room, whether anybody has eaten.

And none of that is a complaint about you. The staves in that card are good things — wands are the suit of fire and drive, and every one of them was something you wanted at some point. This isn’t a card about being used. It’s a card about not setting anything down once you were done needing it.

So here’s what the Ten of Wands won’t tell me. It shows me you’re carrying. It doesn’t show me which of the ten isn’t yours — and there is always at least one that got handed over so quietly that neither of you noticed the transfer.

The five behind this card find it. One card can’t.

What you’re looking at is called the Weight

Six cards. One you can see. Five you can’t.

Six isn’t a number I picked. A weight has five things behind it — whose hands it was in before yours, what it was for, what it costs you now, what it is still protecting, and what puts it down. Plus the load itself. Six.

You’ve seen one of the six. That’s the load and nothing else.

And the five aren’t more description of what you’re carrying. This one told you that you’re carrying. The five tell you where it came from and what it would take to set it down — a different question, and one card cannot hold it.

Here they are, still face down.

Behind the load
Whose hands it was in · What it was for · What it costs you now
And the way out
What it still protects · What puts it down

These 5 are still in my hand

So let me lay the five behind it, and I’ll tell you which of the ten was never yours.

Back to the card, and the detail people miss

Nobody is chasing him.

There’s no storm in that picture, no one behind him, nothing making him hurry. The Ten of Wands is the one hard card in the deck where the difficulty is entirely self-administered — and that isn’t an accusation. It’s the good news in it, because a thing you are doing is a thing you can stop.

So here’s the one to take with you today, which is a poor choice of words and I’ll leave it. Don’t confuse being capable of something with being responsible for it. Nearly every load that stays too long got there through that one sentence.

And the question you’re probably asking

Most people bring me some version of why doesn’t he help. Straight answer: that has no answer in any spread, because it’s about him.

Try this one: which of these did I pick up so nobody would have to ask me to?

That has an answer, and it’s specific and slightly embarrassing, which is how you know it’s the real one. It sits in the five I haven’t laid.

So put that in front of me, in your own words.

What are you carrying?

Tell me, and I’ll lay the other five.

Do that and you find out which armful you can set down without anything falling over.

— Marcus, at the table most mornings
