One cut, before it’s light, and I read what’s on it. That’s the whole method and I don’t vary it.

Three mornings running it has given me two. I have never had that, in fourteen years, and I’m not going to tell you it’s a message meant for you personally — it goes to everybody who reads me. These two are harder than the last pair, so take them slowly. The first is gentler than it looks. The second is kinder than its name.

And the thing that decides how you read them, {{ subscriber.first_name | capitalize }}, same as it was on Tuesday.

You have already chosen. The spread doesn’t decide for you — it’s how you find out what you already decided, some months ago, without saying it out loud.

Door one: the Hanged Man

A figure hanging upside down by one ankle from a living tree. Hands behind the back. One leg crossed easy behind the other. And a ring of light around the head, because he isn’t suffering — he’s looking.

Two things about that picture. The rope is on one ankle. The other leg is free and always was. And the tree is alive, which is the card telling you the waiting is doing something rather than nothing.

Maybe you’d call it giving it time. Maybe you’d call it waiting until he’s ready. Maybe you’d call it not wanting to be the one who ended it, which is the most honest of the three and the one people say last.

What the Hanged Man won’t tell me is how much longer this wait has in it. Some suspensions are a season and they end well. Some have been going four years and have become the life. The card looks identical either way.

The three behind this door say which yours is. One card can’t.

Door two: Death

An armoured rider on a white horse, carrying a black banner with a single white rose on it. A bishop standing to meet him, a child holding flowers, a fallen king. And behind all of it, between two towers, the sun.

This card does not mean a death and never has. It means the thing that is already over — and note that nobody in the picture is being killed. They’re meeting it. The bishop is standing up.

An ending isn’t a failure and it isn’t a verdict on the years. Things end that were good. Things end that nobody wrecked. You don’t need somebody to have behaved badly before you’re allowed to call a thing finished.

What Death won’t tell me is what’s on the other side of those towers. The card shows the sun and nothing past it, deliberately.

Three cards behind this door say what’s there. Two can’t.

What you’re looking at is called the Two Doors

Eight cards. Two you can see. Six you can’t.

Eight isn’t a number I picked. A door is three things — what it takes from you, what it hands you, and who you are on the other side of it — and those three point in different directions more often than not. Three behind each door, plus the two you’re standing between. Eight.

You’ve seen two of the eight. That’s a quarter of it.

And the six aren’t more of what you just read. These two told you what the choice is. The six tell you what each one costs — how much longer the wait has in it, what is actually on the far side of the ending, and who you’d be by the end of either. That is a different question, and two cards cannot hold it.

Here they are, still face down.

Behind door one
What it costs
What it gives
Who you become
Behind door two
What it costs
What it gives
Who you become

So let me lay the six behind these, and I’ll tell you which one you’re actually in.

Back to Death, and the detail that decides the card

The sun in that picture is rising, not setting. You can tell by which side of the towers it sits.

That is not decoration. It is the card arguing with its own name. Every version of this image for five hundred years has put the light on the far side of the ending, which is the oldest thing the deck has to say about endings: they are the earliest part of the next thing, not the last part of the old one.

So here’s the one to carry today. Don’t confuse a thing that is ending with a thing you are ending. One of those is happening to you and one is yours to do, and people spend years defending against the first as though it were the second.

It’s the longest of the seven I lay, the Two Doors, and I only lay it on a morning the cut gives me two. This week it has given me nothing else.

And the question you’re probably asking

Most people bring me some version of will he come back. That question has no answer in any spread I lay, because it isn’t about you and nobody’s cards tell you what another person will do.

Try this one: what has already ended that I’m still attending?

That has an answer, and it’s usually something small and specific — a routine, a role, a way of speaking to somebody that stopped being true. It sits in the six I haven’t laid.

So put that in front of me, in your own words.

What are your two doors?

Send it and I’ll lay the other six.

Do that and you stop waiting on a thing that finished without telling you.

— Marcus, at the table most mornings
