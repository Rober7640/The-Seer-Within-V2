### Where you are: the Four of Cups

`[IMG-2]`

A young man sitting under a tree with his arms crossed. Three cups on the grass in front of him, upright, doing nothing. And out of a small cloud at his shoulder, a hand is holding out a fourth.

He isn't looking at it. He's looking at the three.

That's the whole card and it's more uncomfortable than it first seems. **Nothing has been taken from him.** Nobody is against him. He has three cups. The fourth is being handed over, free, at arm's length, and he's got his eyes on the ones he already knows.

This is the card of the offer you've decided isn't for you before you've properly looked at it. And it is almost always something small. A message you've left unanswered for a fortnight. An invitation you keep meaning to say yes to. Somebody who has been perfectly clear and who you've decided is being polite.

Look at his arms, though. Folded. That's a decision. Sitting there is a thing he's doing, not a thing being done to him.

Now — what the Four of Cups won't tell me.

It shows me the cup is out. It doesn't tell me **why your arms are folded**, and there are only ever a few reasons: you've been handed something like it before and it cost you; you don't think you're owed it; or taking it would mean the three you've got weren't enough, and you'd rather they were.

Those are three completely different readings with three completely different instructions. The five cards behind this one say which is yours. One card can't.

### Back to the cup, and the part that catches people

The hand in that cloud doesn't stay out forever.

That's not a threat, %FIRSTNAME%, it's just how offers work. The person who sent the message stops sending it. The invitation gets extended to somebody else. Not out of spite — out of the ordinary way people read silence.

And here's the bit that stings. He won't remember refusing it. That's the thing about an offer you never turned your head to see: there's no moment of saying no. Nothing to regret later. You just look up in a year and the cloud's gone, and you'd swear you were never offered anything.

<!-- BEAT 13 · concrete ask -->
### In concrete terms, what do you do today?

One thing, and it takes a minute.

Name the fourth cup. Not what you should do about it — just name it. Say to yourself: *the thing being held out to me right now is ___.* Most people can fill that blank in under ten seconds, which tells you they knew all along.

You don't have to take it today. Turn your head. That's the whole instruction, and it's the only free part.

<!-- BEAT 14 · authority flourish. Craft + scarcity of access. ⛔ no price, no SLA -->
### Why a pointing card gets six and not one

A card that opens a fork needs cards on both sides of it. A card that *points* needs cards behind it, which is a different job.

So the five I lay on this one go: the step itself, what's actually in the way, what it asks of you, what changes if you take it, and what it costs you to keep sitting. That last one is the card people remember. Nobody thinks of not-deciding as a purchase, and it is one — it's simply paid in a currency you don't get a receipt for.

Six is the smallest spread I lay. I don't do less than that, because five of the six are the ones about *you*, and there's nothing for them to be about until you've told me what the cup is.
