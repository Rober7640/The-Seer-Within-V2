The Devil gets read as addiction. It isn’t. It’s a card about staying — and this morning it came up beside a card about leaving. If there’s somebody you’ve been deciding about for longer than you’d admit, this pair is about them, and the first sign of it shows in three or four days.

   

Two off one cut — not yet turned

{{ subscriber.first_name | capitalize }} —

Marcus Stone here.

I’m writing before the day starts because this pair won’t keep till tonight.

I don’t choose the cards I lay in the morning. I cut the deck once, before it’s light, and I read whatever is sitting on the cut. No shuffling until something feels right, no second cut, no laying it again if I don’t like what came up.

This morning the cut gave me two. Not one card with another underneath it — two, stuck face to face, come up off the cut together. In fourteen years I’ve seen that maybe a dozen times, and it never means here is your day. It means you’re standing at something.

Read them in the order I’ve laid them. The first door is the one you’re already standing in. The second is the one you keep deciding about and not deciding — take that one twice.

The last time a cut handed me two like that, it was years ago, above a shop I used to read out of. A woman called Hester. Same shape — two doors, both open.

She’d been standing between hers for nine years. Not that she couldn’t decide. That she’d been deciding for nine years and calling that a decision.

What her eight told her wasn’t which door to walk through. It was that she’d already walked through one a long time back, and had been standing in the doorway ever since with one foot out, so she’d never have to admit it. She stopped rehearsing after that. That’s all that changed, and it changed everything.

I’ll show you both doors in a moment, {{ subscriber.first_name | capitalize }}. First, the thing that decides how you read them.

You have already chosen. A choice isn’t a thing sitting in front of you waiting — it’s a thing you’ve been making, quietly, every morning for months. The spread doesn’t decide for you. It’s how you find out what you already decided.

Two cards is a quarter of an answer. The Two Doors is eight — the two you can see, and three behind each. Two tells you there’s a fork. Eight tells you what each door costs, what it gives, and who you turn into on the other side.

Door one: the Devil

A horned figure squatting on a block. Two people chained at the neck below it. And the chains are loose — wide loops that would lift straight off over their heads if either of them stood up.

Nobody in that picture is trapped. They’re staying.

Maybe you’d call it patience. Maybe you’d call it not the right time, not while his mother’s ill, not before the lease is up. Maybe you’d call it loving somebody through a rough patch that is now four years long.

The card doesn’t argue with any of those. It just counts the mornings.

And look again, because there are two of them chained to that block. Somebody is down there beside you, and you knew their name before I said there was one.

Here’s what the Devil won’t tell me. It shows me you’re staying. It doesn’t show me which of the two of you stands up first — and that decides everything. If it’s you, this ends one way. If it’s them, it ends another, and you find out by being left.

The three behind this door tell me which. One card can’t.

Door two: the Eight of Cups

A figure with a stick, walking uphill into rocky ground, away from eight cups stacked neat behind them. Not looking back. The moon’s out.

This isn’t impulsive leaving. The Eight of Cups is the leaving that comes after you’ve already tried — properly tried, more than once, in ways nobody else saw.

Eight cups is not nothing. They’re upright, they’re arranged, they took years. This is the door where you go, and it costs you every one of them.

The cups are the specifics, and they’re never dramatic. The friends who came with him. The Sunday routine. The version of the story you’ve told your mother for six years. Whatever you’d have to start explaining.

And none of it has to have been terrible. That’s what makes this card hard rather than sad. There can still be love in it. There are usually good years in it. You don’t have to hate a thing to be finished with it, and you don’t need somebody to blame before you’re allowed to go.

What the Eight of Cups won’t tell me is what’s at the top of that hill. The figure walks into the dark and the card does not show the other side, so I can’t say from here whether the walk buys you a year or costs you one. From the bottom of the hill those look identical.

Three cards behind this door say which. Two can’t.

What you’re looking at is called the Two Doors

Eight cards. Two you can see. Six you can’t.

Eight isn’t a number I picked. A door is three things — what it takes from you, what it hands you, and who you are on the other side of it — and those three point in different directions more often than not. Plenty of doors cost you almost nothing and leave you somebody you don’t want to be. So it’s three behind each door, plus the two you’re standing between. Eight.

You’ve seen two of the eight. That’s a quarter of it.

And the six aren’t more of what you just read. These two told you what the choice is. The six tell you what each one costs — which specific thing you’d lose, what you’d get in its place, and who you’d be by the end of it. That is a different question, and two cards cannot hold it.

Here they are, still face down.

Back to the Devil, and the part I want you to sit with

The loose chains are the whole card, {{ subscriber.first_name | capitalize }}. Nothing in that picture is holding anybody. It’s a card about staying — and staying is an act, done daily, by somebody who could leave.

Which means the thing keeping you there was never them, and it was never the situation. It’s that walking out would mean admitting how long you stayed. And I can tell you what that admission actually costs. It is not the thing people brace for.

It’s the longest of the seven I lay, the Two Doors, and I only lay it on a morning the cut gives me two. That happened today.

So here is the thing I would have you take out of this pair, whatever you do about it. Don’t confuse a decision you keep making with a decision you haven’t made. Hester lost nine years to that one sentence.

Your job this morning isn’t to pick a door. It’s to notice you’ve been standing in one.

And the question you’re probably asking

Most people bring me some version of will he change. I’ll be straight with you: that question has no answer in any spread I lay, because it isn’t about you. Nobody’s cards tell you what somebody else is going to do.

Try this one instead: what am I still getting from staying?

That one has an answer. Something is being paid out or you’d have gone — safety, a story that still works, not having to be the one who ended it. And it sits in the six cards I haven’t laid.

So put that question in front of me, in your own words, and I’ll lay them.

I’m not going to tell you to manifest a door open. Doors don’t move because somebody wanted them to, and anyone selling you that is selling you the wishing, not the reading.

What a spread does is cheaper and more useful than that. It tells you what each option actually costs before you pay it.

Which is why a fork gets eight and not four. Most readers answer a choice with a two-card cross — one for this, one for that, ten minutes. But a door isn’t one thing. It’s what it takes from you, what it hands you, and who you are at the end of it, and those three point in different directions more often than not. Plenty of doors cost almost nothing and leave you somebody you don’t want to be.

So it’s three to a door. Six behind the two, plus the two you’re looking at. It’s the longest of the seven I lay, and I only lay it on a morning the cut gives me two. That happened today.

So — what are you still getting from staying?

Tell me that, and I’ll lay the other six.

Do that and you stop making this decision by default, at six in the morning, without noticing.

— Marcus, at the table most mornings
