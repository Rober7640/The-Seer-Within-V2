The bracelet my own teacher put on my wrist

She handed it to me years ago and only told me one thing to go with it. It took me the better part of a year to understand what she meant.

Dear {{ subscriber.first_name | capitalize }},

Evelyn here.

I want to tell you about the year I nearly gave this work up — not the reading itself, the money from it.

Clients paid me. That was never the problem.

They said yes, they booked, and then somewhere between the yes and the payment actually landing, something always happened. A cheque delayed. A booking cancelled the morning of, the deposit refunded "as a courtesy."

I was doing the work. I just never seemed to keep what it earned me.

My own teacher noticed before I ever said a word about it.

One evening she took a small bracelet off her own left wrist — black stone, a little carved creature sitting on top of it — and put it straight into my hand.

"Wear this until you understand why," was all she said.

It took me the better part of a year to understand why, and I don't want you taking a year over it the way I did.

Most of what's sold to you as wealth-luck only talks about one half of the problem — pulling money toward you that isn't near you yet. That's real, dear, and Pi Chiu does that too. I'll show you how.

But almost nobody talks about the other half: what happens to money once it's already close. For me, that was the actual problem. Money was already finding me. It just wasn't staying once it arrived.

I hear both shapes of this constantly now — money that never quite gets close, and money that gets close and then doesn't stay.

If either one is familiar, keep reading, because the creature my teacher put on my wrist was built to do both.

His name is Pi Chiu.

Two heads, not two horns — some tellings give him one horn, or two, and leave it there. That isn't this one.

He carries two dragon heads on a single lion's body, command sitting on top of guardianship.

One head pulls: it reaches for what's already somewhere within your reach, and closes the last of that distance toward you.

The other head keeps: once a thing is in his mouth, it does not leave that mouth again.

Both jobs matter equally, dear. A creature that only pulled would just be handing you the same problem I had. A creature that only kept would have nothing to work with.

Every part of him is built around this — pulling what's real closer, then holding it.

The mouth is wide, built to take in more than a small mouth could hold.

The face is fierce on purpose, to turn away bad luck before it gets close.

The claws aren't decoration — they're what a guardian uses. And where you see a paw resting on something round, that's the part that draws wealth in from every direction, not just the one he happens to be facing.

Here's the part that made me sit with this creature longer than I usually sit with any of them.

Look at where his tail is. Sealed. Smooth.

There's a version of this story where the Jade Emperor kept Pi Chiu in his own court, and the creature ate the entire imperial treasury, swallowed it whole. Rather than kill him for it, the Emperor sealed him shut.

No exit. Wealth goes into that mouth, and there is nowhere in his body for it to come back out.

That was meant as a punishment. It's the reason people have kept him close ever since.

I think of a woman named Marisol when I explain the keeping half. She did freelance styling work — invoices, deposits, the slow business of getting paid for creative work.

Her trouble was never that clients said no. They said yes constantly.

Then the deposit invoice would sit, unpaid and uncancelled, for months. Not dead. Just suspended, the way a held breath isn't the same as an exhale.

She wore this same charm on her left wrist and wrote one sentence for the capsule — not "more clients," a specific name and a specific number, the retainer she'd already half written off.

Weeks later that client paid the full amount, late, with an apology, and brought a referral with him.

I won't promise you that exact shape. What changed in her wasn't more work coming in. It was work that had already arrived, finally staying arrived.

What I've had made is a bracelet.

Black agate underneath — a grounding stone, chosen because it holds a charge instead of losing it, which is the whole point here.

A small cast Pi Chiu sits above the stone, both heads facing out.

Worked into the beads themselves is Om Mani Padme Hum, the compassion mantra — not stamped on for decoration, worked in because this isn't a piece about greed.

It's a piece about drawing what's real a little closer, and then holding it once it's yours.

There's a small sealed capsule built into it, meant to hold one sentence, written by you, before you put the bracelet on.

Not "more money" — that's too wide for either head to work with. The actual thing. The client's name. The deal. The specific cheque that went quiet.

Give Pi Chiu something to pull toward, and something to hold onto.

This is the one I'd want on your wrist →

Left wrist, dear. Never the right.

The left is the side that receives — it sits nearer your heart, and this is a creature built around receiving as well as guarding what's received.

The right wrist sends. That's the opposite of the job here.

I want to be honest with you about what this does and doesn't do, because I'd rather you trust me in ten years than believe me for one week.

The pulling head only reaches for what's real. It doesn't conjure a deal that was never going to happen for you at all. If a client was never going to pay you, Pi Chiu can't reach into a place that has nothing in it.

The keeping head only holds what actually arrives. It doesn't multiply it.

So he isn't a creature of magic. He's a creature of pull-and-keep — what's already true, what's already somewhere in reach, he draws a little closer, and then he doesn't let it slip back out the way it's been slipping.

That's a smaller promise than most of what's sold in this space. I think it's also the only one that's actually true.

Let me have one sealed for you →

Write your sentence before it arrives, not after. Seal it. Wear him on the left. Let him pull it closer, then let him keep it.

I'm on your side in this, dear.

— Evelyn
