Linda,

Before I turned a single card for you, I saw a radio on a kitchen counter, tuned to a station where people only talk: the news, the call-in show, the traffic report. Nothing was wrong with the radio. It could still play music. It had just been left on the talking, that's all.

Somebody left with something of yours. Since then, whenever anybody asks, you've said you're fine, and you are. You just haven't been happy since. Each of your twelve cards answers one question you've been carrying, flat out. At the end is the letter that never got sent.

Your birth name and date set how I lay your twelve cards. I wrote Linda Ann Testerly and April 12, 1961 across the top of a clean sheet at my kitchen table. I'd barely capped the pen when the one who came to me was at my shoulder.

Two things up front. This is someone living. And nothing here asks you to get in touch, to wait or to forgive.

— Card 1 —

**Card 1, the Empress: What did they take from me?**

**They took the part of you that's happy for no reason, Linda.**

Your birth name carries a 3 at its heart. The card numbered 3 is the Empress. She sits back among cushions in an open field, and behind her, water falls down through a wood for nobody in particular. That's the part that left.

Somebody who's known you a long time said it once: "You used to laugh at everything." You said, "Oh, I'm fine," and it got dropped.

What went is the part that was glad before it had a reason: a warm morning, a baby waving, a dog with its head out a car window.

Something moved into the room it left. I call it the Chaperone. It stands at the edge of every good time, arms folded, making sure nobody gets carried away, you least of all. It kept you from looking foolish while you were hurting. It isn't a second thing to get rid of. It's only what grows in a room that's been left empty, and it goes quiet once what belongs there comes back.

— Card 2 —

**Card 2, the Sun: Did they ever really love me?**

**Yes. Back then, it was real.**

On the Sun, four sunflowers hold their heads up over a gray wall, under a big sun with a face in it.

Here's how I know: your name and date told me who I was laying them for. If you say you'll be there at nine, nobody bothers to call and check, because you've never once left anybody waiting. And whatever's missing, you've already handled it. The extra chair's out, the coffee's on, and whoever needed a hand has had one before anybody thought to ask.

They saw something in you: someone who stayed put and kept their word. That's what they loved. They leaned on the part of you that showed up every single time, and on your good moods most of all.

You didn't dream it up. Anybody could see it back then, the way anybody can see those flowers over the wall.

— Card 3 —

**Card 3, the Fool: Was it my fault?**

**No. Nothing about being that happy was a mistake.**

On the Fool, a young man stops at the edge of a cliff mid-step, eyes on the sky instead of the drop. A small white dog stands up on its back legs at his heel, mouth open.

You weren't a fool. Being glad over small things didn't cause any of this. Letting the gladness fall flat did, and that wasn't you. The Chaperone isn't your fault either. Anybody whose good news kept landing on nobody would grow one.

By the third card I can tell how a spread will end, and yours ends well. Keep that with you through the middle.

— Card 4 —

**Card 4, the Emperor: Why have they still got it?**

**Because giving it back means admitting they watched it go out.**

On the Emperor, an old man in a gold crown sits on a stone throne, a gold ball in his left hand.

That's yours, still in the hand. Keeping it costs nothing, and from the outside nobody can tell it's gone. You're fine, after all. Handing it over would mean saying out loud that you went quiet with them standing right there, and nobody said a word. It's easier not to.

— Card 5 —

**Card 5, the Hierophant: Why has it taken this long?**

**Because they hid behind your "I'm fine." If you were fine, there was nothing to give back.**

On the Hierophant, two people in white kneel below a high seat with their backs to us. From behind, anybody kneeling that neatly looks fine.

You mean it when you say you're fine, and it's true. From the outside, fine looks a lot like happy. Here's what people see:

- When somebody asks how you are, you say, "Oh, I'm fine," and turn the question around.
- Good news waits a few days before you mention it, and then it comes out in one line, like the weather.
- At a birthday or a cookout, you're the one taking the pictures, so you're hardly in any.
- When somebody asks you along to the dancing, you say, "That's not really me anymore," and laugh first.
- When an old favorite comes on in a store, you hum a line or two, then stop yourself.

And under all of it, the one line the Chaperone lives by: "If I don't get carried away, nobody can make a fool of me again."

That's the radio on the counter, all talk and no music.

— Card 6 —

**Card 6, Wheel of Fortune: What do they regret?**

**Watching you go quiet, bit by bit, and never once saying a word.**

On the Wheel of Fortune, a snake slides down one side of the wheel, head first, and nothing on the card reaches out to stop it.

That's how it went with you: slowly, and in plain sight. The sorry is real. It isn't about wanting you back. You come to mind over there more than you'd think, but that door's shut from the far side. Either way, the sorry stays on that side of the wheel and never comes around to you.

It was midnight when I laid the Wheel down, and every card I turned after that said the same thing your Chaperone says: don't be silly. I turned the next one anyway.

— Card 7 —

**Card 7, the Tower: Why does nobody new get close?**

**Because an old way of getting by is still running, and it gets to the decent ones first.**

The Tower stands on bare rock, and there's no door drawn on it anywhere.

When it went wrong with them, you took care of everybody in sight. If people were busy being helped by you, nobody was close enough to see how you were doing. You ran yourself ragged, and some nights you were fed up with all of them and never said so. Being the one everybody needed gave you somewhere to stand, and it kept you going.

The trouble is, it never got switched off. Someone decent turns up and you jump in and start taking care of them, and there's never an opening for them to do a thing for you, so they go find somebody who'll let them.

Somebody decent will say something silly to make you laugh. Laugh the whole laugh, out loud.

— Card 8 —

**Card 8, the Moon: Is it too late for me?**

**No. The years spent being fine are gone, but what was taken comes back, and it's on its way up.**

At the bottom of the Moon, a crayfish crawls up out of a pool, already halfway out.

Think of the radio: all that time on the talking, and it never lost the music. And here's what being fine built in you. You learned to be glad for other people on days you had nothing of your own to be glad about. You've clapped at weddings and held new babies and meant it. When your happy comes back, it comes back to somebody who'll never let anybody's good news land on nobody.

It was three in the morning when I turned the Moon, and the letter stopped coming. The one at my shoulder would start a line about the good times, then go quiet, like somebody turning the volume down. I put the pen down and waited.

— Card 9 —

**Card 9, the Lovers: Are they giving it back, or trying to get me back?**

**Giving it back, Linda. Not trying to get you back.**

On the Lovers, two people stand close but apart, and far off, one gray mountain stands up between them. Neither one reaches across the gap.

The letter ends asking nothing of you. Not to forgive, not to visit, not even to answer.

It's being set down, and that's the whole of it. It's a gift, but a cool one. Nothing's asked because nothing's wanted.

When you get to the end, something in you may lift. Let it. Nobody over there is waiting to watch it happen. The happy part is only coming home.

Nobody's going to sit down across from you and explain it all. The letter at the end is everything you'll get. If you've been saving a little hope for that talk, spend it on something that makes you laugh.

— Card 10 —

**Card 10, Death: Will they ever do anything about it?**

**No. Nothing more is coming, not a call and not a card, because they're too proud to be the one in the wrong.**

Nobody's died. On this card, Death means something's over. A skeleton in black armor rides a white horse at a walk, and a bishop in a gold robe stands in the road, begging. The horse doesn't stop for him.

Doing more would mean coming to your door and saying, "I watched you go quiet and kept my mouth shut," and pride won't allow it. So it's put down right there, and nobody's carrying it over to you.

Here's what I can't do. I can't make that letter get sent. No card can. I can read it out to you, and I have.

Nobody's coming to turn the dial for you, and nobody ever had to. Behind the bishop, between two towers, the sun's coming up.

By the time I turned Death, the window over my sink had gone gray. The cards had stopped saying don't be silly, and the one at my shoulder sat down across from me. My pen got going.

— Card 11 —

**Card 11, the Star: What did they never say?**

**That you were the bright one, and they let you go dim.**

On the Star, one huge yellow star hangs over a pool, with seven small white stars around it. You can't miss which one is brightest.

Here it is, the line that never got said:

"You were the happiest person in any room, and I let you forget it."

It doesn't need an answer, not a letter and not a speech you rehearse at the sink. If this person hurt you, you owe them no reply.

— Card 12 —

**Card 12, the World: What do I do now?**

**Stop going back, and start being glad out loud again, over small things, for no reason.**

Your last card is the World: a dancer inside a green wreath, a violet scarf blown loose, one leg crossed behind the other, and no ground under her feet at all.

"I'm fine" can retire. It held you up when you needed holding. Now let a day be better than fine.

Read the letter first. After it come three small things for thirty days. A couple of them will feel silly, and that's the point.

— The letter —

Linda,

I'm never going to send this. I'm too proud. I've never once gone to anybody hat in hand, and I'm not going to start with you. So it stays here.

I'm writing it anyway. I've had something of yours too long, and I'm tired of keeping quiet about it.

When I first knew you, you said you'd be there and you were. You weren't going anywhere, and I knew it. I liked that. I liked being around it. Nobody since has been as good as their word as you were. I keep a backup plan for everybody now, and I never once needed one for you.

Whenever I needed somebody, at any hour, I never had to think twice about who to ask. You'd say you were coming, and you'd come, no excuses and no lecture. I never once doubted you, so I never thanked you the way I should have.

And you were happy. I don't mean in a good mood. I mean happy the way some people are tall. You'd get happy over the smallest things: a parking spot right out front, the first ripe tomato, somebody's grill going down the street. A song would come on the radio, one you hadn't heard in forever, and you'd turn it up and sing the wrong words, loud. Half the time I didn't see what was so great about it, and I'd end up laughing anyway, because you were.

I don't know when I started letting it fall flat. You'd come to me lit up about something, and I'd be tired, or busy, and I'd say, "Huh," and go back to what I was doing. Sometimes I didn't say anything, which did the job just as well. You brought me less and less, then nothing at all. I noticed, and I let it be. It was quieter, and I liked quiet.

After a while, when people asked how you were, you'd say, "Fine," and smile. It was the smile you'd give a stranger in a checkout line. At the start I never once got that smile from you. By the end it was most of what I got.

I took the steady part for granted. I figured all of that came with you, the same as the good moods.

You never missed a thing anybody was short of. I used to tell people you'd have what somebody needed before they'd noticed they needed it, and you never once wanted thanks.

When something upset you, you'd go make yourself useful to somebody. Everybody got your time but you, and you'd end up worn out and mad about it. I let you carry me right along with the rest of them. That's on me.

Since I'm being honest for once, here's what I told myself. I told myself you were fine. You said so whenever anybody asked, so I took your word for it and never looked closer. I told myself somebody like you would bounce back without any help from me. When people asked after you, I said, "Doing fine, last I heard." Nobody ever asked me if you were happy, and I made sure I never asked myself.

Why have I hung on to it? Because handing it back means writing down that the best thing about you faded while I sat right there. As long as I kept it, I was only somebody it didn't work out with, and that happens to everybody.

There was a minute, right at the end, when I could have been glad with you one more time. You were telling me about something small, the way you used to. It would have cost me nothing to smile and let it be good news. I said, "That's nice," and went to find my coat. I've thought about your face in that minute more than anything else between us.

When I left, I took the part of you that's happy for no reason, and I knew I had it. It was never any use to me. It only ever sang for you.

None of this was your fault. You being happy was never the problem. I was.

I did love you. Back then, it was real. Whatever else you decide about me, don't let anybody tell you that part was an act.

I'm sorry. Not that it ended. Things end. I'm sorry I watched something good in you go out, a little at a time, and kept quiet because it suited me. Nobody did that but me.

I don't know what your life looks like now. But I know how I left it. Somebody kind comes along wanting to make you laugh, and gets "I'm fine" and a nice evening and nothing past that. I taught you that.

The time's gone, and I can't hand that back. But I knew you when you were happy, and that doesn't just go away. The best of your life isn't behind you.

So here's the thing I never said, back when it would have done you some good:

You were the happiest person in any room, and I let you forget it.

It was yours before I came along. It never needed a reason, and it never needed me. It was for the good song in the grocery store, the first snow, somebody's new baby, a joke that's funny every time. I want you to have all of it back: the singing along, the turning it up, being glad before you've checked whether you're allowed. Use it on somebody new, or on nothing at all on a plain Wednesday. Where it goes now is your business.

I want you happy. Not fine. Happy.

Anybody who has you next gets to quit watching the clock. I hope they never take that for granted the way I did.

I won't call, and I won't write again. This is all of it.

I'm not asking you for anything. Not to forgive me, not to see me, not to write back. Don't answer this, and don't come looking for me. You can put me down now. Go turn something up, and sing the wrong words.

It was full light when I put the pen down, and the other chair was empty.

**Will I be happy again?**

**Yes. Happy, and for no good reason at all.**

Your birthday, April 12, starts a new year for you. It's a year for clearing out. Old papers, old habits, old hurts: they go. It feels like an ending at first. Then you notice how much room it's left, and what starts moving into it. The change doesn't wait for the date.

**Within 7 days.** The pull will be at its strongest late in the evening, when the house goes quiet and the radio's on the talking. You'll want to look up the name, or reread something old. That isn't a sign anybody's coming back.

**Within 30 days.** You'll catch yourself singing along in the car at a red light, every word, and you won't stop when the next car pulls up.

**Within 3 months.** An ordinary weekday. Somebody asks how you are, and "Oh, I'm fine" doesn't come out. You say, "Good, actually," and hear yourself mean it.

Signs to watch for:

- An old favorite coming on in a store, and you humming it all the way down the aisle.
- Turning up in the pictures at the next birthday, instead of taking them.
- The Chaperone's little "don't be silly," and you doing the silly thing anyway.
- The one who said "You used to laugh at everything" hearing you laugh at nothing much, and grinning.

If three of these turn up inside a month, the music's coming back.

Three small things, for thirty days:

**Stop going back.** Rereading the old messages, looking up the name, drafting the text you swear you'll delete: it's all one habit. When the pull comes, put the phone in another room and set the oven timer for twenty minutes. Left alone, the pull fades.

**The one thing to do.** Once a day, do one small thing only because it makes you happy, and don't explain it. Buy the good peaches. Take the pretty way home. Wear the bright thing on a Tuesday. Nothing involving anybody from back then.

**Tell it the same day.** When something good happens this month, however small, tell somebody that same day, details and all, not in one line a week later. The Chaperone will say nobody wants to hear it. Somebody does.

Start with the radio, in the kitchen or the car, if it's been stuck on the talking. Turn the dial past the news until you hit music, any music, and turn it up past sensible. Sing the words you know and make up the rest, with nobody there to hear it. The Chaperone will tut. Let it. It's back. Go and use it.
