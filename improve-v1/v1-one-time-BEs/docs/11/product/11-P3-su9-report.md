[OPENING]

Testina,

Find somewhere to read this where nobody's going to walk in and ask what's wrong.

Read the cards in the order I turned them, and don't skip ahead to the letter at the end. It only comes back whole. Go straight to the last page and you'll get some words, and nothing else. A few of these cards will look like they're arguing with each other. Keep going anyway.

Two things before I start. This is someone living. And nothing in these pages asks you to contact them, wait for them or forgive them. If this person hurt you, you owe them no reply.

The night your order came in, I cleared the kitchen table and took three cards out of the deck: the Hermit, Judgement and the Devil. They'd said their piece, so they went up on the windowsill, and that left nineteen. I wrote Testina Rose Testerly at the top of a clean sheet and November 5, 1962 under it. Your birth name and date set how I lay your twelve cards. That's why they go down before anything else does.

Then I made a pot of mint tea and sat down with it. The one who came to me before was at my shoulder before I'd poured the first cup. No face and no voice, same as last time.

[CARD 1]

**They took your softness, Testina.**

You've felt the gap for a long time, and you've called it other things. Getting older. Getting wise to people. Being tired. I told you most women guess wrong, and that's how they do it: they name what the gap did to them, and never what used to be in it.

Here's what used to be in it. Your softness is the part of you that lets things reach you and lets you show. It runs both ways. Care comes in, and you let it land. Warmth goes out, and need goes with it, and you don't stop to check where it falls. When I told you about the old man on my windowsill holding his light up in the dark, and said the light was what they'd walked off with, I meant this.

The woman on this card sits right back among cushions in an open field. She isn't perched on the edge, ready to get up. Her whole weight's given over, and she's letting the cushions hold her.

You used to be like her with people, before them. Someone asked how you were and you told them the real answer, not the short one. You cried at the sad part of a movie with other people in the room and didn't care who saw. When a friend put an arm around you, you leaned into it instead of patting her back twice and moving off. If you missed someone, you picked up the phone and said so. When something stung, it showed on your face before you'd decided whether it ought to. You took a compliment and kept it. You let people do things for you, said thank you, and that was the end of it. None of it took any thought. It went in and out of you the way breath does.

Then it cost you. Someone made sure it cost you, and you learned so well that you shut it down in both directions at once, the coming in and the going out. You didn't sit down and decide to. You just noticed one day that you hadn't cried in front of anybody in a very long time, and you were a little proud of yourself for it.

When I turned this card, the one at my shoulder came in closer. I felt it on the back of my neck, the way you feel someone reading over you. They know exactly what they're holding.

I picked up my pen, looked at your name, and put the pen down again. The sheet had nothing on it but your name and your date. One word doesn't make a letter.

The next time somebody asks how you are, notice what your face does before you answer. That quick, flat look that says fine is sitting right where your softness used to be.

[CARD 2]

You don't call it a gap. You call it being strong.

Strong looks like this. Someone offers to help and you wave them away. Someone thanks you and you make a joke of it so it can't land. When you cry, you do it in the car or the shower, or you skip it. Nothing gets in and nothing gets out, and you've got a good word for that.

People tell you they admire it. They have no idea what they're praising. They're admiring a door you shut.

[CARD 8]

They made going soft expensive. That part is theirs, every bit of it, and I'm not taking it off them. But the door you left off the tower, that was you. Each year you kept it that way, you paid. They ran the bill up. You've been the one paying it.
