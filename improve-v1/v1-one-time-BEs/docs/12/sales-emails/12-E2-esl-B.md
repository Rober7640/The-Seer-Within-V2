### How your numbers work

Your numbers come from two things: the day you were born and the name you were given.

The calendar has numbers too. Every month carries one, and so does every single day. On most days, the calendar's numbers and yours don't line up. On a few days they do. I call those your open days. You get twelve in 2027, one in every month.

On an open day, you're easy for luck to find. The rest of the year, your numbers still work for you wherever you put them: on a ticket, in your PIN, on your front door.

Most women never get told any of this. So their open days go by like any other day. They stay in. They put the call off till Monday. And luck comes round, finds nobody home, and moves on.

Here's why nobody tells you. A newspaper horoscope is written for millions of people at once. Your numbers are yours alone. Somebody has to sit down and work them out, and almost nobody does.

### Work out your first number. It takes a minute.

Find a pen and the back of an envelope.

Write your birth date as numbers: the month, the day and the full year.

Here's a made-up date, so you can see how it goes. Say someone was born on March 14, 1961. March is the 3rd month. So you write 3, then 14, then 1961.

Now add up every digit, one at a time:

**3 + 1 + 4 + 1 + 9 + 6 + 1 = 25**

You've still got two digits, so add those as well:

**2 + 5 = 7**

When there's one digit left, you stop. That date gives a 7.

Now do yours. If you land on a two-digit number, keep adding. A 10 becomes 1 + 0, which is 1. You'll always finish on a number from 1 to 9.

Got it? Here's what yours says.

### Arm B, and how it differs from A

- **This is arm B: the hand-over list, with no win promised.** Arm A ([12-E2-esl-A](12-E2-esl-A.md)) promises small miracles flatly (a raffle, a cheque, a yes).
- **Differs from A in:** subject, headline, deck, bold line 1, the opening section ("What you'll be holding" here, "What a small miracle is" in A), one sentence in "How your numbers work" ("you're easy for luck to find" vs "small miracles find you easily"), the lottery bullet, the `c=11` and `c=14` labels, the first paragraphs of the close, and the link codes (`c=11…15` here, `c=1…5` in A).
- **Shared with A, word for word:** the promise not to chase, the free first number and the 1–9 lines, the ticket family, "what your first number can't tell you", the stop-number, the uses list apart from the lottery bullet, the ask, the "what you get" list, January and the empathy lines. Keep edits in step across both files, or the test measures the edit, not the arm.
- **Arm B promises no win.** The lottery bullet keeps "I'm not promising you a win, and nobody honest can". The headline and line 1 promise only things the reading really holds: six numbers, four digits, twelve days. No money words beyond "spend money you need" (the promise) and "pension" (number 4's paperwork).
- **Five links, no P.S.** `c=11` is the one bold first-person text link. `c=12` and `c=13` are sentence links. `c=14` is the button-style `###` link. `c=15` ends the body. The January reason and three lines of empathy sit between `c=13` and the button.

### Claims the product code must make true

- **Exactly one open day in every month of 2027.** Twelve, January to December. The first is in January.
- **First number = every digit of month, day and full year added, then added again until one digit is left. Always 1–9.** ⛔ No master numbers (11, 22, 33). A reader who worked out "2" must not get "11".
- **Exactly six ticket numbers.** B's headline says "the 6", so it can't be five or seven. Each adds down to one of her three lucky numbers (7 → 7, 16, 25, 34, 43). All different, and inside the range of the game she plays. ⚠ Ranges differ (UK Lotto 1–59, Powerball 1–69 plus 1–26, many raffles none). The letter stops the example at 43 so it fits any 1–49 game. Settle the range rule at B2.
- **If two or all three of her lucky numbers are the same**, the code must still give six different numbers within range. In a 1–49 game, families 1–4 give six (4 → 4, 13, 22, 31, 40, 49) but families 5–9 give only five (7 → 7, 16, 25, 34, 43). The code needs a rule for that.
- **Exactly four PIN digits, built from her numbers**, not containing the stop-number. Banks refuse weak PINs (1111, 1234), so the code should avoid them.
- **Numbers 2 and 3 come from the full birth name on the certificate.**
- **"One evening" and "within 24 hours"** both hold: "I'll sit with yours one evening. Within 24 hours, your reading is in your inbox." The product's SLA is 24 hours.
- **Inputs are birth name and birth date only** ("That's all I need from you").

### Things I wasn't sure of

1. **"The 4 for your PIN" became "the 4 digits for your PIN"** in the headline and line 1. "The 4" read as one number, the four.
2. **B's headline puts numbers in digits (6, 4, 12)** and the body writes them as words (six, four, twelve), as the rest of the letter does. Digits stand out in a headline.
3. **Subject pick is a list, not a story.** It matches what the operator asked for (the promise turned into uses), but it breaks the "one-line story with a twist" habit. Option 3 (the PIN) is the twist version, if a test of subjects inside arm B is wanted later.
4. **The 1–9 "when" lines are moments, not dates**, so they can't clash with her twelve days. Keep it that way.
5. **12-A:** after about February, "2027" and "your first open day is in January" stop being true.
6. **No emoji in the subjects**, to match 11. Easy to test.
