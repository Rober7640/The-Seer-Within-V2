### Work out your first number. It takes a minute.

Find a pen and the back of an envelope.

Write your birth date as numbers: the month, the day and the full year.

Here's a made-up date, so you can see how it goes. Say someone was born on March 14, 1961. March is the 3rd month. So you write 3, then 14, then 1961.

Now add up every digit, one at a time:

**3 + 1 + 4 + 1 + 9 + 6 + 1 = 25**

You've still got two digits, so add those as well:

**2 + 5 = 7**

When there's one digit left, you stop. That date gives a 7.

Now do yours. If you land on a two-digit number, keep adding. A 10 becomes 1 + 0, which is 1. You'll always finish on a number from 1 to 9.

Got it? Here's what yours says.

## Build notes

- **The named effect is "open days".** It's in the headline, the deck, line 1, two subheads, the CTA labels and the close. Two other names, if the operator prefers:
  - **"marked days"** — echoes the big idea word for word ("your birth date has already marked…"), but it can read as "marked for something bad".
  - **"lucky windows"** — plainer to a new reader, but "window" is a concept-noun and it's 11-and-02's "five to seven day window" word. Weaker.
- **Five links, no P.S.** `c=1` is the one bold first-person text link (mid-pitch, right after the free taste and the stop-number). `c=2` and `c=3` are permission-verb sentence links, as in 11. `c=4` is the button-style `###` link. `c=5` ends the body. Between `c=3` and the button sit the January reason and three lines of empathy, so the two closing asks aren't side by side. Every label works for a reader who did nothing (none says "I've worked out my number…").
- **No price, no win, no cheque, no sum.** The only money words are "spend money you need" (the promise), "a pension" (a type of paper in number 4) and "business/deal/offer" (kinds of luck, no amounts). The lottery bullet says outright that no win is promised.
- **Numbers read openly** ("I read numbers as well as cards"), as the operator decided at Q1. No tarot, no card art, no cards drawn. Banned words checked: no "energy field", "clearing", "the universe". No testimonials. No invented people: the worked example is a made-up date and says so ("Say someone was born on…").

### Claims the product code must make true

- **Exactly one open day in every month of 2027.** Twelve, January to December. The letter says "one in every month" four times.
- **The first open day is in January.** Follows from the line above, but the January section depends on it.
- **First number = add every digit of month, day and full year, then keep adding until one digit is left. Always 1–9.** ⛔ No master numbers (11, 22, 33). If the code keeps 11 as her life path, a reader who worked out "2" in the letter gets "11" in the reading, and it looks like an error. Code and letter must use the same sum.
- **Numbers 2 and 3 come from the full birth name on the birth certificate** (expression and soul urge in the WORKFLOW table).
- **The stop-number is not one of her three** and clashes with them. The rule for "clashes" is still to settle at B2.
- **In her inbox within 24 hours.**
- **Inputs are birth name and birth date only**, entered on the booking page ("Nothing else goes into it"). The booking page must not ask for anything else the reading uses.
- **"Every use above, with your own numbers filled in."** All seven bullets, not a subset.

### Things I wasn't sure of

1. **The 1–9 "when" lines are moments, not calendar dates.** I first wrote "first week of a month", "plain weekdays" and "late in the year". Each could clash with her twelve dates (a 9 with an open day in March). So every "when" is now a kind of moment: when she starts something, on the second try, as something closes. Keep it that way in edits.
2. **"I prepare it from your birth name and your birth date."** Code works out every number (the WORKFLOW ⛔ rule). I kept Evelyn's line to "I prepare it", which 11 also does, and left out "by hand" or "I check every one".
3. **"The two numbers they give are different for almost every woman who reads this."** True in practice (each is one of 1–9 from a different name), but two readers can share them. "Almost" covers it. Cut it if it feels like a stretch.
4. ~~"Your horoscope gives the same day to everyone born under your sign."~~ Softened in review (2026-11-02) to "a newspaper horoscope is written for millions of people at once", so it no longer knocks astrology (the deck sells Luna). Also in review: the `c=3` link said "give me your say-so", which echoed the tick the operator removed on 11. It now reads "put your birth name and date on this page".
5. **The January section is a real window, not a decay device.** It works only while 2027 is still ahead. ⛔ 12-A: after about February the letter's "2027" and "your first open day is in January" stop being true. A later send needs the "next twelve months" version.
6. **The stop-number "sits in one of them, or close to it".** "Or close to it" lets the product say where to look without claiming it found the number. If B2 settles a rule that always finds it in one of the three places, cut "or close to it".
7. **Subject emoji.** The list data says a leading emoji helps (📅 or ⚠️). 11-E2 has none, so I left them off. Easy to test.
8. **Long-copy length.** The body runs inside the 1,400–2,000 brief. The 1–9 list is the bulk of the free taste. Don't trim it: every reader has to find her own number there.
