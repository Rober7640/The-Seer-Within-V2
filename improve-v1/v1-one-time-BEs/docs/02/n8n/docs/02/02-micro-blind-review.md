## House 3 — B wins

Confidence: moderately high.

B teaches the distinction between kinds of speech more effectively and initially leaves the reader more dignity. Two exact quotations explain the preference:

- B: “Silence can be a decision, complete in itself, requiring no explanation to anyone.” This supplies a useful category that protects the reader's agency.
- A: “Someone who insists on perfect conditions before replying is, in practice, declining.” This substitutes a verdict about the reader for an explanation of the tradeoff.

B's short pivot from fluency to sorting helps pace an otherwise abstract passage. Its taxonomy makes the final application understandable. A's claim that answering resolves a matter permanently is an unearned leap from the image, and its public-answer prescription becomes unusually intrusive for a house with no fixed personal prediction owed.

Promise omissions: none under the supplied house-3 contract.

Other contract concerns: both introduce an unsupported personal-history summons with a uniquely expiring opportunity. A adds certainty about the audience and the reader's inability to choose the setting. Both turn interpretation into unnecessary directives. B's acknowledgment of legitimate silence is undercut by its later claim that postponement spends the option and its accusatory ending about speaking without saying anything.

Worst surviving defect in B: it makes a thoughtful distinction, then uses a once-only opportunity and a scolding final sentence to pressure compliance. The central lesson could remain firm without this added personal prophecy.

## House 5 — A wins narrowly

Confidence: moderate. Neither candidate reaches recommendable writing overall, and A's extra forecast is a substantial defect.

A is somewhat less coercive and its transition from romantic recognition to home and work is easier to follow. Two exact quotations explain the preference:

- A: “Recognition on that day will not be a pleasant possibility you can hold in reserve.” This still overstates the consequences of recognition, but keeps the consequence closer to changed knowledge.
- B: “You get the man, and you lose the version of yourself who had not yet chosen him.” This jumps from the required recognition forecast to a guaranteed relationship and an imposed choice, treating the reader's agency as already extinguished.

B's explicit fifth-week timing is cleaner than A's day interval. However, its repeated inevitability language, capitalized TWIN FLAME, and claims that the course cannot be altered weigh down both emotional respect and the desire to continue. A's explanation of recognition versus arrival is direct enough to follow without that closing escalation. Both spend too long repeating that motion has already begun, and both present symbolic associations as if they logically establish particulars.

Promise omissions: B explicitly supplies every listed element. A supplies renewal versus arrival, twin flame, known man, manner and practical activity, another card as evidence, support, house 4/home, and a surrendered non-romantic allocation-of-hours decision. Its timing is explicit and relative, but “Between the sixteenth and the twenty-third day” straddles relative weeks; if the contract literally requires one named relative week, A misses that form of the timing promise.

Other contract concerns: A adds a six-day name-mention forecast that the contract does not require. It also invents a socially arranged gathering and the intentions of others. B adds a reason for the supposed delay, inevitability, and a guaranteed romantic outcome. Both claim more about the man's awareness or intentions than the required manner/activity description establishes. Both convert home/work interpretations into asserted reader biography; the required surrendered decision itself is not penalized, but the surrounding invented circumstances are.

Worst surviving defect in A: the unsolicited six-day omen. It adds an unrelated verification task and prediction rather than developing the promised argument. Its narrow win is not a clean contract pass.

## House 12 — A wins narrowly

Confidence: moderate. B is kinder locally; A explains the evidence problem better and has fewer distracting cross-house assertions.

Two exact quotations explain the preference:

- A: “these are consistent with a man withdrawing, and consistent with a man tired, and consistent with a man withdrawing who is also tired.” This concretely demonstrates why the same observations do not identify a cause.
- B: “The tie you are formally, visibly held in is not the thing coming loose.” This adds a consequential assurance about a formal bond, shifting attention away from the promised distinction and requiring facts unavailable in this excerpt review.

A's train comparison identifies both the analogy and its limit, then distinguishes confidence from information with unusual clarity. B also explains rereading well, and its acknowledgment that fear supplies explanations for understandable reasons is more respectful. But the letter comparison is less stable: the passage moves from existing unread writing to an account not yet composed. B then adds a long-standing informal attachment and a connection to the tenth house, increasing interpretive load without sharpening the core lesson.

Promise omissions: none in either candidate. Both settle present person leaving rather than returning person, give the stage between private conclusion and announcement, distinguish observed withdrawal from fear's causal story, and explain the failure of rereading ambiguous material.

Other contract concerns: A attributes specific fears and a half-watched return to the reader, then announces that the leaving and arriving figures are two different men. That identity distinction exceeds the supplied house-12 requirements. B asserts fatigue, the absence of events, an intact formal bond, and a long-standing unsworn attachment. These may or may not have support elsewhere; no such support was supplied within the authorized review materials. Both prescribe stopping interpretation rather than merely explaining its limits. A's “That architecture is yours, not his” is particularly liable to land as blame.

Worst surviving defect in A: its strong lesson about ambiguous evidence is delivered alongside invented accounts of the reader's fears and a categorical two-men conclusion. That weakens the restraint the lesson asks the reader to practice.

## Overall limits

Winners: 3 B; 5 A; 12 A. These are comparative preferences, not evidence that any full reading deserves 8/10. Across the excerpts, natural variation exists at the sentence level, but the recurring house/card mismatch, visual mechanism, distinction, cost, and closing command begin to feel formulaic.

Product-contract concern, kept separate from editorial scores: house 5 requires a timed twin-flame recognition and house 12 requires a categorical account of another person's inward departure. Those claims are not independently verified facts. I have not rewarded either candidate for weakening or avoiding them, and I have distinguished those required claims from additional invented certainty.

Because candidate identities are unknown, this review cannot determine whether the revised set meets the two-wins/no-regression test. Isolated excerpts also cannot settle whole-report coherence, the reliability of cross-house references, or the effect of the joiner.
