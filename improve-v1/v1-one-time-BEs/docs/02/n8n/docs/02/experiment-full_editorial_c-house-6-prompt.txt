DRAFT
There is a difference between a week that was hard and a week that shows. Anyone who has worked at anything knows the gap: the days that took the most out of you are not reliably the days that produced anything anyone can point to, and the days that produced something visible were sometimes the easy ones. Effort is felt from the inside. Output is read from the outside. Most of the time the two run close enough together that nobody has to choose between them.

Occasionally they come apart, and something formal arrives to settle which one counts. A review, a total, a set of figures, a decision that gets written down somewhere and carries a date. Not a conversation about how things have been going — an entry. That is the character of this house at present: the ordinary week is being read as a record rather than as an experience.

The figure in the card is looking straight out, not down at the scales in her hand. That is the whole of the matter. She is not consulting the weighing; she is not adjusting it while she watches; the pans hang level and her attention is elsewhere, on whoever has come before her. The scales do their work without supervision because the work was already done — accumulated, added up, arrived at. What she is doing is meeting the eye of the person the total belongs to. The sword stands upright, not pointed at anyone. It is available in either direction, which is exactly why the card is honest and not comforting.

The printed commentary is careful to separate this from the other kind of justice, the sort involved in election and dedication — that mysterious order in which some people conceive of being called to the highest things. This card sits outside that order. Its pillars open into the world where consequence is arithmetic. What was actually done gets weighed; what was meant by it is not admitted as evidence. Whether that is welcome depends entirely on what the arithmetic contains, and the card does not pretend to know in advance whose favour the total falls in.

The practical implication is narrower than it sounds. It is not that your working life is about to be dismantled, or that the body is issuing a verdict — this is a card of ledgers, not of alarms. It is that some part of the ordinary round is currently being counted rather than merely lived, and that the counting is happening whether or not anyone has mentioned it to you. If you can already name the thing that is being totalled, you are ahead. If you cannot, that is not negligence; it only means the record is being kept somewhere you are not standing.

What does not change is the routine itself. The week keeps its shape. The demands that were already in it stay in it, and no reorganization of your habits will alter a total that is mostly composed of things already entered. This card removes the illusion that a burst of visible effort now can rewrite what has accrued. It cannot, and the attempt is its own additional cost.

So the useful move is small and undoable: for one ordinary stretch of days, keep your own quiet note of what actually got finished, separate from what felt heavy. Two columns, private, no conclusions drawn. If they match, you have learned nothing and lost nothing. If they diverge, you have found the exact place where you are being weighed by one measure and paying yourself by another.
