# GRADE A — `07-D-tA-eight-swords.md`

**Card:** Eight of Swords, upright. **Seat graded:** the problem (free).
**Scan opened:** `assets/tarot-rws/eight-of-swords.png` — yes, at full size and in four crops
(head/torso, upper-right background, feet, horizon). Nothing below is graded from memory.

**Verdict: do not sign as written. Two corrections and I would.**

---

## What is actually on the card

Before the criteria, the record of what I looked at, because three of the eight turn on it.

- The **dress is red**. The **binding is not**. The strapping round her arms and torso, and the band
  over her eyes, are a flat pale grey-white — a different colour, a dead colour, laid over the red.
- They are **two separate bindings**. A band round the head; a separate wrapping round the arms and
  body, ending about the hip. No knot is drawn on either. Her legs and feet are free.
- **Eight swords, points driven into the ground**, pommels up. None pierce or touch her. They stand
  to her left, to her right and behind. **Nothing stands between her and the viewer.** The arc opens
  at the front.
- **Upper right: a castle** — red-roofed towers on a stepped rocky crag, seen through the swords. It
  is the only built thing in the frame. It is above her and it is on dry rock.
- **Background is not a sky.** It is the card's flat unpainted putty ground. Nothing is falling in it.
- **The ground is a flooded lowland.** Not one puddle: pale blue standing water at her feet, and
  more of it running in bands across the middle distance to left and right, under a low ridge line.
  Her feet are bare in it.

## 1. The two readings — **fail, on the letter's own test**

Signal, from the picture:

> "A cage touches you. Eight blades planted in wet mud with a gap left in them is a fence somebody
> put up." … "there's no hand on that cloth anywhere in the painting"

Position, the problem seat:

> "What's in your way is a rule you're still keeping that nobody is checking."

Those are not two statements. They are one statement in two registers. Set them side by side and the
substitution is one-for-one: *binding* → *rule*, *nobody holding it* → *nobody checking it*, *fence
somebody put up and left* → *rule you made or were handed, in a stretch that ended*. The signal is
already a diagnosis of what is wrong; by the time the seat is reached there is nothing left for it
to do but change the nouns.

The build notes propose the correct test and then fail it:

> "Cover the position name and the second one stops making sense."

Cover it. "A rule you're still keeping that nobody is checking" makes complete sense with no seat
label at all — *because a binding nobody is holding just is a rule you keep yourself.* The seat is
not carrying that sentence; the signal is.

There is one piece of genuine second work in the position beat — "you made it or it was handed to
you, in a stretch when keeping it was sensible. Then the stretch ended and the rule didn't." But
that comes out of the water beat, not out of the seat, and the water beat is the one I fault at 5.

**The two inventions.**

**#2, the red cloth.** The binding is grey. It is grey in the original and grey in the file on this
repo, and the letter does not merely mention the colour, it *leads* on it —

> "Red first. A red dress, and a long strip of red cloth wound round her body and over her eyes."

— which turns a slip into a claim. It also loses the reading it was standing on. See 3.

**#16, "It's rain that's finished."** Nothing on the card dates the water. See 5.

**On #14 and #15:** "nothing's falling in that sky" is sound — nothing is drawn falling. "Grey" is a
stretch of a flat unpainted neutral; harmless on its own, load-bearing only because beat 12 leans on
it.

**On #12:** true as worded — no hand is drawn on the cloth. But the letter asks *"Who wound the
cloth?"* and then rules the picture silent, when there is a castle in it. See 3.

## 3. The pictures — **fail**

**Described and not there:** a red binding. The cloth over her eyes and round her arms is pale grey,
in clear contrast against the red of the dress. This is the letter's central object and the first
thing it points at.

**Described wrongly:** "a long strip … wound round her body and over her eyes" — one strip. There are
two bindings, independent of each other. That matters more than it sounds: it means she is stopped in
two separate ways, and undoing one leaves the other. The single-strip version quietly promises that
one move frees her.

Also loose: "she's turned away from it" (she is frontal and blindfolded — she does not face the
castle, but she is not turned from it), and "some in front" (nothing stands between her and the
viewer).

**In the picture, better than what was used, missed:**

- **The colour the letter got backwards is the good news it went looking for.** She is red, head to
  foot, under the wrapping. The thing round her is the only colourless thing on her body. The card
  separates the woman from what is wound on her, by paint, and says the red did not go anywhere. That
  is a stronger and truer "you weren't daft" than anything in the water — and the letter had to
  overwrite it to make the sentence it wanted.
- **The castle.** Red roofs, on a crag, up and to the right, the only human construction in the
  frame, standing on dry rock while she stands in water. The letter puts the question *"Who wound
  the cloth?"* and answers *"they aren't in that picture."* The card's own answer is up the hill.
  A reader does not have to name who lives there to say: the authority in this scene is housed, dry
  and above you, and you have your back to it.
- **The front is open — completely.** The letter hedges to "a gap between them wide enough to walk
  through." There is no sword at all between her and the ground she is facing. The picture is more
  generous than the letter dared to be, and hedging it cost the strongest true thing on the card.
- **What is used well and is genuinely there:** the swords are in the earth and not on her; the cloth
  is wound and not knotted and ties her to nothing; the feet are bare and unbound. The P.S. is the
  best paragraph in the letter and every word of it is on the card.

## 4. The withhold — **honest, with one wobble**

What is held back is the action and the outcome: *which* rule goes first, what it costs the day
after, what changes in her week. That is genuinely not on one card — it needs her answer and two more
cards — and the letter says so in plain words rather than implying it:

> "that isn't on this card and I'm not going to invent it."

It then does the thing that separates a reader from a performer, and gives up a sale to do it:

> "Two cards won't lift that cloth off you. Nothing on this table has ever unwound a woman, and I'd
> be careful of a man whose cards claim they can."

and turns away the wider cut it could easily have sold:

> "if that's the letter you want, don't send me this one."

That is honest. Nothing is being fenced off that the card already answered.

**The wobble.** The P.S. closes:

> "The one part of her that does the walking is the part nobody bound. [The other two say where the
> first step goes]"

The card *does* say where the first step goes — out through the open side, which the letter itself
read in beat 8 ("it's on the side her feet are already pointed"). It reads the direction off the
card and then sells the direction back. It survives, narrowly, because "which rule, said to whom" is
a different question from "which way is open," and the letter is otherwise scrupulous about the line.
But it is the one place a reader can be accused of charging for something already given.

## 5. The loop — **fail. The good news is manufactured.**

Opened at beat 6, honestly:

> "I don't know yet what that's doing there. Keep hold of it for me."

Paid at beat 12, and this is where the letter breaks:

> "Standing water means it rained here, and it means the rain's stopped … Wet ground isn't rain. It's
> rain that's finished." … "Keep one line out of this letter and let it be this one."

The card does not carry the second half of that. It carries **no information about when it last
rained.** What it shows is a flooded lowland — water at her feet, and more of it lying in the middle
distance on both sides, under a low ridge, with the castle up on the only dry rock in the frame. That
is a mire. Mire is ground that *holds* water. It is the oldest thing said about this card's ground and
it says the opposite of what the letter needs.

The letter is not merely unsupported here, it contradicts itself six paragraphs apart:

> beat 6: "Shallow puddles on **ground that doesn't drain**"
> beat 12: "Wet ground isn't rain. **It's rain that's finished.**"

Ground that does not drain tells you nothing about the weather. If it does not drain, the wet is not
evidence the storm passed — it is evidence the wet stays. The letter names the exact property that
disqualifies its own conclusion, and then draws the conclusion anyway, and then instructs her to keep
that sentence above every other sentence in the letter.

And it is not a harmless comfort. For a large share of 76,000 women the weather has **not** passed —
he is still in the house, the money is still short — and this line tells her the thing making her
careful is a leftover. A reader who says that to the wrong woman has told her to drop her guard on the
strength of a puddle.

The picture had better news available and it was on her body: she is still entirely red under a grey
wrapping. That is a payable loop. The puddles are not.

## 6. The seat's own contract — **pass, the letter's best criterion**

The seat asks for one pointable thing, not her version, not a feeling. It delivers:

> "What's in your way is a rule you're still keeping that nobody is checking."
> "You could say it in one short sentence. We don't bring that up. I don't ask him where he's been.
> I don't spend on myself until everybody else is sorted."

A rule is an object. It is sayable in one line, checkable against her week, and it is not a feeling in
a coat. It is also decisively *not* the version she would have offered — the letter refuses that one
to its face:

> "Most of what arrives is a version of *how do I get out of this*. I'm not answering that one … Out
> isn't a place, so there's nowhere for a card to land."

That paragraph is the best piece of craft in the letter and it is correct.

**One caveat, not a fault:** what is named is a *class* with three worked examples, and she supplies
the instance. In a daily to a whole list that is the most a reader can do, and asking her to fill it
in is the right way to do it.

## 7. Dignity — **pass**

It reads her. It does not flatter and it does not accuse. The needle —

> "You're standing inside a fence you're still being polite about."

— is paid for immediately afterwards, and paid properly, from the card and not from sympathy:

> "You didn't get here by being weak. You got here by being right for a while, and nothing rings a
> bell when a thing stops being right."

That is a reader's sentence. No theatre, no therapy register, no blame.

**The one line to watch:** "It won't tell you why you're the sort of woman who keeps rules." That
converts her from a person into a type, and it is the only place in the letter that does.

## 8. Would I sign it — **no, as written. Yes with two corrections.**

Two things stop my name going on it, and both are one paragraph each:

1. **The binding is grey, not red.** She can open the card on her phone in under a minute and see
   that. Everything this letter's authority rests on is *"I looked at the picture harder than the
   people who send you keywords"* — it says so explicitly, "Then I looked at where the swords are."
   Getting the colour of the central object wrong is the one error that specific claim cannot
   survive. Fix it and the letter gains a reading, it does not lose one.
2. **Beat 12 asserts something the card does not say and the letter has already contradicted.** Cut
   "It's rain that's finished," or demote it from a claim about the card to an offer she can test
   against her own year. Then the loop can be paid from the red under the wrapping, which is real.

What I would keep untouched: the fence-not-cage correction, "wound, not knotted," the refusal of *how
do I get out of this*, the refusal to overclaim what two cards can do, and the whole P.S.

## The four plain answers

**Is this good tarot?**
It is better than most and it is not sound. Three things in it are the real article: the swords are in
the earth and not on her, so this is a fence and not a cage; the cloth is wound and not knotted and
ties her to nothing; her feet are bare and unbound while everything else on the card is wrapped or
driven in. Those are read off the picture, they are defensible in front of anyone, and they earn the
diagnosis. Against that: the letter reads the card once and re-labels it for the seat rather than
reading it twice, it colours the binding wrong in its own opening line and loses the best reading on
the card doing it, it walks past the castle after asking the question the castle answers, and it
manufactures its consolation out of standing water while having already said the ground does not
drain. A working reader would recognise the good half instantly and would not put their name near
beat 12.

**What would a real reader have seen that the letter missed?**
The colour. She is red — dress, sleeves, the whole length of her — and the thing wound round her arms
and eyes is a flat dead grey. Smith painted the woman and the binding in two different substances.
The reading that falls straight out of that is the one this letter spent four paragraphs hunting for
in the mud: *nothing that was done to you got into you; the colour is all still there, under the
wrapping, and the wrapping is the only part of this picture with no life in it.* Second, the castle:
red roofs on a crag, up and right, the only built thing in the frame, standing on dry rock while she
stands in water. The letter asks "Who wound the cloth?" and rules the picture silent. The picture
houses the answer on the hill and turns her back to it. Third, the front of the card: not "a gap
between the two nearest" but no sword at all between her and the ground her bare feet are pointed at.

**Does she trust him more or less after reading it?**
More, on balance — and the trust is built by the right things. Binning his own first paragraph in
front of her, refusing the question everybody sends, and saying flat out that two cards have never
unwound anybody, all buy real credit. He sounds like a man looking at a card rather than a man
performing. But the credit is lent against a claim she can check in sixty seconds.

The single line most likely to lose her: **"Red first. A red dress, and a long strip of red cloth
wound round her body and over her eyes."** She looks at the card — she is being invited to, the scan
is in the email — and the cloth is grey. Everything he says afterwards about looking closer than
other readers is spent in that moment. Runner-up, for a different reason: *"why you're the sort of
woman who keeps rules,"* which is the only line that files her under a type.

**Does she type a question into the box, and is it layable?**
Yes, and yes — this is the strongest part of the letter commercially and it is strong because it is
honest. The sentence handed to her:

> **"What do I do about the rule I'm still keeping that nobody's enforcing?"**
> "Then say what the rule is, in the words you'd use to a sister … It doesn't have to be fair to
> anybody in it, including you."

That is a layable question. It has a subject a card can sit on, and it maps cleanly onto the two seats
being sold — the action reads *which rule and what it costs*, the outcome reads *what is different
after*. It also earns the right to ask, because the letter first shows her why the question she was
going to send is unreadable ("Out isn't a place, so there's nowhere for a card to land"), which is
both true and the most persuasive thing in the letter. Three asks, and the last body line is the
question itself — "So, {{first_name}}. What's the rule?" — so she is left holding it.

**The one risk:** the pasted sentence reads complete on its own, so a share of the list will send it
bare, with no rule named, and that arrives as a shrug — the class without the instance. The fix is
not more words; it is making the paste-line visibly unfinished, so the box looks wrong until she has
filled it in.

## Where the picture errors came from — check this before fixing the letter

The four imagery faults at criterion 3 are **not this writer's inventions. They are inherited**, and
they will be in every letter built from the same source. `briefs/00-BRIEF-five-templates-one-card.md`
lines 15–18 state:

> "**A long red cloth** is wound round her body and arms, and a similar cloth is tied over her eyes."
> … "some in front, some behind." … "**The sky is grey.**" … "she is facing away from it."

All four of my picture faults are that paragraph, copied faithfully. So the letter is guilty of one
thing rather than four — **it did not look.** And the brief told it to, in its own line 12:

> "or any faithful Rider-Waite reproduction, **do, and correct me**. If you cannot, write only the
> details below and invent nothing."

The letter declined the correction and recorded the decision in its build notes:

> "Card details taken only from the brief — nothing invented, nothing claimed about the scan that the
> brief does not state."

That is honest bookkeeping and the wrong call. The scan is in this repo at
`assets/tarot-rws/eight-of-swords.png`; opening it is thirty seconds; the brief asked for it. The
letter went to press describing a grey binding as red because it graded its own accuracy against the
brief instead of against the card.

**Consequence for the run, not just this letter:** correct the brief first. Its "details most readers
walk past" list is otherwise good and is where the letter's three genuinely strong observations came
from — but the colour is wrong at source, the "grey sky" is wrong at source, and the list misses the
red/grey contrast and understates the open front. Fix lines 15–18 and add the contrast to the
walk-past list, or four more letters ship the same error.

**Not inherited, and this letter's own:** beat 12. The brief says only "wet, marshy ground with
shallow puddles" — *marshy*, which is the correct read and the opposite of what the letter concluded.
"It's rain that's finished" is the letter's addition, made against its own source as well as against
the card.
