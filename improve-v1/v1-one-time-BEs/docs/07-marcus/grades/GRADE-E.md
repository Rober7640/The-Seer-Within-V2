## What is actually on the scan

I am putting this first because two of my findings depend on it and the letter disagrees
with the card in front of it.

- She stands in **shallow water and wet mud**, both feet bare, puddles running across the
  whole foreground. Correct in the letter.
- Her gown is **red**. The cloth wound round her chest, upper arms and hips — the only thing
  on this card that is touching her — is **white/pale grey, unpainted**. It spirals down her
  body four or five turns and pins her arms to her sides.
- The blindfold is **the same white cloth**, tied high, across the eyes. **Her nose, mouth and
  chin are completely bare.** Confirmed at 6× — there is nothing near her mouth.
- Her head is **bowed**.
- **Eight swords**, upright, points in the ground: three to her left, five to her right. They
  stand at her sides and behind her in a loose arc. **Nothing at all stands in front of her.**
  The way forward, out of the bottom of the card, is completely unbarred.
- Behind her right shoulder, seen **between** the swords: high dry ground and a **castle with
  two red-roofed turrets**. Occupied, built, and on the rise — while she stands in a puddle.
  The letter never mentions it.

## 1. The two readings — PARTIAL PASS

Both statements exist and the letter is aware it owes me two.

**Signal:**

> "The detail nobody counts is the gap. Those eight swords don't close round her. There's a
> space between two of them wide enough to walk through, and it's been there the whole time
> you've been looking."

**Position:**

> "Look at what's covered on her. The cloth is over her eyes. It is not over her mouth. … That
> isn't a woman who can't speak — nothing in the picture goes near her mouth."

They are two different sentences, and the second does work the first does not. So this is not
the failure the brief warns about. But two things keep it off a clean pass.

First, the signal is not a signal. It is one picture-note standing in for a reading. "There is
a gap" tells me what is drawn; it does not tell me what this card *says* when it turns up
first. The Eight of Swords' signal is a pair, not a half: **she is bound and blind, and the way
out is nonetheless open.** The letter takes only the merciful half and never states the other,
which is convenient given where it is going.

Second, they lean on the same fact twice. The signal says the swords do not close round her.
The position says "not one of them touching her." That is the same observation in new clothes,
and it is doing load-bearing work in both places.

Notes on the ones that matter.

**#11 and #12 are the letter's engine, and the card does not turn it.** Eight swords stood in
the ground around a bound woman are the things that pen her in. To make them *her conversational
topics, which she is skilled at deploying* is not a reading of this card; it is a substitution.
It flips the swords from something done to her into something done by her, and nothing in the
picture licenses the flip. Every persuasive sentence in the problem seat rests on it.

**#10 is an argument from absence.** No tarot card draws a gag. Not this one, not the Two of
Swords, not the Star. You cannot take "there is no cloth on her mouth" as a finding, because
the same move proves she is not gagged in seventy-seven other pictures. The letter puts its
whole problem reading on a negative while the positives that *are* drawn — bound arms, covered
eyes, bowed head — all point the other way and are left out.

**#15 and #16 read cards that are still face down** — see criterion 4.

## 3. The pictures — FAIL on one line, and it is the worst possible line to fail on

> "A long red cloth is wound round her body and her arms, and another is tied over her eyes."

The binding cloth is **white**. The red is her **dress**. The letter's own preheader gets it
right — "A blindfolded woman in a red dress" — so the letter contradicts itself inside its own
send. And `[IMG-2 — the RWS scan]` sits **directly above** this sentence. She is looking at the
picture while he describes it wrong.

"Some in front of her and some behind" is loose. All eight are at her sides and behind her. The
truer statement was also the stronger one and it was free: *nothing stands between her and the
front of the card.*

**Missed, and it would have served the reading better than anything the letter used:** the
**castle with red-roofed turrets on the high dry ground behind her right shoulder**, visible
between the swords. She is in a puddle; the ground rises to somewhere built and occupied. That
is the card telling you the open way goes *somewhere*, not just that it is open. Also missed:
her **bowed head**, and — fatally for this letter's thesis — the fact that the cloth is the one
thing on the card actually touching her.

**On the detail the letter stakes everything on:** the blindfold is over the eyes and not the
mouth. That is **true**, clearly drawn, and I confirmed it at 6×. But it does not carry the
weight put on it, for the reason in #10 above. The one *drawn* thing that speaks to her
situation is the cloth over her **eyes**, and the letter names it in the description and then
never reads it — in the problem seat, where a blindfold is the most obvious problem on the
table.

## 4. The withhold — FAIL

The advertised withhold is honest and well drawn:

> "Two things I'd want before I said anything else, and one card holds neither. Which of the
> eight you set down first… And what your own week does in the days after you stop reaching
> for it."

Sequencing and consequence-over-time genuinely are not in one card in the problem seat. Fine.

But the letter then reads both face-down cards anyway.

> "Which of the eight you set down first, **because it isn't the biggest one.**"

He states a fact about the answer in the same breath as saying he has not got it. Either he can
read it, in which case he is withholding, or he cannot, in which case he should not assert it.

Worse, at the close:

> "What changes is smaller and it lasts. You stop running two conversations at once, and **by
> the end of the week that person costs you less to sit with.**"

The outcome seat is defined four lines earlier as "what's different in your own week once that
subject is back in the room." That sentence answers it, word for word, off no card at all. He
has given away the answer to a card he is charging her to turn — and given it away as a
certainty. That is the fault that costs a reputation rather than a refund, and it is doubly
awkward next to the good line that immediately precedes it ("Two cards won't make you raise
it").

## 5. The loop — PARTIAL

The good news is real and it is on the card: the gap. That is this card's genuine mercy and the
letter finds it honestly — "it's been there the whole time you've been looking" is a true and
well-said thing.

But the gap is stated once and then abandoned. The problem reading that follows ("what's in
your way is that it works") has nothing to do with it. The good news the letter actually closes
on is #16, which is manufactured. So the card offers a comfort, the letter picks it up, puts it
down, and pays the loop with something else.

Credit where due: "Nothing on this cloth has ever made a woman open her mouth, and I'd be wary
of a reader who says different" is the most honest line in the letter.

## 6. The seat's contract — PASS

One pointable thing, and it is a doing:

> "You've built a version of talking to that person that runs smooth with one subject out of
> it, and that's why nothing has moved."

That is not a feeling in a coat. The letter guards the boundary explicitly and correctly —
"Nobody can put a card on how you feel about a thing — try it and you get a horoscope… A thing
you do can go on the cloth." That is craft, and it is right.

The caveat: the pointable thing arrives via the invented swords-as-subjects reading (#11). The
seat is filled. It is filled by the invention, not by the card.

## 7. Dignity — PASS, with a caution

It reads her. It names the lazy read and refuses it — "Just say it, love. You're overthinking"
— and then:

> "That's a woman reporting her own competence at the moment she's meant to be confessing a
> weakness. You weren't making a fuss. You were being accurate."

That is the best paragraph in the letter and it is worth more than everything the card does.
No accusation anywhere.

The caution is flattery. This letter converts a picture of a bound, blindfolded woman with her
head down into a compliment about her skill, and the subject line — "nobody gets good at that
by accident" — sells the compliment. Dignity is real when the reader tells her the hard half
too. The hard half was dropped.

## 8. Would I sign it? — NO, not as it stands

Two things stop me, and both are a morning's work:

1. **The red cloth.** Any of the 76,000 can look at the scan in the email above the sentence.
   A reader who describes a picture wrongly while it is on the screen has no authority left.
2. **The card ratifies instead of overruling** — see below.

Fix the cloth, and read the blindfold honestly in the problem seat, and I would sign it. The
listening in this letter is genuinely good.

## The four plain answers

### Is this good tarot?

No. It is good **listening** wearing tarot's coat. Every load-bearing idea arrives before the
deck is cut — "the word I keep coming back to is **good**", "nobody gets good at something by
accident", "she isn't frozen, she's practised", "that line is a job, not a feeling" — all of it
is settled by paragraph seven, off her sentence alone. The card turns up at line 29 and is
handed a script. It says "she isn't frozen" again in different words, and to manage even that
it has to be told the eight swords are her conversation topics, which they are not. A card that
genuinely overrules a client's filing forces the reader somewhere he did not plan to go.
Nothing in this letter surprises Marcus. And the card, read straight, does not agree with him:
it shows a woman who cannot see, held by a cloth, head down, with the way out open behind her.
The letter had to leave out the binding and the blindfold to keep its conclusion, and it did.

**Does the card overrule her, or decorate?** It decorates. The letter announces the overrule —
"So the card won't have your sentence the way you've filed it" — but the sentence had already
been overruled twenty lines earlier, without a card, and what the card supplies is agreement.

### What would a real reader have seen that the letter missed?

**The castle.** Behind her right shoulder, two red roofs, seen between the swords, standing on
dry high ground while she is ankle-deep in a puddle. It is the second half of the gap and the
better half: the way out is not only open, it goes somewhere built and lived in. The letter
tells her the fence has a hole and stops. A reader says: *the hole faces somewhere. There is a
place on this card with a roof on it, it is behind you, and you cannot see it because of what
is over your eyes.*

**And the cloth.** White, wound four turns round her chest and arms, the only thing in the
picture in contact with her. A reader reads the thing that is touching her before he reads the
things that are not. Read honestly in the problem seat it says the harder, truer sentence this
letter would not write: *you have got very good at this, and the card's answer is that you
cannot see what you are standing in.* That is not an insult. It is what the card says, it is
more interesting than the compliment, and it would have sold the two face-down cards far
better — because a woman who has just been told she cannot see her own situation wants to know
what to do about it.

### Does she trust him more or less?

More, on balance — "you weren't making a fuss, you were being accurate" buys a great deal of
credit, and refusing the "should I bring it up" question is a real service that costs him
something.

**The single line most likely to lose her:**

> "A long red cloth is wound round her body and her arms"

Because `[IMG-2]` is immediately above it. She is looking at a white cloth and a red dress
while he tells her otherwise. It is one word, and it undoes the whole "nobody read your
sentence as carefully as I just did" claim — he did not read the card as carefully as she can.

### Does she type a question into the box, and is it readable?

**Yes.** This is the strongest business element in the letter and I would keep it whole.

The handed sentence:

> **"What do I do about the thing I keep talking round?"**

That is readable. It names a doing, not a permission and not a mood, and an action card and an
outcome card can both sit on it. The letter earns it properly by refusing the bad question
first — "The letter I get most is some version of *should I bring it up*. That one I hand
straight back. It asks whether you're allowed, and no card on this table gives permission" —
which is correct, and is the sort of thing only somebody who actually lays cards knows.

The closing prompt is better still: **"What's the subject you got good at instead?"** That asks
for a concrete noun — her mother, the money, the drinking, the house. A noun you can lay a card
on.

One caution: "Send me the flat version and I'll do to it what I just did to hers" will bring in
a share of boxes reading *i keep doing this* with no object in them. Readable-ish, but thinner
than what the question form would have got. Worth the trade; worth knowing it is a trade.
