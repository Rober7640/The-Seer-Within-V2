# Grade — `07-D-tC-eight-swords.md`

**Card in the free seat:** Eight of Swords, upright. **Second face turned up and then discarded:** Eight of Wands, upright.
**Scans opened before grading:** `assets/tarot-rws/eight-of-swords.png`, `assets/tarot-rws/eight-of-wands.png` — both viewed whole and in crops (the figure, the sword line, the ground at her feet, the top right of the swords card; the wand ends at both edges and the whole of the landscape).
**Grading only the tarot.** Prose, beats and marketing are somebody else's job.

---

## 1. The two readings — PASS, but the second one is borrowed from another card

The signal:

> "The first card says nothing can move."

and, in the ruling:

> "That one's you. Not because you're trapped. Because you're the only person in it."

The position:

> "Same card, first seat, and the seat asks something else of it: what's actually in the way, as one thing you could put a finger on. … What's in the way is the one route you keep going back to."

These are two different statements, not one statement said twice, and that is more than most letters manage. The signal is a state — she is stopped, and she is the only person here. The position names an object. Good.

The trouble is the object. The Eight of Swords does not show a woman trying anything. She is standing still. Nothing on this card repeats, returns, or is attempted again — that is the Nine of Wands' business, or the Two of Pentacles', not hers. "There's a door you've tried, and you tried it again since, a bit more politely" is a reading of a card that did not fall.

It is rescuable in one sentence the letter never writes. She is blindfolded. A blindfolded woman can only go back to what she can find by touch — and what she can find by touch is the thing she already knows. Tie the door to the cloth over her eyes and the position read becomes carried by the picture instead of hovering over it. As it stands the letter asserts the blindfold in one section and the door in another and never joins them.

**Verdict:** two genuine readings. The signal is defensible and thin — most of the space where the signal read should live is spent on the adjudication instead. The position read is thin, not invented, and one sentence away from sound.

## 2. The claim ledger

**Sound — 15.**

**Thin — 4.**

- **"no woman and no ground worth the name"** / "an empty field". There is a great deal of ground. A green hill with a small building on top of it, hedged fields, scattered trees, a river across the bottom right and a fenced structure on the bank. What is absent is a *person in view*, not habitation. The letter flattens the landscape to make the ruling look obvious, and it throws away the most useful thing on the card while doing it (below).
- **"Here's the bit nobody says out loud about that second picture."** Every reader says it out loud. The descent is the first thing anyone is taught about the Eight of Wands. Not a tarot error — a salesman's flourish laid over an orthodox read.
- **"What's in the way is the one route you keep going back to"** — see criterion 1.
- **"Most mornings they agree, so I only tell you about one."** Two cards off a cut of a 78-card deck do not agree most mornings. This is a claim about his own table, and it is not credible.

**Invented — 3.**

- **"it lands this week whether anybody decides a thing."** The Eight of Wands says *soon* and says *already in the air*. It does not say *this week*. He put a date on it. That is the one claim in the letter she can be disappointed by on a fixed day.
- **"that's my rule and I've not had a morning where it didn't hold."** See the adjudication section. The rule cannot hold, and his own test cannot apply, to two figureless cards or two peopled ones.
- **"The door, tried again on Sunday."** Nothing on this card, or the other one, dates anything she did. This is a cold-reading specific with no card under it, and it is checkable in the two seconds it takes her to remember her own Sunday.

## 3. The pictures, with the scans open

The Eight of Swords description is honest work. Everything it says is on the card, including two details most letters get wrong — that the swords do not close, and that the cloth is wound rather than tied. Nothing is described that is not there. That is a better record than most.

Three things are on that card that would have served this seat better than the door, and all three were missed:

1. **The castle.** Top right, behind the swords, on a rocky crag: a grey keep with red roofs. Nobody is in it and nobody has come down from it. For a seat whose job is *"what is actually in the way, named as one thing she could point at"*, this is the strongest thing on the card — what bound her was decided somewhere uphill, behind her, by people who are no longer paying attention. It is pointable, it is drawn, and it needs no invented Sunday.
2. **Her feet.** Bare. On wet mud. Nothing on them, nothing round them, and the cloth stops at her hips. The letter says "weight on both feet" and stops. The good news the letter goes hunting for at the end is already at the bottom of the card: her legs were never tied.
3. **The ground the swords are in.** Soft wet mud with water running through it. Those swords are not set in rock. A fence stood up in mud is not a fence — that is a harder and better version of "it describes a room with one door in it" than the gap alone.

On the wands card, the missed detail is the one the letter wrote over: the river and the fenced bank at the bottom right, and the house on the hill. The wands are not coming down on nothing. They are descending across water toward a place where somebody lives. If the wands are "the room", that is the room named precisely — and the letter settled for "the weather".

## 4. THE ADJUDICATION — the letter's own claim, graded hard

**Is adjudicating rather than blending real practice?** Half. The distinction underneath it is old and sound: some cards read the querent, some read the conditions around her. Readers have used a significator against an environment card for as long as there have been spreads. But that distinction is normally made by the *seat* — the position tells the card what job it has — not by a contest between two cards to decide which one gets to be her.

And these two cards are not in disagreement. Read the letter's own sentence:

> "The first card says nothing can move. The second says everything already is, and it lands this week whether anybody decides a thing."

Those are not contradictory claims about one subject. They are claims about two subjects, which the letter concedes itself two paragraphs later. The conflict is manufactured so that it can be settled. Worse for the letter: the plain combined read of these two cards takes one sentence and lands exactly where the adjudication lands after two hundred and fifty words — *things are already moving fast, and she believes she cannot move, so the stopping is in her head and not in the situation.* That is the orthodox read of Eight of Swords with Eight of Wands. The letter takes the scenic route to it and charges the reader the tension on the way.

The stated rule is also false as stated:

> "When two faces disagree, one of them is you and one is the room you're standing in — that's my rule and I've not had a morning where it didn't hold."

Cut to the Eight of Wands and the Three of Swords and he has two rooms and no woman. Cut to the Queen of Cups and the Knight of Swords and he has two people and no room. His own test — *go and look for a person* — cannot resolve either. A rule that fails on any morning with two figureless cards has failed on many mornings.

**Is the ruling defensible for the two cards that actually fell?** Yes, and I would not rule the other way. The Eight of Swords is a person, bound and blindfolded, in a restraint she can walk out of — the card is her state of mind by construction. The Eight of Wands has no agent in it at all; it is the only Minor you can say that of, and what it depicts is movement that is nobody-in-frame's doing. If you are going to force a choice, no competent reader picks the wands as the portrait. Credit where it is due: the argument he gives — *find the person* — is the correct argument, and he gives it plainly.

What he then does with the winner is where it goes wrong. Ruling the wands "the room" does not make the room decorative. Eight of Wands as conditions does not mean *noise*; it means the thing is resolving itself at speed, elsewhere, without her. The honest problem-seat read that follows is not "the door you keep going back to" — it is *you are still negotiating with something that has already been decided somewhere else*. He gets within a foot of it —

> "You stop reading a shut door as news about your life"

— and then sells the rest.

**Is reading a card and then discarding it honest?** No. The letter convicts itself twice.

> "The other goes back in the pack. The weather's real, and it isn't a reading."

Then, at the bottom of the same letter:

> "**P.S.** One more true thing about the wands, now I've ruled against them."

A card that is back in the pack does not yield one more true thing. It never went back in the pack. It stayed on the table for the whole letter, doing the work of urgency, and it is still working in the last line she reads.

And it is the discarded card that creates the thing he is selling:

> "I've ruled against the wands. I never said they were lying. They're on the way down, and what I can't get off this one card is what you do while they land."

Read that slowly. He turned up two cards. He used the second one to establish that something is arriving and arriving soon. He then removed it from his own hand on a rule he made up. He then said he cannot answer the question the removed card raised, because he only has one card. Then he charged for the answer. The withhold does not come from the limits of the cut. It comes from a card being taken out of the cut *after* it had done its selling.

The file's own preamble says why, and it is not a reader's reason:

> "the second card is named, painted, ruled against — and then goes back in the pack instead of into a position, because a letter cannot name a seat the registry lacks."

That is the fault named exactly. The card was discarded for a filing reason and the letter tells her it was discarded for a reading reason — *"that's my rule and I've not had a morning where it didn't hold."* A technical constraint has been dressed up as a twenty-year practice. That is the sentence I would not sign.

The honest versions are both available and both cheap. Either the wands are not in the read — then do not paint them, do not rule on them, do not take a P.S. off them and do not let them set the clock. Or they are in the read — then say in one line what two cards say together, and sell the *seats*, which is what the seats are for. This letter takes the benefit of both and the responsibility of neither.

## 5. The withhold — fails, and it fails on the discard

What he withholds is named properly, not as an unlabelled "more":

> "the move that's yours this week, and what starting it takes out of you. Nor what your week looks like after."

With one card in a problem seat, that is a fair withhold. A single Eight of Swords genuinely does not name her specific move, and the letter does not hold back the card's own obvious advice — it gives that away free in the re-read.

But he did not have one card. He had two on the cut, and with those two the action is not a mystery: stop working the door, because the thing is already in the air and is landing somewhere you are not standing. He read that, out loud, and then unread it. **The withhold is honest about the seat and dishonest about the hand.**

## 6. The loop — pays, and pays from the picture

> "Look at how those swords stand. They don't make a ring. There's a gap in them wide enough to walk out of, and it's been there all along. The cloth is wound round her, not knotted to anything. Eyes covered, head down, and she's facing the open side."

Every clause of that is on the card. The promised better news is real, drawn, and checkable by anyone who looks. The aphorism —

> "**You don't have to see the gap to walk through it.**"

— is carried by the blindfold, the gap and her free legs together. This is the best-founded passage in the letter and it is the part a working reader would recognise as reading rather than talking. It would have been better still with the bare feet in it.

---

## 7. The seat's contract — delivered

The seat asks for one thing she could point at, not a feeling. The letter refuses the feeling out loud —

> "Your version is that you can't move until you know more."

— and hands her an object instead:

> "That's the thing to point at this morning. Not your situation. The door, tried again on Sunday."

That is pointable. She can name it in four words. The contract is met. The object just is not on the card, and the date on the end of it is a fabrication.

---

## 8. Dignity — mostly reads her

He reads her rather than flattering her, and the shame line is properly done:

> "Going back to the same door isn't stupidity — it's what's always worked before, and everybody who loves you would tell you to keep asking properly."

Two lines lean the wrong way. "The door, tried again on Sunday" tells her a fact about her own week that he cannot have. And the closing question —

> "Which of those two pictures have you been treating as your life?"

— makes her wrong about her own life on the authority of a rule he invented four hundred words earlier. It is a small accusation wearing a question mark.

---

## 9. Would I sign it? No — but the corrections are one morning's work

I would not put my name on three things in front of 76,000 women who can look both cards up before their tea is cool.

- **"it lands this week"** — a date the card does not carry.
- **"The door, tried again on Sunday."** — a fact about her life invented for effect.
- **"The other goes back in the pack"** followed by a P.S. that reads it again — a card used for its urgency, disowned for its responsibility, and still selling on the way out.

Take those three out, tie the door to the blindfold, and put the castle and her bare feet in, and I would sign the rest the same morning. The picture work on the swords is genuinely good and the ruling is correct.

## The four answers

**Is this good tarot?**
It is good reading with a false mechanism bolted to the front of it. The Eight of Swords is described accurately, read twice for two different jobs, and its good news is taken off the actual picture rather than manufactured — that is more than most letters do and it is the part I would defend. But the adjudication that gives the letter its whole claim is theatre. The two cards were never in conflict; the conflict was built so it could be settled; the settling arrives at the reading any competent person would have given in one sentence without it; and the losing card was then removed from the hand on an invented rule so that the answer it was already giving could be sold back. Under that sits one invented date and one invented fact about her week. It is a reader's eye in a performer's staging.

**What would a real reader have seen that the letter missed?**
The castle on the crag behind her, top right — a keep with red roofs, uphill, nobody in it, nobody coming down. That is what is actually in the way and it is drawn, so the problem seat could have been filled without inventing a door or a Sunday: *what is holding you was settled somewhere behind you by people who have stopped thinking about it.* Then her bare feet on the wet mud, and the mud itself — nothing is on her feet, nothing is round her legs, and the swords that look like a fence are stuck in soft ground with water running through it. The letter went looking for good news and walked past it twice on the way to the gap.

**Does she trust him more or less?**
More for eight hundred words, then less at the end. The single line most likely to lose her is **"The door, tried again on Sunday."** Everything else in the letter is a claim about a picture she can check and agree with, or a claim about her feelings she can accept or ignore. This one is a claim about her diary. The woman whose Sunday was quiet reaches the closing question knowing he was guessing, and then re-reads "I've not had a morning where it didn't hold" in that light.

**Does she type a question into the box — and is it layable?**

> "**What do I do about the one thing I keep going back to?**"
> "Then say which thing. The person, the office, the letter you keep re-sending."

Yes, and yes. The instruction to name the thing is what saves it: it turns a shrug into a subject, and a named subject plus "what do I do" is exactly what an Action seat and an Outcome seat can be laid on. It is the strongest piece of commercial work in the letter and the file's own preamble is too hard on it.

One cost, though, and it comes straight from the invented door. The question only fits the woman who *is* going back to something. Give the same card to a woman who has simply stopped — which is what the Eight of Swords actually depicts — and the box asks her about a habit she does not have, and she closes the letter with nothing to type.
