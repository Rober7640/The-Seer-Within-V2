# VOC 02 — What working readers say they get asked

**Territory:** professional tarot readers, psychics and mediums writing publicly about the
questions clients actually bring them — blogs, FAQ pages, codes of ethics, reader-facing
advice, and first-person accounts from phone-line readers.

**Collected:** 2026-09-05. Web research only (WebSearch + WebFetch). No customer database
was touched — that is VOC 01's job.

**Why this file exists:** Marcus Stone's daily emails read as concepts. This file is the
opposite of concept. It is the actual sentence a woman says out loud when she sits down.

---

## How to read the evidence here

Everything below is **one reader's report of what they hear**. It is testimony, not data.

| Grade | Means |
|---|---|
| **A — reported querent speech** | The reader presents this as words a client said to them. Closest thing to her voice. |
| **B — reader's illustrative example** | The reader writes it as "people ask things like…". Probably a composite of many clients, not a transcript. Still their ear, still useful. |
| **C — SEO / keyword phrasing** | Appears on a content page built to rank for the search term. Tells you what she *types into Google*, not what she *says to a person*. Weakest. |

⛔ **Never add the frequency claims up.** Six readers each saying "the most common question
I get is X" is six anecdotes, not a survey. There is no census of tarot questions.

⚠️ All quotes were pulled by an automated read of each page. Before any of these goes into
live copy word-for-word, open the source and eyeball the sentence.

## 1.4 The full-sentence version — how she actually talks

⭐ This is the highest-value single find for the "her actual Tuesday" brief. California
Psychics' reader-authored guide reproduces what a client message actually looks like when
she is allowed to write it in her own words, rather than compressed into a four-word
question:

> **"Lately, the man that I have been dating seems like he is pulling away from me. When I
> ask him if anything is wrong, he says everything is fine, but I know something is
> different. Is he lying to me, and can you tell me if he is seeing someone else?"**

Grade B — presented by the reader as a model of a good client question, so it is her ear
rather than a transcript. But look at the shape:

1. A stretch of time, not an event — *"lately"*
2. A behaviour, not a feeling — *"seems like he is pulling away"*
3. **She already asked him and he already lied** — *"he says everything is fine"*
4. Her own knowing, which she does not trust — *"but I know something is different"*
5. Only then, the question.

Marcus's emails currently start at step 5. Everything that makes a woman feel *known* is in
steps 1–4.

Two more from the same page, same structure:

> **"My parents have been insisting that I become a doctor because we come from a family of
> doctors. But that doesn't seem right. I've been thinking about dropping out of school.
> Can you tell me if this is the right path?"**

> **"I don't know what direction to go in my life. I believe I have talent, but I don't
> know how to express it. Can you please help?"**

# PART 3 — Questions readers refuse, and exactly why

Useful twice: it maps the compliance edge, **and** a refusal in Marcus's own voice is one of
the strongest moves available to the offer. A reader who says "I won't answer that, and here
is what I will answer instead" is more credible than one who answers everything.

|---|---|---|

## 3.5 Third party — the biggest one

This is the refusal readers care most about, and the one that matters most to Marcus,
because **most of her questions are third-party questions.** "Does he love me" *is* a
third-party question.

| Refused by | Their exact wording |
|---|---|
| Joy Vernon | "I will only perform readings about you, not about others… If you ask about another person I will rephrase the question; if during the course of the reading I discover you have asked about someone else, I will throw a new spread that focuses on your role in the situation." Reason: "questions about another person tend to cause confused, convoluted, unhelpful and inaccurate readings." |
| Maenad Tarot | "I will not do readings about a third party, including readings about someone else's thoughts, feelings, or actions." Reason: "This is unethical, and more likely to represent the seeker's own hopes and fears anyway." |
| Brigit Esselmont | privacy violation, plus the reading only shows "the situation from your/the client's perspective and not necessarily the situation at hand" |
| Gord Hall | "They cross an ethical line" |
| Christiana Gaudet, on cheating specifically | "Never do I want to say for sure that the deed has been done. It's not my job to bust someone, and it is not my job to break up marriages." |

⭐ **The reframe move, which readers do constantly and Marcus should steal:** they do not
say no and stop. They swap the question. Joy Vernon "throws a new spread that focuses on
your role in the situation." Lisa at Angelorum swaps "Is he cheating?" for **"What do I need
to know about this situation and how to best move forward from it?"** Brigit swaps "Will my
lover leave his wife?" for **"What can I do to improve on my relationship with so-and-so?"**

That is: *I won't answer the question you asked. Here is the better one, and I'll answer
that for free.* It refuses and gives at the same time.

## 3.6 "Will I win"

Universally refused. Brigit Esselmont, Tucumcari Tarot and Gord Hall all list
"Will I win the lottery?". Sample codes of ethics say it flatly: **"I do not predict the
Lotto Numbers."**

## 3.7 Curses and hexes

The teachmetarot sample code of ethics: **"I Do Not Invoke or Remove Spells or Curses."**
Worth noting because a caller in the HuffPost phone-line account rang specifically for this
— "This lady, she put a curse on me, and I gotta get rid of it." It is a live demand and a
refusal Marcus could make loudly.

## 3.8 The formal industry line

- **TABI (Tarot Association of the British Isles):** readers will suggest the client consult
  a qualified professional, such as a health or legal professional, where appropriate.
- **American Tarot Association:** members must recommend clients consult a licensed
  professional for legal, financial, medical or psychological advice the reader is not
  qualified to give.
- **Sample reader's code (teachmetarot):** "I do not give Specific Financial, Legal, Medical
  or any other Advice that I am not Professionally Qualified to do so." Plus an age clause:
  "I do not give Readings to Persons under the age of 18 without the consent of their Parent
  or Guardian."
- **Joy Vernon:** "This reading is not meant to take the place of professional medical,
  legal, or financial advice."

# PART 4 — The gap: what she asks vs what she wants

This is the section Marcus's emails are actually built on. Ordered by how sharp the
observation is.

### 4.1 She is not asking for a prediction. She is asking for permission.

> **"You are not looking for a prediction. You are looking for permission."**
> — pseudonymous reader, Medium (single source, unverified — but the line is the cleanest
> statement of the idea found anywhere)

Same piece:

> **"Some came in with pages of prepared questions, organized by category, highlighted in
> three colors, and every single time, the real question was not on the list. The real
> question was underneath it."**

> The one question underneath all of them: **"Am I too much, or not enough?"**

### 4.2 She wants the answer that lets her do the thing she has already decided to do.

The sharpest and best-sourced observation in the file, from a 20-year full-time professional:

> **"Often the client wants to hear that their spouse is cheating to justify ending the
> relationship."**
> — Christiana Gaudet

She is not asking *is he cheating*. She is asking *am I allowed to leave*. The cheating
would be the permission slip.

Gord Hall says the same thing from the other direction: clients often seek **"confirmation
of a predetermined outcome"** rather than guidance.

### 4.3 She asks again because she does not want the answer.

> **"Often when folks are casting too many readings on the same topic it is because they
> don't really want to know the answer - because deep down inside they already know it."**
> — Alexis J. Cunningfolk, Worts & Cunning

> **"Casting too many readings at a time often comes from a place of uncertainty and through
> tarot we're trying to find something certain to hold onto."**
> — same

And the behavioural proof, from two readers watching it happen:

- Brigit Esselmont's client: same question, three times in five days. What changed?
  **"Uh, he texted me."**
- Gord Hall on his TikTok lives: **"the same people return week after week, asking about the
  same ex."**

| Closed questions **"put all the power outside of you"** | Paul O'Mara |
| **"they didn't want to know their futures as much as they wanted hope"** | former phone psychic, Vice |

# PART 5 — ⚠️ SENSITIVE MATERIAL — handle deliberately

Everything in this block touches health, pregnancy, death, money desperation or self-harm.
**None of it goes into an email without an explicit decision.** It is here because the
refusal-in-Marcus's-voice move needs to know exactly where the edge is.

### Health, pregnancy, death
Covered in §3.1–3.3. The verbatim client questions in that section include a named relative's
cancer and "When will I die?". These are **real questions readers report being asked.**
Marcus can *refuse* them by name. He must never *answer* them.

### Money desperation
- Phone-line operator: callers were **"so broke that gambling seemed like their only way out
  of poverty"**, and lotto calls were the single largest call category.
- Vice: a caller repeatedly asked about selling **"priceless paintings"** to cover bills, and
  was spending an inheritance on the calls themselves.
- Vocal: a caller wanted permission to quit her job for music after buying a car she could
  not afford.

⛔ This is the ugliest finding in the sweep and the clearest compliance line: **the readers
who take the most money take it from the people who have the least.** Marcus's offer must
not be built on a lotto-caller mechanic. Money emails should be about the ledger she can
actually see, not a windfall.

### Self-harm and abuse
- HuffPost: the phone line had standing protocols — **"Suicidal people were directed to
  hotlines"**, **"Abused women were directed to women's shelters"**.
- Vice: the writer encouraged callers into therapy and says HR discouraged it.
- Vocal: a caller wanted a missing adult son located. The reader declined.

**Implication for the programme:** a daily email that opens a spread and invites a question
will, at some volume, receive one of these. The fulfilment pipeline needs a documented
handling path before scale, not after. This is a build item, not a copy item.

### Bereavement
Mediumship territory, adjacent to ours. Reported drivers: wanting to know a loved one is at
peace, needing to hear something left unsaid, wanting validation that signs are real. See
the existing standing decision in memory (`fb-ad-bereavement-crosscut`) — bereavement is a
deliberate inclusion decision, never a default.

# PART 6 — How good is this evidence, really

**What is solid**
- Six independent named professionals converge on the return question ("will they come back
  / will s/he come back / will I get back with my ex") as their most-heard. Different
  countries, different formats, no shared source. That convergence is real signal.
- The refusal list is very solid. Health / pregnancy / death / legal / third-party / lottery
  recur across individual readers **and** the two industry bodies (TABI, ATA) **and**
  published sample codes of ethics. This is a settled professional norm, not opinion.

**What is thin**
- **Almost everything is grade B.** Readers write "people ask things like X", not "on
  Tuesday she said X". True transcript-grade quotes are rare. The grade-A set is small:
  Biddy Tarot's difficult-client lines, and one Medium café scene.
- **The Biddy / Tucumcari overlap.** Their two lists of forbidden questions are near
  identical in both content and order ("Will my lover leave his wife?", "Will I win the
  lottery?", "Does my ex hate me?", "When will I die?", "Am I pregnant?", cancer remission,
  "Should I take the job?", vague-question, "When will I get married?", court case). One
  almost certainly derives from the other. **Count them as one source, not two.**
- **No dataset exists publicly.** The Katz & Goodwin 80,000-question survey is the only real
  quantitative work located, and it lives inside *Tarot Face to Face* rather than online.
- **The Medium "200 people" piece** produced the best-phrased insights in the file but is
  pseudonymous, recent (Mar 2026), and reads as though it may be AI-assisted. Its ideas are
  corroborated elsewhere; its specific sentences should be treated as inspiration, not as
  quotable testimony.
- **The ghosting cluster** ("why hasn't he replied", "why did he ghost me", "he stopped
  texting me") appears everywhere in search but almost entirely on SEO content pages built
  to rank for the phrase. Grade C. It tells us what she *types*, which is still worth
  knowing — but no reader in this sweep reports hearing it out loud.

**What I could not get**
- `tarotforum.net` — the thread "What are the most popular questions that clients ask the
  tarot reader and experiences" is the single most promising unread source in the territory
  (working readers, in their own words, at length). Blocked by bot protection through both
  direct fetch and a text proxy. **Worth a manual browser visit.**
- The Reluctant Tarot Reader substack has posts titled "The question behind all of the
  questions from clients" and "The question that tortures many clients" that surface in
  search but whose URLs 404 on fetch. Also worth a manual look.
- Reddit r/tarot professional-reader threads did not surface usefully through search.
