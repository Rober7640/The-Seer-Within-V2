# %FIRSTNAME%, how do you let love into a life you've worked so hard to protect?

*There's a small practice I want to share with you this morning. It begins with noticing what you do when kindness reaches you.*

%FIRSTNAME% —

Evelyn here.

Have you ever wanted someone to get closer, then found yourself pulling back the moment they did?

You've waited for the message. When it arrives, you read it twice, looking for what might be wrong.

Someone says something kind, and you make a joke before you let yourself believe them.

You think about meeting someone new, then remember how much of yourself you gave the last time.

If any of that feels familiar, dear, stay with me a moment.

Because wanting love and allowing yourself to receive it can ask very different things of you.

You can be quite certain you want someone beside you. Someone who remembers what matters to you. Who calls when they say they will. Who notices you've gone quiet and cares enough to ask why.

Then an opportunity for closeness comes, and you hear yourself saying you're busy.

You might even be relieved when they stop asking.

Until later, when the house is quiet and you wish they hadn't.

### First, notice what you still want

Perhaps it's been a while since you've let yourself say it plainly.

You'd like to be held. You'd like affection that you don't have to keep earning. You'd like to tell someone about an ordinary day and feel that they're glad you called.

There can be embarrassment around admitting that, especially when you've spent years being the capable one.

The woman who manages. Who gets on with things. Who tells everyone she's fine because explaining the rest would take too long.

But you can manage your own life and still want someone to share it with, dear.

Let yourself finish this sentence before you read any further:

**“What I miss most about being close to someone is…”**

Whatever came to mind, give it a moment. You don't have to make it sound sensible to anyone else.

### Then notice what happens when it comes close

This part asks for a little more honesty.

Think of the last time someone offered you kindness.

A compliment. Help with something. An invitation. A few minutes of their attention when they could see you needed it.

What did you do?

Did you accept it?

Or did you explain why the compliment wasn't deserved, insist you could manage, or immediately begin thinking of how to repay them?

It's such a small thing. Easy to overlook.

But if you've become used to carrying everything yourself, receiving can feel strangely uncomfortable. You may catch yourself looking for the obligation hidden inside an ordinary kindness.

And if affection once came with conditions, you may be especially careful about accepting it again.

You have reasons for being careful, dear. Keep your judgment. Take your time with people.

Alongside that care, leave yourself room to notice when someone is being good to you.

### The habit can come with you

This is the part I most want you to sit with, %FIRSTNAME%.

A different person can arrive, and you can still find yourself preparing for the same disappointment.

You hear a kind word and wait for the catch. You enjoy an evening and spend the next morning looking for signs that you enjoyed it more than they did.

It can become exhausting to want closeness while watching so carefully for the moment it will hurt.

And the habit doesn't always announce itself. Sometimes it sounds perfectly reasonable.

“I'll reply tomorrow.”

“They were only being polite.”

“There's no point getting my hopes up.”

If you recognise your own voice in those words, the next question is a practical one:

**How do you make a little room for affection while keeping the boundaries you've learned you need?**

### Begin with one kindness you can receive

In the spiritual language of this practice, that's what I mean by opening the heart.

The heart chakra gives us a place to focus our attention on love, compassion, and our willingness to receive. You can bring that intention into something very ordinary today.

The next time someone says something kind, let your first answer be “thank you.”

Give yourself a moment before you explain it away.

If a friend offers help you would welcome, let yourself accept it. If someone makes time for you, allow yourself to enjoy that time without immediately calculating what you owe.

Choose one small kindness, dear. Start there.

You can try this as soon as you finish reading.

But I want to give you a way to remember it later, too—when you're back in the middle of your day and the familiar answer is already on your lips.

### And if there's still someone from your past on your mind?

You can begin while those feelings are still there.

You don't have to settle every question about that person before accepting kindness from the people around you today.

A friend can care about you while you miss him. You can enjoy an afternoon while part of you still wonders whether he'll call.

Let yourself have those moments, %FIRSTNAME%.

And if someone new comes into your life, you can take your time. An open heart can still ask questions, notice how it's treated, and choose carefully.
