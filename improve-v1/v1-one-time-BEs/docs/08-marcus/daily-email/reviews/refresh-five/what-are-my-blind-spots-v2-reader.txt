Subject: Margaret, what if more thinking won’t show you what’s missing?

Margaret,

You can replay a conversation until you remember every word and still wonder what you missed. This morning I’ve laid ten Rider–Waite cards on blind spots. Three are face up, and the first has a small detail that’s easy to overlook.

One — what uncertainty can hide. The Moon.

A dog and a wolf look up at the moon between two stone towers. A path winds into the distance. Down at the water’s edge, a crayfish is climbing out.

It’s close enough to see clearly. Yet the animals’ raised heads draw your attention towards the sky.

When something feels uncertain, your attention can go towards what might happen or what somebody might have meant. A quieter clue can get less consideration: a difference between words and actions, or a fact you haven’t checked.

I read the crayfish as a reminder to look at what’s within reach. Before adding another explanation, ask what you’ve actually seen or heard. A possibility can occupy a great deal of thought without becoming evidence.

Two — what you keep weighing. The Two of Swords.

A blindfolded woman sits beside the sea, holding a sword in each hand. Her arms are crossed across her chest.

Look at what that position requires. To reach for something else, she would have to change the way she holds those swords.

You may know the arguments for two choices very well. You can go through one, then the other, and find yourself back where you started.

Beside The Moon, this card asks whether you’re giving the same information another hearing when you need something new to consider. A direct question might help more than another attempt to guess the answer.

That doesn’t mean you should rush your decision. You can pause the comparison long enough to find out what else you need to know.

Three — what another view can add. The Three of Pentacles.

A craftsman stands on a bench beneath a stone arch. Two people face him, and one holds a drawing of the work.

The worker and the person holding the plan are different people. That matters: they can compare what’s being made with what was intended.

You don’t have to distrust your own judgment to make room for another view. Someone you trust may notice a detail you’ve overlooked. Someone involved may have information you haven’t heard.

Their view still needs considering. It doesn’t automatically settle the question. But it gives you something to examine alongside your own conclusion.

Those are the three face-up cards in this morning’s reading.

Together, they describe how a blind spot can survive careful thought. Your attention goes towards an explanation; you keep comparing the same possibilities; the missing information stays outside that comparison.

The useful next move may be a question, a check, or an honest conversation. More time thinking cannot supply a fact you haven’t learned yet.

Where might that matter in your life? An assumption about love can affect what you ask for. An assumption about money can affect a choice before you’ve looked closely at the figures.

I’d like to continue into those parts of your life, Margaret. The seven remaining cards explore what you keep excusing, what you feel you are owed, what love needs from you, what you want to win, what you show people, what home feels like, and what a blind spot costs in money. We’ll look for assumptions worth questioning and where another perspective could help.

I’ll need your full birth name, including your first and last name, and your date of birth. I’ll begin with the strengths and challenges your birth date represents in the reading. Your name identifies a personal tarot card, which adds a closer look at strengths and familiar habits—especially how you weigh advice and make room for a view different from your own.

Explore what you may be overlooking.

Marcus

P.S. The crayfish is close enough to see. Check what’s in front of you before following another possibility.
