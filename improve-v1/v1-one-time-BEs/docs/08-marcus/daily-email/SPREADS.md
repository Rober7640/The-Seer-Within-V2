# 08 · THE SPREADS

The roster Marcus lays from. **Six spreads, 3 to 12 cards.** One is laid each morning on one
named thing, roughly a third of it turned face up and read free in the letter, the rest left face
down for the paid reading.

The letter **names the spread out loud**, the way it already credits the Rider–Waite deck, 1909.
So every provenance line below has to survive being read by someone who looks it up.

## The three tests

A spread has to pass all three. Two are hard exclusions and one is a preference.

**1 · ⛔ NO SIGNIFICATOR.** If the traditional form needs a card **chosen** — not drawn — to stand
for the woman asking, the spread is out. Marcus sells *her card* as the lens the paid reading is
laid around (PAID-READING §3). A spread that already puts her card on the table competes with the
product. ⚠ A card that is *drawn* into a "who you are in it" position is fine. The test is chosen
versus drawn, nothing else.

**2 · ⛔ POSITIONS MUST TRANSLATE.** Every position has to be sayable as a bare `what` / `who` /
`where` clause of 2–7 words — *"what he's protecting"*, *"what ends it"*, *"who you'd have to stop
being"*. Never `how`, never `why`. If a position can only be said in tarot vocabulary — *"what
crosses you"*, *"hopes and fears"*, *"the querent"* — the spread fails. **Tarot vocabulary never
reaches the letter.**

**3 · ⚠ PREFER A REAL SPREAD.** Relaxed from an exclusion, 2026-09-09. Use an existing popular
spread wherever one genuinely answers the named thing. Invent only where none does, and then:

- ⭐ **Design it backwards from the named thing it finds.** 07 built seven spreads first and forced
  a concept onto each afterwards, "which is why the letters read as arguments rather than as
  readings" ([`07-30-DAY-PLAN.md`](../../07-marcus/07-30-DAY-PLAN.md)). An in-house spread exists
  because a named thing needed positions no traditional spread has.
- ⛔ **Never claim provenance it hasn't got.** Every spread below carries a **Kind** —
  `traditional` / `folk` / `in-house` — and the letter must never call an invented spread ancient.

---

## ⛔ The Celtic Cross is excluded

It is the most famous spread there is, it is the one everyone will ask for, and it fails **two** of
the three tests. Recorded here so nobody proposes it again.

A.E. Waite, *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot*, 1911, §7, in
[`reference/pictorial-key-to-the-tarot-waite-1911.txt`](../reference/pictorial-key-to-the-tarot-waite-1911.txt):

> "The Diviner **first selects** a card to represent the person or, matter about which inquiry is
> made. This card is called the Significator."

- **Fails test 1.** The significator is chosen by the woman's age, sex and colouring — Queen for a
  woman over forty, Page for a younger one — and laid down before anything is drawn. It is exactly
  the seat Marcus's name-card has to occupy.
- **Fails test 2.** Waite's position 7 is *"himself — that is, the Significator"*, which restates
  the chosen card as a position. Position 2 is *"what crosses him"* and position 9 is *"his hopes or
  fears"*. None of those can be said in her language without inventing a different position.

⚠ Worth knowing anyway: Waite's own provenance for it is as thin as any folk spread's — *"I do not
think that it has been published — certainly not in connexion with Tarot cards."* The most
"traditional" spread in English has no source earlier than the man who printed it.

# 1 · THE THREE

**Cards:** 3 **Kind:** folk **Turned free:** 1

**Provenance.** ⚠ **The standalone past/present/future draw has no documented origin.** No named
inventor, no first publication, no historian's account. Every history repeats a vague 18th-century
European attribution and none of them cites anything.

The *division* is genuinely old — but always as **zones of a larger layout**, never as three single
cards. Two ancestors, both read:

| Year | Source | What it actually is |
|---|---|---|
| 1861 | Louisa Lawford, *The Fortune-Teller; or, Peeps into Futurity*, London: Routledge, Warne, and Routledge — section "Present, Past, and Future" | A **32-card piquet pack**, not tarot. Thirty cards dealt into three parcels of ten: *"The left-hand pack represents the Past; the middle, the Present; and the one on the right hand, the Future."* |
| 1889 | Papus, *Le Tarot des Bohémiens*, trans. A.P. Morton — Fifth Lesson | Three Major Arcana in a triangle, named past / present / future — but as **one half of a seven-card figure**. Quoted in full under spread 4 |

⭐ **The safe published wording, and it is true:** *the past/present/future division is documented in
card divination from at least 1861 and in occult tarot from 1889, but always as part of a larger
layout. The three-card spread on its own has no documented origin.*

⚠ The 1861 date needs its edition named — some catalogues cite an 1851 Lawford. The copy read
carries an 1861 title page with publisher ads dated December 1860. ⚠ Lawford's method *does* use a
chosen card for the reader, but it is shuffled back into the pack rather than laid, and it is a
playing-card method, so it is an ancestor of the idea and not a tarot source.

Sources read in full:
`https://archive.org/download/fortunetelleror00lawfgoog/fortunetelleror00lawfgoog_djvu.txt` ·

# 2 · THE CROSS

**Cards:** 5 **Kind:** traditional **Turned free:** 2

**Provenance.** Oswald Wirth, *Le Tarot des Imagiers du Moyen Âge*, **Paris, 1927** — chapters 15
("Consulting the Tarot"), 16 ("Interpretation of the Oracle") and the worked example in 17. Read as
primary text in the English translation, *The Tarot of the Magicians*, Samuel Weiser, 1985,
pp. 185–189, whose copyright page reads *"First published in Paris in 1927 under the original title:
Le Tarot, des Imagiers du Moyen Age."*
`https://archive.org/stream/TarotOfTheMagiciansByOswaldWirth/Tarot_of_the_Magicians_by_Oswald_Wirth_djvu.txt`

⛔ **Not 1926.** One widely-read blog dates it 1926. The book's own copyright page says 1927.
⛔ **Not "Péladan's spread".** Wirth writes only *"from Stanislas de Guaita we have a method which
Josephin Peladan pointed out to him."* No primary text by either man was found. If it is ever
mentioned, it is *attributed by Wirth to* — never as fact.
⛔ **Never attach past/present/future to it.** That belongs to an unrelated modern spread called the
Cross of Truth, which has no named origin. Wirth's cross is a for / against / judge / verdict
structure. Anyone calling "the traditional five-card cross" a timeline is not citing Wirth.
⚠ The French position names have not been read — the 1927 scan is blocked. **Do not print French.**

### ⭐ Two facts that make this spread unusual, and both are good

**Only FOUR cards are drawn. The fifth is arithmetic.** Wirth sums the numbers of the four drawn
arcana and reduces the total: if it is 22 or under, that arcanum is the synthesis; if over, the two
digits are added (his own examples: 23 → 2+3 → 5; 57 → 5+7 → 12). The Fool counts as 22.
⛔ **"Draw five cards" is a wrong instruction.** It is four drawn and one calculated.

**Twenty-two Major Arcana only.** No Minors, no court cards. Wirth's draw is also unusual and worth
keeping: shuffle, the consultant names the first number that comes into her head, 22 or under, that
many cards are counted off and the last one taken. Four separate shuffles, four intuited numbers.

⛔ **Significator: definitively none.** The word appears **zero times** in the whole book, all four
cards are drawn, the fifth is arithmetic, and a Majors-only spread has no court card that could
serve as one. It is structurally impossible here, not merely absent. *(One 2015 blog claims Wirth
adds a significator spread. Chapters 15–19 contain no such thing. Modern addition, mis-attributed.)*

⭐ **And the arithmetic rhymes with the product.** Marcus already turns a name into a number and the
number into a Major Arcanum (PAID-READING §3). Wirth turns four cards into a number and the number
into a Major Arcanum. Same move, 1927, in print. This is the one spread whose own mechanism argues
for the thing being sold.

| 4 | **needs her** | |

**What it answers.** A named thing shaped like a decision with a real case on both sides — *do I

**Hero layout.** FIVE tarot cards in a CROSS in front of the raven, ⛔ **all five MAJOR ARCANA** —

LEFT, one directly to its RIGHT, one directly ABOVE it and one directly BELOW it, each about a
card's width clear of the centre so the cross reads as a cross and the four arms do not touch. All
five upright. The two side cards sit level with the middle of the centre card. *(Wirth's own worked

Strength in the centre — 4 + 18 + 2 + 14 = 38 → 3+8 → XI.)*

# 4 · THE CROSS AND TRIANGLE

**Cards:** 7 **Kind:** traditional **Turned free:** 2

**Provenance.** ⭐ **The strongest small spread on the roster.** Papus (Gérard Encausse),
*Le Tarot des Bohémiens*, Paris 1889; English translation A.P. Morton, 1892; **Fifth Lesson,
"Fortune-Telling by the Tarot — I. Rapid Process."** Read in full from the Internet Archive plain
text and quoted verbatim below.

> "Take up the cards so that you can see them and read the oracles, noticing that the card placed at
> number 1 indicates the commencement. The card placed at number 2 indicates the apogee, at number 3
> the obstacles, lastly, at number 4 the fall.
> The major arcanum placed at I. indicates the influences that have weighed in the affair during the
> Past. The major arcanum in II. indicates the influence exerted over the Present. The arcanum at
> number III. shows the influence which will affect and determine the Future."

⚠ **The name is ours.** Papus calls it only the "Rapid Process". *The Cross and Triangle* describes
its actual shape. The letter credits **Papus, 1889** — it must not claim the name is his.

⚠ **Papus contradicts himself on positions 2, 3 and 4.** His Fourth Lesson diagram labels the cross
Commencement / Apogee / **Decline** / Fall in a different order from the Fifth Lesson text above.
We use the Fifth Lesson, because that is the passage inside the actual reading instructions. Nobody
has checked the 1889 French, so this may be the Morton translation's fault rather than Papus's —
**do not publish either version as the single correct one.**

⛔ **Significator: none, and Papus says so outright** — *"when the rapid process is used for
fortune-telling, the figures do not exclusively represent persons of especial complexion."*

⚠ **One thing is chosen, and it is not her.** Papus picks the *suit* by topic: *"If it is a love
affair, take the Cups or Hearts. For a law-suit, or any struggle, take Swords or Spades. In a money
matter, the Pentacles or Clubs."* Choosing a suit for the **subject** is not a significator and
passes test 1. ⭐ It is also a gift: the four cross cards are always the right suit for the morning's
named thing, and the three triangle cards are always Major Arcana, which SHAPE.md already treats as
the heavy ones.

**Provenance.** C.C. Zain (Elbert Benjamine), *The Sacred Tarot*, The Church of Light, Los Angeles,

`https://archive.org/details/1936-benjamine-zain-the-sacred-tarot`. Quoted verbatim:

> "When the influence and outcome of anything is sought, the cards may be dealt on a diagram similar
> to the one at the front of this booklet, one after the other, until 10 cards are laid out according
> to the sequence there indicated. … Card 1 signifies the spiritual result, and card 10 the material
> result. 6 is the result concerning life and love, and 9 the result as affecting the home. … Thus
> if on 2, Wisdom is the cause; if on 3, Intelligence is behind it; if on 4, Mercy has an influence;

Complete Guide to the Tarot*, 1970. ⚠ We use **Zain**, because Gray tells the reader to choose a
court card for the querent — she sets it aside rather than laying it, so it would technically pass,
but Zain uses none at all and is the cleaner source.

# 6 · THE TWELVE HOUSES

**Cards:** 12 **Kind:** traditional **Turned free:** 4

**Provenance.** Form from C.C. Zain, *The Sacred Tarot*, 1936, **"The Horoscope Spread"** — same
book and same edition as spread 5. Verbatim:

> "To use the diagram as a tarot spread the cards are dealt one by one upon the houses of the
> horoscope in the order indicated by the numbers in the diagram. The reading is used to foretell the
> near future of every department of life of the one making the consultation. The card falling upon
> any house of the chart will reveal the fortune of those things ruled by that house astrologically.
> The cards should be turned over only as read."

**Twelve cards, one per house, no thirteenth, no significator.** That is the whole reason this
version was picked.

⚠ **Two sources, and you must say which is which.** Zain gives the form but numbers his houses his
own way — his house 1 is *"personal things and health"* and his house 12 is *"cash and personal
property"*, which is not the standard astrological order anybody recognises. The **house meanings**
below are the standard twelve, as printed by **Eden Gray, *A Complete Guide to the Tarot*, Crown,
1970**. So: **form from Zain, meanings from Gray.** It is a composite of two documented sources and
the file says so rather than pretending it is one.

⛔ **Gray's own version fails test 1 and we drop that part of it.** She writes: *"a thirteenth card,
that of the Querent, is laid in the middle of the circle to serve as the 'Ruler' of the horoscope"*
— and in her worked example she *chose* the King of Cups for it. Papus's twelve-house circle
(1889) puts the Inquirer in the middle too. **Both centre cards are out.**

⭐ **And the empty middle is the point.** The one seat these two authors fill with a chosen card is
the seat Marcus's name-card already owns. The circle arrives with a hole in it exactly the shape of
the thing she buys. Nothing else on this roster fits the paid product this well.

⛔ **Significator: none, in the form we use.**

is a reading of every department of life, which is what a blind reading promises and what nothing
smaller can deliver.

⚠⚠ **Build this one last, and read the numbers before building the second.** Two warnings, both
earned:

1. **07 already ran a zodiac spread and retired it.** Its nine paid passages were starved at ~78
   words, and its free positions sat at 1, 7 and 12 — not contiguous. This one has **eight** paid
   passages (~87 words at the $35 rung, under the ~115 floor) and its free-able set breaks
   contiguity at position 4. It repeats both faults in a milder form.
2. **The free block is four cards.** 07 measured that the second free card costs ~300 words and the
   third buys nothing. Four has never been tried.

The counter-argument is that it is the only spread that answers the biggest named thing there is.
That is worth one test, not four.

**Hero layout.** TWELVE tarot cards in front of the raven, laid in a LARGE CIRCLE like the numbers
on a clock face, with a WIDE EMPTY SPACE of bare cloth in the MIDDLE — no card in the centre. The
cards are spaced evenly all the way round, each one lying flat and pointing outward from the centre
so that the ones at the top and bottom are upright and the ones at the left and right of the circle
lie ON THEIR SIDES, the way spokes sit on a wheel. All twelve whole and clearly separate. Laid by
hand and it shows — the circle is not perfectly round and the spacing is uneven.

⚠ This is the one layout that will not fit the wide landscape framing in
`scripts/make-08-heroes.py` without pulling the camera back further than any other spread.

## Where a guess is still standing

Named plainly rather than smoothed over.

| | |
|---|---|
| ⚠ **Spread 2, the French** | Wirth's position names were read in the 1985 Weiser English translation. The 1927 French is behind a 403 and nobody has read it. ⛔ Never print French terms as Wirth's words |
| ⚠ **Spread 4, positions 2–4** | Papus's own two passages disagree — his Fourth Lesson diagram labels the cross in a different order from his Fifth Lesson text. We use the Fifth Lesson. The 1889 French has not been read, so this may be the Morton translation's fault rather than Papus's |
| ⚠ **Spread 6, two authors** | The form is Zain's and the house meanings are Gray's. No single book prints the version we lay |
| ⚠ **Every "free-able" mark** | Applied by reading PAID-READING's rule, not by testing. The rule is *can he answer this honestly for any woman with that topic on her mind* — a real letter is the only thing that proves it |
| ⚠ **Eden Gray, generally** | Her three books are all lending-restricted and **nobody has read a page of them.** Every Gray quotation here reaches us through an unofficial scan or a full-text index. It is the one piece of physical legwork left |
