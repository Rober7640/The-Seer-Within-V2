MORNING EDITION SIX CARDS · ONE QUESTION

An interest can keep drawing you back without telling you where it belongs in your life. Is it something to enjoy when you have time, or something you want to pursue more seriously?

 

That’s the question behind the first of this morning’s six Rider–Waite cards.

 
 
THE TABLE THIS MORNING — six cards on one question. Two of them are looking back at you. Four of them are not. Rider–Waite–Smith deck, 1909.
 
 
 
One — what keeps drawing you back. The Star.
 
FIG. I · POSITION ONE
Turned. The Star.

A woman kneels beside a pool beneath a sky of stars. She holds two jugs, pouring water from one into the pool and from the other onto the ground. One foot rests in the water; her other knee rests on the land.

Both the pool and the ground receive water.

I read that detail as an invitation to give inspiration a place in ordinary life. Something that moves you doesn’t have to remain an idea you visit when your responsibilities are finished.

If an interest keeps returning, you can give it a little time in which to become something you actually do. You might practise, learn, or make a first attempt. The important part is having an experience to consider, beyond imagining what the experience might be like.

The Star offers encouragement to explore. A returning interest still needs that exploration before you can understand its place in your life.

 
 
 
Two — what deserves a fair trial. The Seven of Pentacles.
 
FIG. II · POSITION TWO
Turned. The Seven of Pentacles.

A man leans on a long-handled tool and looks at gold coins marked with stars among the greenery before him. He has paused his work. There is already something growing for him to examine.

That is what changes the reading for me: his effort has given him something to judge.

You may want to know whether an interest is your calling before making room for it. But some of the answer comes from the doing. Do you want to return after the first excitement fades? Does the work itself mean something to you? What are you willing to learn when it becomes difficult?

Those questions become easier to answer after a fair trial. Give the interest an amount of time and effort your life can accommodate, then look at what you learned.

The Star makes room for inspiration alongside practical life. The Seven of Pentacles asks you to examine what develops when you do.

 

Those are the two face-up cards in this morning’s reading.

 
 

You don’t have to settle the whole question of your purpose before taking one interest seriously. A trial can help you discover whether you want to continue, change your approach, or leave it as something you enjoy occasionally.

What deserves that chance—and how could you give it room without neglecting what already depends on you?

I’d like to continue with those questions in the remaining four cards: what gives you meaning, what needs room in your life, what makes beginning difficult, and where you can begin.
