Subject: What gift are you overlooking?

%FIRSTNAME%,

When you think about having a gift, do you picture something impressive enough to announce? A beautiful voice. An extraordinary memory. Something unmistakable.

The first of this morning's six cards asks you to look somewhere less dramatic: at what's already within reach.

**One — what is already available. The Magician.**

A man stands behind a table, one hand raised and the other pointing down. On the table lie a cup, a sword, a wand and a gold disc. The tools of all four suits are there in front of him.

His raised hand catches the eye. But he also has something to work with, right where he stands.

That's the detail I want to stay with. A gift doesn't have to arrive as a startling new ability. It may be something you use so often that you barely register what you're doing.

Perhaps you can take a muddled explanation and find the practical question inside it. Or notice which small change would make a difficult task easier. You finish, move on, and give little thought to the skill that helped.

The Magician offers a way to look: pay attention to what you can already do, especially where it produces a useful result. Familiarity can make a skill easy to miss.

**Two — what attention can develop. The Queen of Pentacles.**

The Queen sits on a carved throne with a gold disc resting in her lap. Her hands hold it, and her face is turned down towards it. Flowers and greenery surround her.

She is looking closely at what she holds.

Beside The Magician, that attention matters. Having a useful ability is a beginning. Understanding how you use it gives you a chance to develop it deliberately.

If explaining something comes easily, for instance, notice what makes your explanation work. Do you choose a good example? Put the steps in a helpful order? Spot the exact point of confusion? Once you understand the skill, you can practise it instead of simply hoping it works next time.

The Magician has the tools available. The Queen gives one thing her attention. Together, they ask you to take an ordinary competence seriously enough to learn more about it.

Those are the two face-up cards in this morning’s reading.

You don't need to decide that you're exceptional before an ability deserves care. Start with evidence: something you do that helps, solves a problem, or makes a worthwhile difference. Consider what part of that result came from your own judgment or effort.

The more personal question is which ability deserves that attention from you now. Being capable of something doesn't mean you want to spend more of your life doing it.

I'd like to explore that distinction in the remaining four cards: the strength you may overlook, where it could be useful, what keeps it small, and something you could try to discover how much you want to develop it.
