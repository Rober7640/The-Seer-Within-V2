Subject: 🌙 %FIRSTNAME%, the clue you’re missing may be right in front of you

%FIRSTNAME%,

You can replay a conversation until you remember every word and still miss what an outside observer would notice immediately.

That is the question behind this morning’s Tree of Life spread. I laid ten cards from the 1909 Rider–Waite deck to ask what you may be missing even after careful thought. The first clue appears in a small detail at the bottom of The Moon card.

**One — how a blind spot affects you. The Moon.**

The moon hangs high between two stone towers. A dog and a wolf look up at it. A long path winds away into the distance.

But look at the bottom of the card. A small creature is climbing out of the water. It is the nearest thing in the picture, yet everything else draws your eyes upward and away from it.

That is what a blind spot can do. When something feels uncertain, your attention moves away from what is in front of you. You think about what may happen later or what somebody could have meant. Meanwhile, a less dramatic clue may already be there: your first reaction, a contradiction between words and actions, or a direct question you have not asked.

The Moon doesn’t mean that whatever made you uncertain is imaginary. It warns that possible explanations can begin to feel more important than the evidence you already have.

**Two — what you already understand. The Two of Swords.**

A blindfolded woman sits beside the sea. She holds a sword in each hand, the blades raised evenly over her shoulders.

Notice what keeping them balanced requires. Her arms must remain crossed in front of her body.

You may already understand the two choices before you very well. You can explain the case for one, then the case for the other. You can keep them so evenly matched that waiting begins to feel like control: no mistake has been made, no door has been closed, and nothing has been lost.

But she cannot keep that perfect balance and reach for anything new at the same time.

Read with The Moon, this card shows how a blind spot can survive careful thought. The problem isn’t always that you haven’t thought about the decision enough. Sometimes comparing the same two options again simply keeps you stuck.

**Three — what you have concluded. The Three of Pentacles.**

A craftsman stands on a bench, working on the stone arch of a building. Two other people face him. One of them holds an architectural drawing.

The person doing the work is not the person holding the drawing.

That separation is the key to this card. The craftsman has skill. He has put in the effort. But his view from the bench cannot show him everything about the building. He needs to compare what he is making with the plan someone else is holding.

You may have thought carefully and reached a sensible conclusion. The card doesn’t ask you to distrust your judgment. The Three of Pentacles asks whether someone with different information has challenged that conclusion. An answer to a direct question, an inconvenient fact, or honest feedback from someone you trust may reveal more than another hour spent thinking by yourself.

Together, the three cards give a plain answer. Your blind spot may be this: you expect more thinking to reveal information that only a new point of view can provide. The Moon pulls your attention into the distance. The Two of Swords keeps the decision locked between the same two options. The Three of Pentacles brings in another point of view.

Three of the ten cards are face up. They describe a blind-spot pattern that many people can recognize. The remaining seven show where that pattern may be affecting your own life.

The first three cards cannot tell us which part of your life is most affected. Each remaining position examines one area: what you keep excusing; what you feel you are owed; the love you give and receive; the result you want; what you let other people see; your life at home; and your financial choices. Read together, those seven cards can show where trying to solve everything inside your own head may be hurting you most.
