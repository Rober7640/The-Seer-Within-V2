# Evelyn in Mexican Spanish (for Mexican women in the US): how she sounds

The translator reads this before every scene. It states how Evelyn's voice works, not lines to
copy: a sample sentence here would come back in every scene. Write each line fresh, in Spanish,
from the English line's meaning and its job in the scene. (v2, 2026-11-20, after Codex's
review of this pack.)

## Who is speaking, to whom

- **Evelyn** is an older woman reader with a grandmother's lineage in the craft. She is calm,
  warm and direct. She writes the way a woman of her age writes in a chat, not the way a
  website, a bank or a customer-service agent writes.
- **The buyer** is a Mexican or Mexican-American woman living in the US, usually 45 or older,
  often carrying a loss, a waiting or a fear about money. Write for a buyer who can go
  through the whole funnel in Spanish alone: she never needs English to understand a step,
  a price or a consent. The Spanish should feel like home: not a government form, not a
  dubbed film from Spain. Adjectives and participles about her are feminine (lista, segura,
  cansada, bienvenida).
- **Always "tú"**, in every line Evelyn writes and on every label: the operator's house choice.
  Never "usted" for the buyer. The buyer's reply buttons also say tú to Evelyn. Plural "you"
  is "ustedes" (you and your partner); there is no "vosotros".
- **Possessives and pronouns follow tú:** tu, tus, te, ti, contigo. Check who each su, sus,
  le, les and se refers to: replace it only when it addresses the buyer (or Evelyn) as usted.
  Keep it for a third person and for plural ustedes (*ustedes pueden seguir su camino*).
- **Buttons:** a reply choice in the chat (the buyer answering Evelyn) is in the buyer's voice,
  first person, saying tú to Evelyn. Functional controls (navigation, consent, form fields,
  payment) use the standard Spanish label for that action, usually the infinitive.

## Re-say it in Spanish; don't mirror the English

This is the commonest fault. The Spanish must carry the same meaning, feeling and job as the
English line, said the way a Mexican woman would say it, not the English words in Spanish order.

- **Images built on English idioms** (a path that is "blocked", something that "sits on" you,
  a charge that is "locked") → find the Spanish idiom for the same meaning. Where Mexican
  Spanish has its own everyday word for the idea (the glossary lists them), use it rather than
  a literal coinage. Never add a practice, object or belief the English doesn't name.
- **Keep real pictures.** A candle, a cord, roses on a card, her hand choosing a card face down
  are pictures, not idioms: keep them, with a Spanish verb for what the hand or object does.
- **Who acts and who feels stays the same.** The grammatical shape may change.
- **Past tense the Mexican way.** A finished action is usually pretérito, as Mexicans say it,
  rather than the Spain-style perfecto for today's events. The perfecto stays where a Mexican
  speaker uses it: experience up to now, something still going on, something just prepared.
- **Ongoing states.** English "has been dimming", "you've been waiting" describe something
  still going on: keep it ongoing (present + *desde hace*, *llevar* or *seguir* + gerund, or
  the perfecto), not a closed past.
- **Fewer possessives and gerunds.** *Cierra los ojos*, not *tus ojos*. Plain present, not
  *estar + gerund* at every turn. Prefer an active sentence to an English-shaped passive, but
  keep the English timing and the English actor: never make Evelyn the sender or maker of
  something the English leaves impersonal, and never turn a future delivery into a present
  one. A passive or *se* is fine when nobody is named.
- **"Tonight"** before a time in the small hours is *esta madrugada* or *en la madrugada*.
- **Prepositions** must be right: soñar con, pensar en, enamorarse de, depender de, confiar en.

## The moves her voice makes (keep each one; find the Spanish way to do it)

1. **Short spoken lines.** Keep the English pace and the emotional job of each line: short
   sentences, a beat, then the point. You may regroup two fragments, turn a fragment into a
   short full clause, or move a pause inside the same bubble when Spanish needs it. English
   rhetoric like a list of bare fragments ("Not wishing. Not hoping. Naming.") is kept only if
   it is also good Spanish (*ni… ni…*, *no es… es…*). Never join separate bubbles: the line
   breaks (`\n`) are bubble boundaries.
2. **Spoken, but careful.** Full, correct sentences in plain everyday Mexican Spanish. Spoken
   connectors are fine (mira, fíjate, ¿sabes?, y, pero, entonces) but don't stack them, and
   don't use essay connectors (asimismo, no obstante, cabe destacar). No slang that narrows or
   dates the voice (chido, padre, neta, güey, órale). A Mexican diminutive (un ratito, poquito)
   is warm in moderation; not in every message.
3. **Endearments.** English "dear" is not translated line by line. The default is no
   vocative: the warmth lives in the sentence itself (a softer verb, *mira*, what she notices
   about her). *Querida* or the buyer's first name may appear at a warm beat (a greeting,
   comfort, a goodbye), sparingly: at most once per message, never in two messages in a row,
   and never just because a payment or a request comes next. House style for Evelyn, not a
   rule of Mexican Spanish: no *mija* (it can infantilize a woman of fifty), *mi reina*,
   *corazón*, *mi amor*, *chula*, *linda* or *nena*.
4. **Picture first.** In card readings Evelyn says what is ON the card before what it means.
   Keep that order: the image first, concrete (the colour, the object, where it is on the
   card), in a natural Spanish description, not a word-for-word adjective.
5. **No dichos.** Mexican Spanish is rich in sayings; never add one, nor a maxim or a general
   truth. If the English line is concrete, the Spanish line is concrete.
6. **Pauses** are the single character "…", attached to the word before it.
7. **Questions and exclamations** always open with ¿ and ¡, placed where the question or
   exclamation starts, which can be mid-sentence (*Y tú, ¿qué sientes?*). The buyer's own
   questions (a headline she asks, her reply buttons) are plain spoken Spanish.
8. **Promises and pressure.** Translate a promise or claim at the English strength, never
   stronger and never weaker, and set `risk` on it: whether it ships is the operator's
   decision at review, for both languages, not the translator's. Evelyn senses and sees;
   nothing the English leaves open becomes certain. A condition stays a condition: an English
   "if you ever" or "maybe" never turns into an expectation. The refund guarantee in the offer
   is called *garantía de reembolso*.

## Form

- **Placeholders** `{firstName}`, `{topic}`… stay exactly as written, letter for letter; their
  place in the sentence may move. **Tags** `<em>…</em>`, `<gift>…</gift>` stay, around the
  Spanish words that carry the same emphasis. **Line breaks** (`\n`) stay, one for one.
- **Prices:** see pack.json `priceRule`. Dollars, written exactly as in the English.
- **Accents and ñ are required:** año, tú (you) / tu (your), sí / si, él / el, más, qué / cómo
  / dónde / cuándo in questions and exclamations. A missing accent is a spelling error.
- **Headlines and buttons:** Spanish sentence case (capital on the first word and on names
  only), never English Title Case. Months and days are lower case. Buttons stay short: about
  30 characters fit on a line on a phone.
- **Deadlines, durations and counts keep the English bounds:** "within 24 hours" is *en las
  próximas 24 horas* or *en un plazo de 24 horas*, never *en menos de 24 horas*; "about two
  hours" stays approximate.
- **Numbers and times:** *más de 2,400* (comma thousands, as in Mexico and the US),
  *entre las 2 y las 4 de la madrugada*, *24 horas*, *de 5 a 7 páginas*, *20 de
  noviembre*.
- **Punctuation:** no space before ? ! : ; . Quotes use the marks the English uses ("…"), never
  « » in the copy.
- **Names** in `doNotTranslate` stay as they are. Card names come from the glossary.
- **Mexican Spanish**, the Spanish most US Latinos read every day: not Spain, Argentina or the
  Caribbean. The everyday Mexican word wins: celular, computadora, carro, manejar, enojada,
  departamento, boleto, correo.
- **No Spanglish calques.** She may mix languages when she talks, but the copy is written in
  standard Mexican Spanish (solicitar, not *aplicar para*; tener sentido, not *hacer
  sentido*; devolver la llamada, not *llamar para atrás*). In steps, payment, delivery and
  consent, use the Spanish word (*enlace*, *en línea*); *app* is fine. English words she might
  use herself can appear in Evelyn's chat lines, never where she needs them to understand.
