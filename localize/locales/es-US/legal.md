# United States, Spanish-speaking buyers: rules that change copy

Not legal advice. v2 (2026-11-20) after Codex's review, which corrected v1. The citations are
Claude's and Codex's and have not been checked by a lawyer. The English funnel runs in this
market under the same law. A faithful Spanish translation is a draft for review, not an
approval: a line that is risky in English is just as risky in Spanish.

## For the translator (applies to every segment)

- **Translate faithfully, add nothing.** No stronger fear words, no extra urgency, no new
  deadline, no new claim about Evelyn, her work or her clients.
- **Don't soften either.** Keep a promise or claim at the English strength and tag it: the
  operator decides at review whether it ships, for both languages at once.
- **No new health claims.** Nothing heals, cures or treats unless the English says so, and then
  no more than the English says.
- **Disclosures in full.** Prices, what will be charged and when, refund terms, text-message
  terms and any disclaimer are translated completely, as clear as the English, never shortened.
- **Text-message keywords** STOP and HELP stay in English as the words to type.
- **Law names:** keep the statute, the acronym and the US jurisdiction; a Spanish name or a
  short explanation may come first (Comisión Federal de Comercio (FTC)). Never substitute a
  Mexican law, authority or remedy.
- **Tag the risk, don't fix it.** When a line carries one of the risks below, translate it as
  it is and set `risk` so the review page groups it for the operator:
  - `urgency`: a limit on time, places or availability ("tonight only", "limited spots").
  - `danger`: what happens to her, her family or her life if she doesn't buy ("the shadow
    will grow").
  - `claim`: a fact that needs proof (customer counts, testimonials, Evelyn's biography, her
    doing the ritual by hand, a promised result, a health effect).
  - `ai-identity`: Evelyn presenting herself as a human.
  - `consent`: email, text or tracking opt-ins, and refund wording.

## For the operator: decisions before any Spanish traffic

1. **Disclosures in Spanish.** FTC policy (16 CFR 14.9) says that when an ad or sales page is
   in Spanish, the disclosures that a rule or order requires must be in Spanish too. It sets
   the language, not which disclosures are required. Operational policy: Spanish checkout,
   order bump, one-click upsell charges, refund policy, text-message opt-in and legal pages.
2. **Claims and pressure.** The danger, urgency and claim lines carry the same exposure in
   Spanish as in English: FTC Act §5 (deceptive or unfair practices); health claims need
   scientific evidence; testimonials must reflect real experiences, say what buyers can
   generally expect when the results shown are exceptional, and disclose material connections
   (Endorsement Guides, 16 CFR 255); fake reviews and testimonials are banned (16 CFR 465,
   since 21 Oct 2024). A refund guarantee doesn't substantiate a result. The FTC has run cases
   and outreach aimed at fraud against Spanish-speaking consumers.
3. **Evelyn and AI.**
   - California's BOT Act (Bus. & Prof. Code §17940–17943): a bot may not talk to a person in
     California online with intent to mislead about its artificial identity, knowingly
     deceiving her, to sell, unless it clearly discloses that it is a bot. Where it applies,
     the disclosure must reach the Spanish-speaking buyer in the chat itself; a line buried in
     English terms is not enough.
   - Utah requires a clear answer when a consumer asks whether she is talking to AI. Decide an
     approved Spanish answer to "¿Eres una persona real?" (and the English equivalent).
   - California SB 243 and New York GBL Article 47 regulate companion chatbots (notices,
     crisis referrals). Decide whether they apply to how Evelyn actually works; a sales chat
     is not automatically a companion.
4. **Fortune telling.** New York Penal Law §165.35 makes paid fortune telling an offence,
   including some curse-removal and spirit services. Its exception covers a show or exhibition
   solely for entertainment or amusement; an English disclaimer carried into Spanish does not
   settle whether a paid personal reading or a cleansing or protection upsell qualifies. This
   is a decision, not a translation.
5. **Text messages.** The opt-in card shows in the US market.
   - Law (TCPA): where prior express written consent is needed, the Spanish consent must name
     the seller, the kind of messages, and state that consent is not a condition of purchase.
   - Carrier standards (CTIA, not law): program name, message frequency, "message and data
     rates may apply", HELP and STOP instructions, links to terms and privacy.
   - Translate the confirmation and HELP replies too.
   - A request to stop in her own words ("no me mandes más mensajes", ALTO, PARAR) must be
     honoured; never say STOP is the only way. Codex reports an FCC amendment on how senders
     may designate opt-out methods taking effect 25 Nov 2026 (unverified): check the rule in
     force at launch.
6. **Privacy notices.** If the business is covered by the CCPA, its notices (at collection,
   the sale/sharing opt-out) must be offered in the languages it ordinarily uses with
   consumers: a Spanish funnel means Spanish notices (CCPA regulations §7003).
7. **Email and shipped goods.** Spanish marketing emails need a Spanish unsubscribe and the
   sender's postal address (CAN-SPAM). Physical items (stone, bracelet) follow the Mail Order
   Rule (16 CFR 435): keep shipping promises the operator can meet, and send delay or
   cancellation notices in Spanish. The emailed reading is not shipped merchandise.
8. **Crisis lines.** The Spanish crisis copy points to 988 (call and press 2 for Spanish, or
   text AYUDA to 988; 988lifeline.org/es) and to Crisis Text Line (text AYUDA to 741741;
   crisistextline.org/es), both checked 2026-11-20. The app's hotline entry
   (server/lib/crisisHotlines.ts) needs the same Spanish text.
